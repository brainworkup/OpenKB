# Dyslexia

Dyslexia is a neurodevelopmental learning disorder primarily characterized by difficulties with accurate and fluent word recognition, poor spelling, and weak decoding abilities. These difficulties typically arise from deficits in [[concepts/phonological-processing]] — the ability to perceive, manipulate, and work with the sound structure of language. Dyslexia is not related to intelligence and occurs across the lifespan, though it is most commonly identified during the school-age years. At younger ages (e.g., age 7), diagnoses are considered provisional and should be confirmed with follow-up evaluation (e.g., at Grade 3).

## Core Features

- **Phonological processing deficits**: Difficulty segmenting, blending, and manipulating phonemes (individual sounds in words)
- **Decoding difficulties**: Struggling to map letters and letter combinations to their corresponding sounds; tendency to process letter sounds in isolation rather than as whole words
- **Impaired reading fluency**: Slow, labored, or inaccurate oral reading (see [[concepts/reading-fluency]])
- **Spelling difficulties**: Weak orthographic knowledge, inconsistent letter-sound mapping
- **Working memory involvement**: Phonological loop deficits may compound reading challenges (see [[concepts/working-memory]])
- **Secondary impacts**: Reading difficulties often cascade into vocabulary, comprehension, and academic achievement gaps
- **Common co-occurrence with ADHD**: Sustained attention difficulties and executive function challenges frequently accompany dyslexia, requiring dual-track intervention

## Relationship to Phonological Processing

The phonological processing hypothesis is the most empirically supported explanation for dyslexia. Children and adults with dyslexia typically show weaknesses in:
- **Phonological awareness**: Recognizing and manipulating sounds (rhyming, blending, segmenting)
- **Phonological memory**: Holding sound sequences in short-term memory
- **Rapid automatic naming**: Quickly retrieving verbal labels for familiar items

Interventions targeting [[concepts/phonological-processing]] are therefore central to evidence-based dyslexia treatment.

## Evidence-Based Interventions

### Phonics Programs
An intensive, explicit phonics program is recommended as the starting point — particularly for young children. The focus is concretely connecting phonemes to orthography to support sight-word retention. Key programs include:
- **Orton-Gillingham** (see [[concepts/orton-gillingham-reading-intervention]])
- **Reading Recovery**
- **Lindamood-Bell**
- Minimum frequency: 3–4 sessions/week; daily is optimal; approximately 6 months of intensive intervention before progressing to fluency and comprehension work

### Orthography
- **Seeing Stars Program** for orthographic processing
- Practice writing letters and words at least 2x/week (letters in order, family names, copying from books, writing messages)
- Writing strengthens motor cortex allographic letter representations and reinforces phoneme mapping
- Orthographic awareness game: generate multiple plausible spellings of the same word while maintaining pronunciation

## Strategic Reading for Young Adults with Dyslexia

For young adults — particularly those in college — the focus shifts from foundational decoding to strategic, efficient engagement with complex reading loads. The key principle is to never simply begin reading an assignment linearly. Instead, a structured pre/during/post reading sequence is recommended (see [[concepts/strategic-reading]]):

1. **Skim first**: Read title, subtitles, graphs, introduction, and conclusion before attending a lecture. For non-textbook chapters, read the first and last paragraphs and sample topic sentences.
2. **Attend lecture and take notes**: Listening before reading deeply helps prime comprehension.
3. **Read the full assignment** as soon as possible after the lecture.
4. **Annotate**: Take notes, write in margins, or use color-coded highlighting for main ideas vs. specific details.
5. Supplement with Cliffs Notes or published critiques for complex literary texts.

For class discussions (rather than lectures), complete skimming, reading, and note-taking *before* the discussion.

### Active Reading Strategies
- Highlight and underline key points; use electronic highlighting tools for digital resources.
- Pause every 1–2 paragraphs to summarize aloud; re-read if unable to summarize.
- Answer in-chapter comprehension questions to check understanding.
- Use Post-It notes to flag study guide answers for quick retrieval later.

## Academic Accommodations for Dyslexia in Higher Education

Young adults with dyslexia (often co-diagnosed with ADHD) should formally apply for academic supports through their institution (see [[concepts/academic-accommodations-higher-education]]):

- **Extended time** on exams (×1.5 or ×2.0)
- **Low-stimulus or isolated testing environments**
- Option to **sub-vocalize multiple-choice questions** during tests
- Access to **professor outlines or PowerPoint slides** prior to class to reduce simultaneous listening/writing load
- Right to **audio-record lectures** for later review — particularly important given the multitasking demands of simultaneous listening and note-taking
- Access to **audiobooks or text tapes** to supplement reading; listening while reading significantly increases comprehension
- Use of **dictation software** (e.g., Dragon Naturally Speaking) at home and school for writing tasks (see [[concepts/writing-accommodations]])

## Writing Strategies

Writing is a particular challenge for individuals with dyslexia. Recommended strategies include:

- **Brainstorm** by dictating initial thoughts into an audio recorder or smartphone before writing
- Create an **outline** with thesis and sub-topics before drafting
- Plan each paragraph on a single page, listing the ideas and facts needed to make the argument
- Draft on a computer to leverage spell-check and grammar tools
- **Proofread in three separate passes**:
  1. Review content and logical sequencing of ideas (ask: What is this paragraph's purpose? How does it follow and lead the adjacent paragraphs?)
  2. Eliminate redundancy; simplify and shorten awkward sentences by reading them aloud
  3. Correct spelling and grammar errors using software tools

## Test-Taking Strategies

Beyond formal accommodations (see [[concepts/test-taking-accommodations]]), the following strategies help manage test performance:

- Survey the entire test first to allocate time appropriately
- Read each question carefully; attend to details
- Answer the easiest or immediately known questions first
- Prioritize high-point-value items (e.g., essays)
- Skip stuck items and return after completing the rest — other sections may provide helpful context
- For essay questions, quickly outline main ideas in the margins using bullet points before writing

## Lecture and Note-Taking Strategies

- Experiment with computer-based note-taking versus handwriting
- Stay after class to review and supplement lecture notes while content is fresh
- Recording lectures reduces the cognitive burden of simultaneous listening and writing — especially relevant given frequent co-occurrence of ADHD and executive function demands
- When professor-provided outlines are available, annotate directly on top of them
