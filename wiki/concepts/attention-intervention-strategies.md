# Attention Intervention Strategies

Attention intervention strategies are evidence-based behavioral, environmental, and instructional techniques designed to reduce inattention, distractibility, and off-task behavior in children with chronic attention difficulties. These strategies are applied across classroom and home settings and are typically recommended as part of a neuropsychological or behavioral report. Related strategies also address co-occurring executive function deficits, learning difficulties such as math skill deficits, reading, writing, and verbal language development.

See [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.attention-problems]] for the primary source document detailing attention recommendations, [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.executive-fx]] for executive functioning recommendations, [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.build-math-skills]] for math-specific intervention strategies, and [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.building-verbal-skills]] for verbal and language development recommendations. See also [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.dyslexia]] for a comprehensive set of recommendations for children with dyslexia and co-occurring ADHD.

## Definition of Attention Problems

Attention problems are characterized by chronic and severe inconsistencies in the ability to maintain and regulate focus. Core features include:
- Distractibility (internal and external)
- Inability to concentrate for extended periods
- Disorganization
- Failure to complete tasks
- Lack of study skills

These difficulties persist even in quiet, structured environments. See [[concepts/adhd-clinical-features]] for related clinical features.

## Categories of Intervention

### 1. Environmental Modifications
- Remove distractions and extraneous noise at school and home.
- Provide a quiet, supervised workspace away from peers.
- Establish a consistent physical arrangement, schedule, and behavioral expectations — a structured learning environment.
- Maintain a seating arrangement that minimizes peer distraction; preferential seating should be individualized rather than defaulting to the front of the class.
- Many students with ADHD find that listening to soft background music while studying is actually helpful.
- For students with math difficulties, allow use of **graph paper** to compensate for poor visual-organizational skills when aligning multi-digit numbers.
- Set aside dedicated, low-distraction time for verbal activities such as family discussion, shared reading, and conversation practice.
- Establish consistent homework routines: same time, same place, and same subject order each night to reduce decision-making overhead.

### 2. Movement and Sensory Strategies
- **Motor breaks:** 3–5 minutes of physical movement after every 15–20 minutes of cognitive effort.
- **Stretch breaks:** Allow standing during extended seat work.
- **Fidget tools:** Wiggle seats or stress balls to channel excess physical energy in a socially appropriate manner.
- Allow students to stand during seatwork or complete physical errands as task reinforcement.
- Schedule the most demanding attention tasks in the morning, as attention and impulse control tend to decline over the course of the day.
- Allow frequent breaks to move around inside and outside the classroom.

### 3. Instructional Modifications
Aligned with the Modified Task-Presentation intervention framework:
- Use multiple teaching modalities to prevent over-reliance on any single sensory channel.
- Mix high- and low-interest tasks (e.g., follow lecture with hands-on activity).
- Break long, complex tasks into smaller, sequential steps delivered one at a time; provide feedback and reinforcement at each step.
- Assign every third problem rather than every one for overwhelming assignments; give one sheet at a time.
- Use highlighting, underlining, colors, symbols, or font changes to emphasize key information.
- Offer student choice among instructional activities.
- Provide guided notes and instruction in attending to relevant information.
- Modify in-class assignments, responses, and homework as needed; reduce homework load to guided practice on mastered material.
- Incorporate **manipulatives, drawing, and hands-on activities** when teaching mathematical concepts to support engagement and retention.
- Use **visual cues** to teach operations or skills involving sequencing and serialization.
- Reduce complexity of classroom discourse: control vocabulary level, sentence length, grammatical complexity, and number of steps in commands; provide written support.
- Organize new information into visual categories meaningful to the student to aid recall.
- Cue students (and the class) ten minutes before the end of a period to finish up, write down assignments, and prepare for transition.
- Provide anticipatory guidance for upcoming events, assignments, and projects.

**Pre/During/Post Lesson Supports for Comprehension:**
- *Before:* Pre-teach upcoming content and key vocabulary; skim material before the class lecture.
- *During:* Use visual supports (pictures, diagrams, graphs); give verbal cues for important information; supply written outlines of main ideas; take notes during the lecture.
- *After:* Have students immediately paraphrase key information; review notes after the lecture; thoroughly read assigned material as soon as possible after the lecture.

See [[concepts/iep-accommodations]] for related academic accommodation frameworks.

### 4. Behavioral Techniques
- **Non-punitive cueing:** A prearranged, unobtrusive signal (e.g., tap on shoulder) to redirect off-task behavior; discussed with the student prior to use.
- **Behavioral reinforcement:** Reinforce target behaviors; use charting of successful assignment completion.
- **Daily Behavior Report Cards (DBRCs):** Track behavior daily using a letter-grade rating system; communicate results to child and parents; apply contingency reinforcement.
- **Immediate reinforcement:** Provide immediate positive reinforcement for demonstrations of increased self-control or longer periods of sustained attention.
- **Proofreading habits:** Encourage reviewing work hours or days after completion; offer partial credit for self-corrections. Teach students to check work before and after completion (e.g., color-coding problem types on worksheets as a pre-check strategy).
- **Emphasize accuracy over speed:** Provide separate grades for accuracy and speed; avoid rushing students; allow extended time on in-class assignments.
- **Positive feedback for verbal engagement:** When a student engages in conversation, adults should respond with reciprocal conversation, requests to elaborate, and affirming comments — reducing reluctance to express.
- Teach eye contact before delivering instructions; have students paraphrase directions back to confirm comprehension — at home as well as school.
- **Praise effort over ability:** Praising students for working hard — rather than labeling them as "smart" — encourages perseverance when facing difficult concepts and supports the development of executive functioning.

See [[concepts/behavior-modification]] and [[concepts/functional-behavioral-assessment]] for foundational frameworks.

### 5. Self-Management and Metacognitive Strategies
- **Self-talk:** Inner verbal reminders such as "Sit up straight, eyes on the speaker" to maintain focus during critical tasks.
- **Self-monitoring charts:** Track on/off-task behavior at timed intervals; calculate percentage of on-task time; gradually increase interval length as attention improves.
- **Advocacy skills:** Teach students to request extended time for tests and to remove themselves from sources of distraction.
- **"Big picture" orientation:** Before beginning a task, ask the student to restate what the task requires and develop a plan; during the task, relate steps back to the overall plan; review after completion. This builds self-awareness and reflective thinking.
- Provide external organizational scaffolding (previewing tasks, commentary during execution, and review after completion) to support students who cannot yet self-organize independently.

See [[concepts/self-monitoring-behavior]] for related self-regulation strategies.

### 6. Social and Peer-Based Supports
- Assign a peer buddy ("study buddy") for task clarification, note-sharing, and homework support via phone or video chat.
- Use small group formats, peer tutoring, or paired work to support task engagement.
- Consider classwide peer tutoring and computer-assisted instruction as supplemental options.

### 7. Test-Taking Accommodations
Students with attention difficulties and co-occurring learning disorders (e.g., [[concepts/dyslexia]], reading disorder) benefit from the following test-taking supports:
- Extended time (1.5x–2x) on tests, quizzes, and standardized exams.
- Testing in a small, structured, low-distraction room separate from the general classroom.
- Teaching strategic test-taking: survey the full test first; complete easiest items first; prioritize high-point items; skip and return to difficult items.
- For essay questions, quickly outline main ideas in the margins before writing.
- For reading-heavy tests, allow sub-vocal reading of questions.
- Do not count spelling errors against students on content-area tests (history, science).
- Encourage proofreading tests before submission; offer partial credit for corrected errors.

See [[concepts/test-taking-accommodations]] and [[concepts/iep-accommodations]] for related frameworks.

### 8. Written Expression and Keyboard Accommodations
For students with attention difficulties compounded by graphomotor weaknesses or reading disorder:
- Allow use of a keyboard or computer rather than handwriting for written expression tasks.
- Permit use of dictation software (e.g., Dragon) at home and school.
- Allow spell check, word prediction, read-back, and speech recognition software on written assignments.
- Do not penalize spelling errors on content-area or expressive writing tasks.
- Use a structured writing process: dictate brainstorm → outline → draft on computer → three-pass proofreading (content/sequence; wordiness; spelling/grammar).

See [[concepts/writing-accommodations]] and [[concepts/dysgraphia]] for related topics.

### 9. Verbal Language and Communication Strategies
For students with speech-language difficulties or weak verbal expression, targeted strategies include:

**Conversation and Verbal Expression**
- Adults ask open-ended questions and avoid interrupting; follow up with elaborating questions.
- Provide positive feedback when the student engages in conversation to encourage continued expression.
- Set aside regular, distraction-free time for family discussion of daily events.

**Shared Reading and Early Literacy**
- Use [[concepts/dialogic-reading]], an evidence-based shared reading intervention, to build early literacy, vocabulary comprehension, and enjoyment of reading.
- Ask child-specific questions during reading to foster interest and engagement.
- Parents should read aloud to children daily for at least 15 minutes; children should choose books (mix of easy, just-right, and challenging).

**Vocabulary Development**
- Expose students to new vocabulary in real-world contexts (e.g., naming objects in a store, ordering food at a restaurant).
- Reinforce new vocabulary through multimodal practice: finger-painting letters, clay molding, flash cards, and sentence construction.
- See [[concepts/vocabulary-development]] for cross-document vocabulary strategies.

**Verbal Categorization**
- Play naming games: list as many objects as possible sharing a characteristic (color, texture, shape).
- Use everyday tasks (sorting laundry, putting away toys) to practice object classification.

**Analogical Reasoning**
- Practice sentence-completion analogies to develop relational language and reasoning.

**Object Attributes**
- Adults describe an object's attributes while the student identifies the object — building descriptive language and active listening.

**Story-Time Activities**
- Read stories multiple times with deliberate changes to characters, action, or sequence; the student identifies and describes what changed.
- Ask the student to complete incomplete stories or participate in story dramatization.

See [[concepts/speech-language-development-disorders]] for related clinical context.

### 10. Reading-Specific Intervention Strategies
For students with [[concepts/dyslexia]] or reading difficulties co-occurring with attention problems:

**Phonological and Phonics Interventions**
- Provide an intensive, explicit phonics program (minimum 3–4x/week, daily preferred) that concretely connects phonemes to orthography.
- Recommended approaches include Orton-Gillingham, Reading Recovery, and Lindamood-Bell.
- Play phonological awareness games: rhyming, sound blending, syllable segmentation, and sound deletion.
- Use letter cards to build and manipulate words; incorporate orthography to support phonological processing.

See [[concepts/phonological-processing]] and [[concepts/orton-gillingham-reading-intervention]] for related content.

**Orthographic Processing**
- Seeing Stars Program is a recommended option for orthographic processing.
- Practice writing letters and words at least twice a week to strengthen allographic representations.
- Play orthographic awareness games: spell the same word as many ways as possible while maintaining the same pronunciation.

**Reading Fluency**
- Focus on [[concepts/reading-fluency]] rather than individual word analysis once foundational phonics are established.
- Techniques: imitative reading, repeated reading, radio reading, phrase reading, paired reading, and echo reading.
- Record students reading passages aloud; have them listen back to build confidence.
- Provide high-interest, low-readability books for pleasure reading.
- Track reading progress (fluency and comprehension) daily or weekly; graph data to monitor growth.

**Active Reading Strategies (Home)**
- Highlight and underline main points; use electronic highlighting tools for digital resources.
- Pause every 1–2 paragraphs to summarize aloud; re-read if unable to summarize.
- Answer in-chapter comprehension questions; use Post-It notes for study guide answers.
- Scaffold reading by discussing subject matter before reading; pre-read end-of-chapter questions and boldfaced headings.

**Strategic Reading (Lecture-Based Settings)**
- Skim material (title, subtitles, introduction, conclusion) before the lecture.
- Take notes during the lecture; review notes after.
- Thoroughly read the assigned material as soon as possible after the lecture.
- If a class discussion rather than a lecture precedes reading, skim, read, and make notes before the discussion.

**Recorded and Assistive Reading**
- Provide access to recorded textbooks; student follows along while listening.
- Allow recording of lectures for later review; request professor PowerPoints/outlines in advance.
- Use text-to-speech tools and audiobooks to support comprehension and reduce decoding burden.

### 11. Math-Specific Intervention Strategies
For students with [[concepts/dyscalculia]] or significant math learning difficulties, targeted interventions include:

**Foundational Number Skills**
- Use egg cartons and physical objects to develop rote counting and one-to-one correspondence.
- Practice skip counting (by 2s, 3s, 4s, 5s, 6s, etc.) to build a foundation for multiplication as repeated addition.

**Math Fact Automaticity**
- Use mnemonics, rhymes, and finger tricks (e.g., multiplying by 9).
- Use flash cards and digital games with immediate feedback and reinforcement to build retrieval speed.

**Conceptual and Relational Learning**
- Use weighted plastic bags to teach relational concepts around mass (heavier/lighter).
- Attach coins to index cards with illustrated prompts to support money skill recall.
- Encourage real-world generalization: shopping, making change, cooking, estimating travel time.
- Parents should model mathematical thinking in everyday life.
- Explicitly remind students to double-check math work; use a calculator to verify homework.

**Assessment Accommodations for Math**
- Allow calculator use on tests assessing procedural knowledge rather than computational skill.
- Provide extended time on math tests and quizzes.

**Evidence-Based Math Intervention**
- Participate in a structured, evidence-based [[concepts/math-interventions]] program targeting specific areas of weakness.
- Monitor progress closely; escalate to a more intensive intervention if adequate progress is not made.

### 12. Executive Functioning Interventions
Executive functioning (EF) encompasses self-control, impulse inhibition, time management, goal-setting, planning, and flexible problem-solving. Children with attention difficulties often show co-occurring EF deficits. The following strategies target EF directly.

See [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]] and [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.executive-fx]] for the primary source document.

**Impulse Control and Self-Control**
- Provide immediate reinforcement for demonstrations of self-control or sustained attention.
- Use mindfulness-based training to help children ignore distracting thoughts and concentrate on the task at hand. Mindfulness supports impulse control across all age groups. See [[concepts/mindfulness-based-interventions]] for related strategies.
- Practice delay of gratification: structured exercises in waiting for rewards have been shown to regulate impulses and promote positive social interactions.
- Play structured inhibition games:
  - "Freeze" games: stop all movement when a specific sound (e.g., a bell) is heard.
  - Clapping games during songs, where students clap except during certain words — increasing complexity over time (e.g., clap twice for some words, not at all for others).
  - These activities build motor inhibition and regulation of motor output.

**Time Management and Sense of Time**
- Set appropriate time limits for clearly defined tasks; allow the student to monitor his or her own progress with a timing device.
- Enhance sense of time by having the student estimate how long specific activities will take before beginning them.
- Teach use of a schedule: record deadlines, appointments, and homework assignments on a calendar; check progress daily.

**Goal-Setting and Task Planning**
- Teach the student to set realistic goals and monitor progress toward those goals.
- Use a **contract approach** — developed collaboratively with the student — to build independence and self-direction. Student involvement in creating the contract increases investment in the outcome.
- Break multistep assignments into smaller, manageable steps; set sub-goals for each step and monitor progress.
- Teach and rehearse specific problem-solving steps before complex tasks; provide logical structures or procedures when possible.
- Encourage the student to assume responsibility for completing and submitting assignments on time; establish clear expectations around timeliness.

See [[concepts/goal-setting-self-monitoring]] for related frameworks.

## Structured Intervention Frameworks

### Daily Behavior Report Cards (DBRCs)
A systematic feedback loop for attention improvement:
1. Define target behaviors.
2. Monitor and record behavior daily.
3. Provide reinforcement for meeting targets.
4. Communicate results to children and parents.

**Phases:** PREP → IMPLEMENT → EVALUATE
- Evaluation compares pre/post ratings and requires moderate-to-large behavioral change for the intervention to be considered effective.

### Modified Task-Presentation Strategies
Antecedent instructional modifications informed by functional behavioral assessment:
- Identify which modifications best fit the child, content, and setting.
- Present task using the modified approach.
- Observe and compare the impact of each modification; retain effective strategies, discontinue ineffective ones.

### Skills Trainer / Monitor Model
For students requiring intensive support (e.g., dyslexia + ADHD), a skills trainer or monitor can:
- Serve as a liaison between the student and teachers.
- Check in with the student at the beginning and end of each week (and briefly each day).
- Assist with developing plans to address organizational skills, time management, study skills, and test-taking.
- Receive regular progress and concern reports from all teachers.

## Memory and Organization Supports
- Start with main ideas; build general knowledge before adding detail.
- Classify new information into clusters based on meaning similarity.
- Use eye contact before delivering instructions.
- Use **mnemonic devices** and visual imagery (mental pictures, first-letter cues) to support memory for new information.
- **Chunking:** Group pieces of information into larger units to reduce memory load (e.g., grouping phone digits into pairs).
- Link new information to prior knowledge to support encoding and retrieval.
- Use spaced, repeated study sessions rather than massed cramming; introduce facts gradually and drill to mastery.
- Use color-coded flash cards organized by subject or theme.
- Use visual supports for lessons and text (pictures, diagrams, graphs) to anchor understanding.
- Use a day planner or calendar to track upcoming assignments, events, and projects.
- Maintain a well-organized notebook and filing system; assist students with this explicitly.
