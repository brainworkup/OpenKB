# Major Depressive Disorder: Clinical Features and Assessment

Major Depressive Disorder (MDD) is one of the most common and serious mood disorders encountered in clinical psychological and neuropsychological evaluation. It is characterized by a constellation of affective, cognitive, somatic, and behavioral symptoms that significantly impair daily functioning. Accurate identification requires integrating self-report data, behavioral observation, and standardized assessment instruments.

## Core Clinical Features

### Affective Symptoms
- Persistent sadness, emptiness, or hopelessness
- Anhedonia — loss of interest or pleasure in previously enjoyed activities
- Feelings of worthlessness or excessive guilt
- Pervasive pessimism and negative self-evaluation

### Cognitive Symptoms
- Difficulty concentrating and attending to tasks
- Ruminative thinking, dwelling on past failures
- Hopelessness about the future
- In severe cases, suicidal ideation or planning
- Thought blocking, confusion, and indecision (seen in younger or more severely affected patients)

### Somatic Symptoms
- Sleep disturbance (insomnia or hypersomnia)
- Changes in appetite and/or weight
- Decreased energy and fatigue
- Psychomotor slowing or agitation
- Decreased sexual interest
- Note: Some presentations (particularly in younger patients) may show primarily cognitive/affective features with relatively few physiological signs — as observed in Raven Colbert ([[summaries/pai_202]]), where depressive symptomatology was prominent but physiological markers (energy, appetite, weight, sleep) were relatively intact

### Behavioral Features
- Social withdrawal and isolation
- Reduced performance in occupational and social roles
- Passive, unassertive interpersonal style
- Avoidance of previously enjoyed activities
- Low initiation of relationships; submissive stance in social interactions

## Diagnostic Considerations

DSM-5 requires at least five of the above symptom categories to be present for at least two weeks, with at least one symptom being depressed mood or anhedonia. Specifiers such as *single episode* versus *recurrent*, and severity ratings (mild, moderate, severe) guide treatment planning.

Common differential diagnoses and comorbidities include:
- **Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia)** — lower intensity but chronic course; frequently co-occurs with MDD as "double depression" (see [[concepts/persistent-depressive-disorder]])
- **Generalized anxiety disorder** — worry and tension often co-present with depressive features, as seen in younger adults where anxiety and depression overlap significantly
- **Somatic symptom disorder** — physical complaints that may amplify or reflect depressive features (see [[concepts/somatic-symptom-disorder]])
- **Posttraumatic stress disorder** — trauma-driven mood disturbance with overlapping features (see [[concepts/trauma-informed-clinical-assessment]])
- **Borderline personality disorder** — affective instability that can mimic or co-occur with depressive episodes (see [[concepts/borderline-personality-disorder]])
- **Psychotic disorders** — when depression is accompanied by unusual ideation or perceptual disturbances (see [[concepts/psychosis-clinical-features]])
- **Social anxiety disorder** — social withdrawal and avoidance may reflect comorbid social anxiety, particularly in adolescent and young adult presentations
- **Other specified personality disorder (mixed borderline/avoidant features)** — rule-out when passivity, self-criticism, and interpersonal avoidance are prominent
- **Paranoid personality disorder** — hypervigilance, suspiciousness, and interpersonal hostility may co-occur with depressive features in complex presentations (see [[concepts/paranoia-and-suspiciousness]])
- **Cyclothymic disorder** — mood fluctuations that may overlap with or precede a full MDD episode; included as a rule-out in younger adult presentations such as Corey Ho ([[summaries/pai_96]])
- **Panic disorder** — somatic anxiety and panic attacks may accompany or mimic depressive episodes; rule-out in complex presentations (see [[summaries/pai_96]])
- **Substance use disorders** — depression and drug/alcohol use are frequently bidirectionally related; depression can drive substance use, or be a consequence of substance-related disruption (see [[concepts/substance-use-clinical-assessment]])

## Presentation in Young Adults

Adolescent and young adult presentations of MDD may differ from adult presentations in notable ways:
- Cognitive and affective symptoms may predominate over somatic/vegetative features (e.g., energy, appetite, weight, sleep may remain relatively intact)
- Uncertainty about identity, goals, and life direction is commonly associated (see [[concepts/identity-disturbance-clinical-features]])
- Social withdrawal and avoidance may be more salient, complicating treatment engagement
- Thought disorganization, confusion, and difficulty concentrating may raise differential questions about psychotic-spectrum disorders
- Suicidal ideation, including passive ideation and self-harm behaviors, warrants careful monitoring
- Co-occurring anxiety disorders (generalized anxiety, social phobia, panic disorder) are particularly common in this age group
- Mild or transient depressive symptomatology may present alongside features of emotional lability and interpersonal instability, raising the question of comorbid personality pathology (e.g., borderline features)
- Impulsive and risk-taking behavior may co-occur, particularly when substance use is present
- Attention and concentration difficulties may be prominent enough to warrant consideration of an inattention index score, as in Corey Ho ([[summaries/pai_96]]), where the Inattention (INATTN) Index was elevated (T=77) alongside a Neuro-Item Sum of T=69

Corey Ho ([[summaries/pai_96]]) illustrates a typical young adult MDD presentation: a 22-year-old male with significant depression, anxiety, identity uncertainty, cognitive disorganization, and elevated suicidal ideation (Suicide Potential Index T=68). His PAI profile showed a diagnostic group coefficient of fit of **0.757** for MDD, the highest fit among all diagnostic categories. Rule-outs included cyclothymic disorder, panic disorder, and borderline personality disorder.

See also [[concepts/anxiety-clinical-features]] and [[concepts/social-avoidance-and-withdrawal]].

## Presentation in Middle-Aged Adults with Complex Trauma

In middle-aged adult presentations (e.g., widowed adults in their 40s), MDD frequently co-presents with PTSD, somatic preoccupation, paranoid features, and significant suicidal risk. Benjamin Chiu ([[summaries/pai_94]]) illustrates this pattern:
- MDD recorded as the highest diagnostic profile fit (0.910) in a 41-year-old widowed male
- Severe anxiety, PTSD features, paranoid personality features, and somatic complaints co-present
- Suicidal ideation at crisis level (Suicide Potential Index T=93), with explicit endorsement of plans to kill self (item 100, VT) and active consideration of suicide (item 340, VT)
- Disruptions in life circumstances (bereavement, relationship losses) leave the individual uncertain about goals and fearful about the future
- Social isolation and perceived lack of support compound depressive severity and elevate suicide risk
- Negative impression management present alongside denial of substance use — requiring careful interpretation

## Assessment with the PAI

The [[concepts/personality-assessment-inventory]] (PAI) is a widely used self-report instrument for assessing MDD and related features. Its Depression scale (DEP) and subscales — DEP-C (Cognitive), DEP-A (Affective), and DEP-P (Physiological) — capture the multidimensional nature of depressive experience.

### Key PAI Indicators for MDD
| Indicator | Relevance |
|---|---|
| DEP full scale | Overall depressive severity |
| DEP-A (Affective) | Sadness, anhedonia, emotional withdrawal |
| DEP-C (Cognitive) | Hopelessness, worthlessness, concentration |
| DEP-P (Physiological) | Sleep, appetite, energy, psychomotor changes |
| Suicide Potential Index (SPI) | Suicidal ideation severity |
| Level of Care Index (LOC) | Intensity of care needed |
| Mean Clinical Elevation | Broad psychopathology load |
| RXR (Treatment Rejection) | Treatment readiness/resistance |
| Inattention (INATTN) Index | Attention/concentration difficulties |
| Neuro-Item Sum | Neurological/cognitive symptom burden |

### PAI Profile Fit for MDD
MDD shows high diagnostic group coefficients of fit in the PAI's profile-matching algorithm across multiple clinical cases in this knowledge base:
- Profile fit of **.910** in Benjamin Chiu (age 41; [[summaries/pai_94]]), the highest single MDD fit observed across the knowledge base; co-presenting with PTSD (.865), paranoid personality disorder, and a critical-level Suicide Potential Index (T=93)
- Profile fit of **.866** in Raven Colbert (age 20; [[summaries/pai_202]]), where MDD was the second-highest diagnostic group fit behind borderline personality disorder (0.894)
- Profile fit of **.830** (AJ Zhang, age 19; [[summaries/pai_11]])
- Profile fit of **.819** or above seen in other adult cases (e.g., [[summaries/pai_04]])
- Profile fit of **.757** in Corey Ho (age 22; [[summaries/pai_96]]), where MDD was the primary diagnostic consideration with single episode unspecified as the DSM-5 coding; persistent depressive disorder (0.751), schizoaffective disorder (0.729), and anxiety disorders (0.725) were next in order of profile similarity
- Profile fit of **.738** in Dylan Kay (age 20; [[summaries/pai_199]]), where MDD is listed as a rule-out diagnosis alongside persistent depressive disorder as the primary consideration

A coefficient above **.42** represents a statistically significant association; values above .80 are strongly indicative. In presentations where dysthymia rather than MDD is the primary diagnosis (e.g., [[summaries/pai_199]]), MDD profile fit still appears prominently in the differential, underscoring the spectrum nature of these conditions.

### Critical Items for MDD
PAI critical items particularly relevant to MDD include:
- **DEP-A items** (e.g., "I have no interest in life" — endorsed "Slightly True" in [[summaries/pai_96]], [[summaries/pai_202]], and [[summaries/pai_94]]; endorsed Very True in [[summaries/pai_11]])
- **DEP-P items** (e.g., Item #75: "I have no trouble falling asleep." [keyed False] — endorsed False at score 3 in [[summaries/pai_202]] and [[summaries/pai_94]] and [[summaries/pai_96]], indicating sleep difficulty)
- **SUI items** (e.g., "I've made plans about how to kill myself" — endorsed Slightly True in [[summaries/pai_96]] at item 100; endorsed Very True at maximal severity in [[summaries/pai_94]] and [[summaries/pai_202]])
- **BOR-S items** (self-harm when distressed — co-occurs in presentations with borderline and depressive features, as in [[summaries/pai_202]])

## Suicide Risk in MDD

Suicidal ideation is a critical concern in MDD evaluation. The PAI Suicide Potential Index (SPI) provides a quantitative index, and specific critical items flag explicit suicidal planning or self-harm. Elevated SPI scores — particularly at or above 68T — warrant **immediate clinical intervention** and safety planning.

Notable case examples:
- **Benjamin Chiu** ([[summaries/pai_94]]): SPI = **93T** (highest observed across this knowledge base), Chronic Suicide Risk Index = **83T**, profile fits with "current suicide" (.912) and "suicide history" (.871) behavior groups at near-maximal levels. Critical items include explicit plans to kill self (item 100, Very True) and active suicidal ideation (item 340, Very True). Risk is compounded by social isolation, absence of social support, and situational stressors including widowhood and relationship losses.
- **Raven Colbert** ([[summaries/pai_202]]): SPI = **90T**, Chronic Suicide Risk Index = **83T**, profile fits with "current suicide" (.867) and "suicide history" (.868) behavior groups at near-maximal levels. Critical items include explicit plans to kill self (item 100, Very True) and active suicidal ideation (item 340, Slightly True). Risk is compounded by poor impulse control, social isolation, and lack of social support.
- **AJ Zhang** ([[summaries/pai_11]]): SPI elevated at 68T; Chronic Suicide Risk index at 57T; profile fit with "current suicide" behavior group at .822.
- **Corey Ho** ([[summaries/pai_96]]): SPI = **68T**, Chronic Suicide Risk Index at 51T; profile fit with "current suicide" (.737) and "suicide history" (.652) behavior groups both statistically significant. Critical item #100 ("I've made plans about how to kill myself") endorsed Slightly True. The report explicitly identifies the combination of hopelessness, agitation, confusion, and stress as placing the respondent at increased risk for self-harm. Follow-up of suicidal thoughts and evaluation of life circumstances was warranted.
- **Dylan Kay** ([[summaries/pai_199]]): SPI elevated at 62T with periodic and perhaps transient thoughts of self-harm; report recommends specific follow-up regarding suicidal thoughts.

Across cases, even mild SPI elevations require careful evaluation. At the highest SPI levels (T ≥ 90, as in [[summaries/pai_94]] and [[summaries/pai_202]]), immediate intervention is mandatory. See [[concepts/suicide-and-violence-risk-indices]] and [[concepts/suicide-risk-assessment-pai]] for further detail.

## Self-Concept and Interpersonal Features

Depression is frequently associated with a characteristic pattern of self-concept and interpersonal functioning:
- Harsh negative self-evaluation; self-critical and self-blaming
- Pessimism about the future; dwelling on past failures
- Submissive, passive interpersonal style with avoidance of social initiation
- Low perceived social support; dissatisfaction with quality of relationships
- Anxiety in social contexts where unconditional acceptance is not guaranteed
- Tendency to attribute successes to others rather than to oneself
- Uncertainty about goals and life priorities, especially following major life disruptions

This pattern is especially pronounced in younger adult patients where identity uncertainty compounds depressive self-appraisal. In Raven Colbert ([[summaries/pai_202]]), self-concept was described as poorly established and fluctuating — oscillating between harsh self-criticism and periods of relative self-confidence, with self-esteem highly dependent on the status of close relationships. In Dylan Kay ([[summaries/pai_199]]), similar features were prominent: negative self-evaluation, self-blame for past failures, and a withdrawn, introverted interpersonal style coexisting with a desire for closer social connection. In Corey Ho ([[summaries/pai_96]]), self-concept was poorly established with harsh self-criticism and severe self-doubt; self-perception varied as a function of close relationships, and self-esteem was fragile, prone to plummet in response to perceived slights. In Benjamin Chiu ([[summaries/pai_94]]), a widowed middle-aged adult, the depressive self-concept was characterized by pronounced pessimism, dwelling on past failures, and a sense that future success depends entirely on others — compounded by bereavements and relational losses.

## Comorbid Somatic Presentation

MDD frequently co-occurs with somatic preoccupation. Clients may report physical symptoms as their primary complaint, with mood disturbance secondary or unacknowledged. Both Raven Colbert ([[summaries/pai_202]]) and Benjamin Chiu ([[summaries/pai_94]]) demonstrate this pattern — frequent minor physical symptoms (headaches, pain, GI problems), vague fatigue and ill health, with the PAI pattern consistent with somatic symptom disorder. The respondent's self-image may be largely influenced by a belief that they are handicapped by poor health, and social interactions tend to focus on health problems. This pattern is especially notable in medical-legal contexts such as motor vehicle accident (MVA) claims, where physical and psychological distress intermingle. The PAI's Somatic Complaints scale (SOM) and its subscales help delineate genuine somatic concerns from symptom amplification. See [[concepts/somatic-symptom-disorder]] for more.

## Comorbid Anxiety and PTSD Features

In many PAI-assessed cases where MDD is the primary or rule-out diagnosis, elevated anxiety and trauma-related features co-present:
- Affective anxiety (tension, difficulty relaxing, fatigue from perceived stress) may dominate the clinical picture even when cognitive or somatic anxiety features are less pronounced
- Somatic anxiety signs (sweaty palms, trembling, palpitations, shortness of breath) are prominent in more complex presentations such as [[summaries/pai_202]] and [[summaries/pai_94]]; Corey Ho ([[summaries/pai_96]]) similarly describes overt physical signs of tension
- ARD-T (Traumatic Stress) subscale elevations suggest a traumatic history continuing to produce recurrent distress; in Benjamin Chiu ([[summaries/pai_94]]), all three ARD-T critical items were endorsed at Very True; in Corey Ho ([[summaries/pai_96]]), both ARD-T critical items were endorsed (items 34 and 114, ST), indicating reliving of horrible experiences and long-standing troubled memories
- Phobic avoidance — including possible agoraphobia or multiple phobias — may co-occur with the depressive picture in complex presentations
- The combination of MDD, anxiety, and PTSD-related features produces the highest PAI diagnostic profile fits

See [[concepts/anxiety-clinical-features]] and [[concepts/trauma-informed-clinical-assessment]].

## Comorbid Paranoia and Hostility

In some adult presentations of MDD, particularly following significant life trauma or loss, paranoid and hostile features emerge as prominent comorbidities:
- Hypervigilance and suspiciousness of others' motives
- Extreme sensitivity to perceived inequitable treatment; tendency to hold grudges
- Interpersonal hostility perceived by others, straining working relationships
- Fears of being treated as a target or conspired against
- Preoccupation with perceived slights even when unintentional

This pattern is prominent in Benjamin Chiu ([[summaries/pai_94]]), where paranoid personality disorder was among the three primary diagnostic considerations alongside MDD and PTSD. When paranoid features accompany MDD, therapeutic alliance is particularly difficult to establish. See [[concepts/paranoia-and-suspiciousness]].

## Comorbid Substance Use

Depression and substance use disorders are frequently intertwined in complex clinical presentations:
- Depression may drive substance use (self-medication), or be a consequence of the disruption associated with substance abuse
- Withdrawal syndromes can produce or exacerbate depressive symptoms (concentration problems, anxiety, low mood)
- In Raven Colbert ([[summaries/pai_202]]), drug use was described as above average even for specialized treatment populations, with likely dependence and probable withdrawal symptoms; MDD co-presented at a profile fit of .866
- In Benjamin Chiu ([[summaries/pai_94]]), the respondent denied alcohol or drug problems, but profile characteristics (personality features associated with substance involvement) raised the possibility of denial
- In Corey Ho ([[summaries/pai_96]]), self-report indicated no significant problems with alcohol or drug abuse or dependence, and the PAI ALC and DRG estimated scores were close to actual scale values (ALC estimated 7T higher than ALC; DRG estimated 1T lower than DRG), supporting the self-report at face value
- Validity concerns are heightened when substance use is present, as clients may minimize or deny alcohol/drug involvement while simultaneously overreporting psychological distress

See [[concepts/substance-use-clinical-assessment]].

## Treatment Implications

- **Psychotherapy:** Cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) and behavioral activation are first-line psychological treatments
- **Pharmacotherapy:** Antidepressants, particularly SSRIs/SNRIs, are common adjuncts; antipsychotic medication co-prescription is suggested when psychotic-spectrum or paranoid features are present
- **Treatment motivation:** Clients with MDD may present with ambivalence; the PAI Treatment Process Index (TPI) and the RXR scale inform readiness — low RXR estimated scores flag potential resistance. In Corey Ho ([[summaries/pai_96]]), RXR Estimated Score was 7T higher than actual RXR (T=32), suggesting that RXR may slightly underestimate true openness to change; the Treatment Process Index was at T=60, indicating treatment motivation typical of clinical populations
- **Trust and alliance:** Depressed clients with avoidant, borderline, or paranoid features may struggle to form therapeutic alliances; this is particularly salient when fear of abandonment or rejection is prominent (see [[concepts/fear-of-abandonment-and-rejection]])
- **Disorganization as barrier:** Severely depressed clients may be too disorganized or overwhelmed to participate meaningfully in structured therapies early in treatment — a concern explicitly noted in [[summaries/pai_202]], [[summaries/pai_94]], and [[summaries/pai_96]]
- **Social support deficits:** When social isolation is severe, the therapeutic relationship takes on heightened significance; Corey Ho ([[summaries/pai_96]]) showed average stress and intact social support, which are favorable prognostic signs relative to cases such as [[summaries/pai_94]] where social isolation is severe
- **Level of care:** Elevated Level of Care Index (LOC) scores signal need for more intensive services (e.g., LOC T = 89 in [[summaries/pai_202]]; LOC T = 92 in [[summaries/pai_94]]; LOC T = 61 in [[summaries/pai_96]])
- **Anger expression:** MDD patients with meek, unassertive styles may have difficulty with appropriate anger expression; anger management was within normal range in Corey Ho ([[summaries/pai_96]])
- **Somatic resistance to psychological framing:** Clients with prominent somatic complaints may be reluctant to acknowledge psychological origins of their difficulties and may resist psychological treatment
- **Suicidal ideation monitoring:** Periodic or transient suicidal thoughts require ongoing follow-up even when SPI elevations are moderate rather than severe; at the highest SPI levels (T ≥ 90), immediate intervention is mandatory
- **Attentional difficulties:** When INATTN index elevations are present (as in Corey Ho, T=77), treatment modalities that do not rely heavily on sustained attention and complex verbal processing may be preferable in early stages

See [[concepts/treatment-motivation-and-compliance]] for more on treatment engagement across PAI cases.

## Validity Considerations in MDD Assessment

Depressed clients may show inconsistent responding on lengthy self-report instruments, particularly toward the end of the test when fatigue or disengagement increases. The PAI Back Random Responding index can flag this pattern (e.g., T = 87 in [[summaries/pai_11]]), requiring cautious interpretation. This should not be confused with intentional malingering or defensiveness.

In more complex presentations, a dual distortion pattern may emerge — simultaneous negative impression management (overreporting of symptoms, possibly as a "cry for help") alongside selective denial (e.g., minimizing substance use). This was observed in [[summaries/pai_202]] (NIM predicted profile fit .895, "fake bad" fit .852) and in [[summaries/pai_94]] (NIM predicted profile fit .854, Malingering Index T=84, Negative Distortion Scale T=78).

In contrast, some presentations show fully valid protocols with 0 missing items and all distortion indices within normal limits. Corey Ho ([[summaries/pai_96]]) exemplifies this: 0 missing items, all negative distortion indicators in the normal range (Malingering Index T=44, NIM well below threshold), all positive distortion indicators normal (Defensiveness Index T=38), and consistent responding throughout — enabling confident interpretation. Similarly, [[summaries/pai_199]] presents a fully valid protocol. See [[concepts/validity-and-response-styles]], [[concepts/random-responding]], and [[concepts/malingering-detection]].
