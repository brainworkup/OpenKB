# Parent Training for Child Behavior

Parent training is a **psychoeducational (social learning) intervention** that targets parental behavior and thought processes to improve child behavioral outcomes. Rather than working directly with the child, it focuses on equipping parents and caregivers with concrete skills that foster positive interactions and reduce coercive cycles that perpetuate conduct problems and antisocial behavior.

See source recommendations in [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.conduct-problems]].

---

## Core Rationale

Coercive parent-child interaction cycles are a primary driver of escalating behavior problems. These cycles typically begin with a parental directive, followed by a negative child response, leading to a coercive parental reaction — creating a feedback loop that reinforces antisocial behavior. Parent training interrupts this cycle by:

- Teaching parents to recognize and avoid coercive disciplinary patterns
- Increasing the use of **positive reinforcement** and structured discipline
- Building relationship quality to support child compliance and emotional regulation

This intervention is recommended as part of a multi-pronged approach to [[concepts/conduct-problems-interventions]], alongside [[concepts/social-skills-training]] and [[concepts/anger-management]].

## Essential Skill Domains

Parent training curricula typically address the following domains:

### Reinforcement Strategies
- Verbal praise
- Social reinforcers
- Tangible reinforcers
- Activity reinforcers
- Token economy systems and reward charts
- Contingency contracts

### Discipline and Behavior Management
- Response-cost techniques
- Timeout procedures
- Punishment and extinction principles
- Observation skills

### Relationship Enhancement
- Play skills
- Communication and problem-solving
- Partner support

### Self-Regulation for Parents
- Mood management
- Self-determination
- Relaxation techniques
- Stress reduction
- [[concepts/anger-management]] techniques
- [[concepts/self-monitoring-behavior]] and self-reward

## Procedural Framework

### PREP Phase
- Conduct an **intake and family climate assessment** to identify needs and barriers
- Determine whether group or individual training is appropriate for the setting
- Identify parental skill deficits and formulate measurable goals and objectives
- Establish logistics (format, location, session count, timing)
- Consider creating a **partnership contract** to formalize commitment

### IMPLEMENT Phase
- Teach **one specific parenting technique per session**, using verbal descriptions, examples, and discussion
- Address parental concerns and provide evidence of technique effectiveness
- **Model the technique** and role-play using parent-provided real-world examples
- When appropriate, involve the child directly in session role-plays
- Assign **homework**: independent implementation and documentation between sessions
- Begin each session by reviewing the prior technique, discussing homework, and answering questions

### EVALUATE Phase
- Monitor parent uptake and application of trained techniques
- Adjust training rate and duration based on sustained effectiveness
- Track both process (training participation) and outcome (child behavior changes)

## Clinical Context

Parent training is indicated when:
- A child presents with elevated risk for or confirmed [[concepts/conduct-problems-interventions]]
- [[concepts/functional-behavioral-assessment]] identifies coercive home dynamics as a maintaining factor
- Parents demonstrate skill deficits in reinforcement, discipline, or relationship quality
- [[concepts/behavior-modification]] at the child level alone has proven insufficient

It is frequently combined with [[concepts/social-skills-training]] delivered directly to the child, and may be embedded within broader [[concepts/aggression-interventions]] or multimodal treatment plans.

---

## Theoretical Foundations

Parent training is grounded in **social learning theory** and operant conditioning principles. It assumes that parenting behaviors are learned and modifiable, and that changes in parenting practices produce measurable improvements in child behavior. The approach is strongly evidence-based and recognized in guidelines including the **BASC-3 Behavior Intervention Guide**.
