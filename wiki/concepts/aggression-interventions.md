# Aggression Interventions in Children

Aggression in children is characterized by hostile or destructive behaviors — both physical (e.g., hitting) and verbal (e.g., name-calling) — and represents one of the most clinically significant behavioral and emotional problems addressed in neuropsychological practice. Children with aggression problems commonly show deficits in problem solving, perspective-taking, identifying alternatives, considering consequences, and determining causality.

See the source recommendations document: [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.aggression]]

## Core Deficit Areas

Aggressive children often struggle with:
- Generating alternative solutions to social problems
- Considering the consequences of their actions
- Determining causality in interpersonal conflict
- Means-ends thinking
- Taking the perspective of others

These deficits place aggression within the broader framework of [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]] and [[concepts/social-skills-training]] needs.

## Evidence-Based Intervention Strategies

A range of interventions have demonstrated effectiveness in remediating childhood aggression:

- **Bully Prevention**
- **Child-Centered Play Therapy**
- **Classroom Social Dynamics**
- **Cognitive Restructuring** — challenging and replacing distorted thinking patterns that fuel aggression
- **Counseling Groups**
- **Good Behavior Game (GBG)**
- **Incremental Theory Training** — teaching that abilities and traits can change with effort
- **Mindfulness Training** — see [[concepts/mindfulness-based-interventions]]
- **Peer-Mediated Conflict Resolution and Negotiation**
- **Problem-Solving Training**
- **Replacement Behavior Training**
- **Social Skills Training** — see [[concepts/social-skills-training]]
- **Verbal Mediation**

## Intervention 1: Problem-Solving Training

Problem-solving training teaches children a sequential, deliberate process for navigating negative social situations. The overarching goal is to build new cognitive and behavioral skills that reduce aggression and other problem behaviors.

### Essential Steps
1. Recognize that a problem exists
2. Define the problem accurately (whose problem, and why)
3. Generate possible solutions
4. Evaluate each solution for pros and cons
5. Design, implement, and evaluate the chosen solution

### The ICE Acronym
A key generalization tool is the **ICE** framework:
- **I** — Identify the problem
- **C** — Create alternatives
- **E** — Evaluate options

Children are taught to apply ICE across diverse situations, building transferable conflict resolution skills.

### Implementation Phases
- **PREP:** Identify aggression triggers; gather prior examples.
- **IMPLEMENT:** Children anonymously share problems; group defines, generates solutions, evaluates options, and role-plays the plan. Reinforcement is applied at a 1:1 ratio for new appropriate behaviors.
- **EVALUATE:** Children demonstrate the process independently, report on ICE outcomes, and rate the program. Aggression incidence is monitored.

This approach aligns with [[concepts/cognitive-flexibility-training]] and broader [[concepts/behavior-modification]] principles.

## Intervention 2: Replacement Behavior Training

Replacement behavior training (RBT) targets the *function* of aggressive behavior rather than just its form. Because aggression often serves a purpose (e.g., escaping a demand, gaining attention, obtaining a tangible), the intervention replaces it with a socially acceptable behavior that fulfills the same function — making it *functionally equivalent*.

### Essential Steps
1. Conduct a **Functional Behavioral Assessment (FBA)** — see [[concepts/functional-behavioral-assessment]] — to operationally define the behavior and identify its antecedents, setting events, consequences, and function.
2. Identify a replacement behavior that is as accessible as the problem behavior and yields the same outcome.
3. Provide immediate reinforcement for the replacement behavior; ignore the aggressive behavior.
4. Evaluate and revise as needed.

### Implementation Phases
- **PREP:** Assess the child's developmental readiness; administer FBA; hypothesize and test the function; select the replacement behavior and reinforcers.
- **IMPLEMENT:** Introduce the concept to the child; teach via direct instruction; use prompts or agreed-upon signals to cue the replacement behavior in context.

## Relationship to Other Clinical Areas

Aggression interventions frequently overlap with:
- [[concepts/behavior-modification]] — reinforcement schedules, prompt fading, generalization
- [[concepts/functional-behavioral-assessment]] — underpins Replacement Behavior Training
- [[concepts/social-skills-training]] — many aggression interventions build prosocial skills
- [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]] — problem-solving deficits are a core driver
- [[concepts/iep-accommodations]] — school-based aggression intervention plans often appear in IEPs
- [[concepts/cognitive-flexibility-training]] — supporting perspective-taking and adaptive responding
- [[concepts/mindfulness-based-interventions]] — mindfulness is listed as a standalone intervention option

## Clinical Context

These recommendations are drawn from the BASC-3 Behavior Intervention Guide framework and are intended for use in both individual and group treatment formats. Intervention selection should be guided by the child's cognitive and developmental readiness, the function of the aggressive behavior, and the available treatment setting.
