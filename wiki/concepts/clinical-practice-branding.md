# Clinical Practice Branding

Clinical practice branding encompasses the full system of identity decisions — name, visual design, tone, typography, and messaging — that position a healthcare or clinical provider in the minds of their target audiences. Unlike general consumer branding, clinical practice branding must simultaneously signal professional authority, regulatory credibility, and human warmth, often to very different audiences at the same time.

---

## Core Challenges

### Audience Tension
Clinical practices frequently serve multiple, psychologically distinct audiences whose expectations conflict:
- **Patients and families** want warmth, reassurance, and clarity
- **Legal and institutional referrers** (attorneys, courts, hospitals) want authority, credentials, and precision
- **Peer referrers** (physicians, psychiatrists, schools) want professional competence signals

This tension is central to the [[summaries/brainworkup-branding-concepts]] document, which explores seven branding directions for a pediatric and forensic neuropsychology practice and explicitly addresses how each concept serves different audience combinations. The [[concepts/dual-audience-design]] concept addresses this challenge directly.

### Authority vs. Approachability
A recurring tradeoff in clinical branding is the spectrum from warm/personal to institutional/authoritative. Moving toward authority (e.g., adding "Institute" to a practice name, using a seal or crest) gains credibility with legal and academic audiences but risks alienating parents who want to feel they are working with a person, not a bureaucracy.

## Key Identity Dimensions

### 1. Practice Name
The name carries significant brand weight:
- **Personal names** (e.g., "Dr. Trampush Neuropsychology") signal individual expertise and accountability
- **Descriptive names** (e.g., "Brainworkup Neuropsychology") balance personality with specialty clarity
- **Institutional names** (e.g., "Brainworkup Institute of Neuropsychology") signal scale, research mission, and gravitas

The "Institute" designation in particular implies a center of expertise, future capacity to expand with associates or fellows, and alignment with academic credentials like university faculty appointments.

### 2. Visual Identity
Key visual decisions include:
- **Logo metaphor** — must resonate with multiple audiences (e.g., a lighthouse evokes both "finding answers" for parents and "illuminating evidence" for attorneys)
- **Color palette** — warm palettes (teal, coral, cream) read as approachable; cool/dark palettes (navy, gold, white) read as authoritative. See [[concepts/brand-color-system]] and [[concepts/brand-theming]].
- **Typography** — humanist sans-serifs feel accessible; serifs feel academic; monospace fonts are inappropriate for clinical contexts. See [[concepts/brand-typography]].
- **Favicon and secondary marks** — distilled logomarks that work at small scale are essential for digital presence

### 3. Tone and Voice
Clinical practice voice strategy must be calibrated to audience. The [[concepts/brand-voice-strategy]] concept explores this more broadly, while [[concepts/clinical-communication-register]] addresses the specific registers appropriate for clinical content. In practice:
- Parent-facing content: conversational, jargon-light, emotionally resonant
- Forensic/legal-facing content: precise, evidence-based, credentialed
- Peer-referrer content: field-appropriate terminology, methodology emphasis

### 4. Letterhead and Document Design
For clinical practices — especially those doing forensic or legal work — letterhead is not merely stationery but a professional artifact that may be entered into legal proceedings. Design considerations include:
- Whether the logo appears top-left, top-center, or as a seal
- Placement of credentials and affiliations
- Whether a single letterhead serves all audiences or multiple versions are maintained

See [[concepts/clinical-report-structure]] and [[concepts/modular-report-architecture]] for related document design considerations.

## Strategic Archetypes

The [[summaries/brainworkup-branding-concepts]] document identifies several archetypes applicable beyond any single practice:

| Archetype | Tone | Mechanism |
|-----------|------|-----------|
| Warm & Supportive | Family-facing | Soft palettes, rounded type, child-focused metaphors |
| Clinical Modern | Mixed | Minimal geometry, clean grids, credential-forward |
| Academic Authority | Institutional | Seals, serifs, publication-linked content |
| Dual Identity | Segmented | One mark, two color treatments, forked web paths |

The **Dual Identity** approach (Concept 7) is the most sophisticated: a unified logomark rendered in warm colors for pediatric materials and authoritative colors for forensic materials, with a website that forks based on visitor type ("I'm a parent" vs. "I'm an attorney"). This is maximally effective but most complex to implement.

## Website Considerations

Clinical practice websites require a different aesthetic from technical or data science sites:
- **Light, airy layouts** — cream or white backgrounds, not dark mode
- **Humanist or serif typography** — never monospace
- **Clinical imagery** — children, families, abstract neuroscience
- **Prominent credential display** — board certifications, institutional affiliations
- **Clear calls to action** — scheduling, referral forms
- **HIPAA-aware testimonials** — outcome data within privacy bounds

For practices currently using technical site generators like [[concepts/quarto]], migration to a custom clinical theme or a platform like Squarespace may be warranted to achieve the right aesthetic. See [[summaries/brainworkup-branding-concepts]] for specific guidance on transitioning from a data-science-styled Quarto site.
