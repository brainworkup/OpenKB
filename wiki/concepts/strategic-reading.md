# Strategic Reading

Strategic reading refers to a deliberate, structured approach to engaging with written material — as opposed to simply reading linearly from start to finish. It is especially important for individuals with [[concepts/dyslexia]], reading disorder, or [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]], where passive reading produces poor comprehension and retention.

The core principle: **never simply start reading an assignment.** Instead, follow a sequenced routine that leverages preview, active engagement, and post-reading consolidation.

## The Core Sequence

### 1. Pre-Reading: Skim First

Before reading a full text, orient yourself to its structure and key ideas:

- Read the **title, subtitles, graphs, introduction, and conclusion**.
- For book chapters (without clear headings), read the first 1–2 paragraphs, randomly sample one topic sentence per page, and read the final 1–2 paragraphs.
- The goal is a mental map of the content before detailed reading begins.

### 2. During Lecture or Discussion

- **Attend the lecture after skimming** — prior exposure creates a scaffold for new information.
- **Take notes** during the lecture. Recording lectures can reduce the cognitive load of simultaneous listening and writing (especially relevant for individuals with ADHD or reading disorder).
- Review and expand notes immediately after the lecture while memory is fresh.

### 3. Post-Lecture: Thorough Reading

- Read the **full assignment as soon as possible after the lecture** — the lecture context improves comprehension of the text.
- For class discussions (rather than lectures), complete skimming, full reading, and note-taking *before* the discussion.

### 4. Annotation and Active Engagement

- **Highlight or underline** main ideas and specific details, using different colors to distinguish between them.
- Write in margins; use Post-It notes to flag answers to study guide questions.
- Pause every 1–2 paragraphs and **summarize aloud** what was just read. If unable to summarize, re-read the passage (aloud if needed).
- Answer comprehension questions embedded within the chapter.
- Supplement complex texts with Cliffs Notes or published critiques.

## Why This Matters for Learners with Dyslexia

Individuals with [[concepts/dyslexia]] often have reduced [[concepts/reading-fluency]] and effortful decoding, which depletes cognitive resources needed for comprehension. Strategic reading compensates by:

- Building **prior knowledge** through skimming before full reading reduces decoding burden.
- Leveraging **lecture context** as an auditory scaffold before encountering the text.
- Using **annotation** to externalize comprehension and reduce working memory load (see [[concepts/working-memory]]).
- Using **repeated exposure** (skim → lecture → read → notes) to encode information more deeply.

Audiobook access and text-to-speech tools (e.g., audio textbook tapes or CDs) further support comprehension by decoupling decoding from understanding.

## Application in Higher Education

Strategic reading is a core skill taught as part of academic support for college students with learning disabilities. It pairs closely with:

- [[concepts/academic-accommodations-higher-education]] — extended time, professor outlines, and lecture recordings create the conditions for strategic reading to succeed.
- [[concepts/iep-accommodations]] — similar frameworks applied at the secondary level.
- [[concepts/writing-accommodations]] — writing strategy parallels (outline, draft, proofread in passes) mirror the structure of strategic reading.
- [[concepts/phonological-processing]] — foundational skill whose weaknesses motivate compensatory strategies like strategic reading.
