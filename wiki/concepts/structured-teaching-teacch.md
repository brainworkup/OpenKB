# Structured Teaching and the TEACCH Approach

Structured Teaching is the core intervention philosophy developed by **Division TEACCH** (Treatment and Education of Autistic and related Communication handicapped Children) at the University of North Carolina, Chapel Hill. It provides a foundational framework for supporting individuals with [[concepts/autism-spectrum-disorder-clinical-features]] across all ages and functioning levels, emphasizing visual organization, predictability, and individualized supports.

See also: [[summaries/CARS2-Manual_extracted]] and [[summaries/autism_recommendations_for_adults_summary]]

## Historical Context

Division TEACCH was founded in 1966 as the first statewide program in the United States for individuals with autism and communication disabilities. It operates nine regional centers in North Carolina and is also the birthplace of the Childhood Autism Rating Scale (CARS), now the CARS2. The TEACCH approach has been empirically validated across several decades and is widely cited in the autism intervention literature.

## Core Philosophy

Structured Teaching rests on the concept of the **"culture of autism"**—a recognition that individuals with autism share a distinctive learning style shaped by their neurology. Key characteristics of this learning style include:

- Strong preference for and reliance on **visual information**
- Difficulty with central coherence (attending to the whole meaning rather than details)
- Challenges with [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]] such as flexibility and attention shifting
- Reliance on routine and predictability
- Uneven cognitive profiles with areas of relative strength and weakness

The primary goal is to use an individual's **strengths**—especially visual processing skills—to teach new skills and compensate for areas of difficulty. These principles apply across the lifespan, from early childhood intervention through adult support programs.

## The Four Components of Structured Teaching

1. **Physical Organization of the Environment**
   Arranging physical spaces to minimize distracting sensory input (visual and auditory), clearly delineate work and activity areas, and make expectations visible. Reduces overstimulation and supports focused attention. For adults, this translates directly to workplace accommodations such as quiet workspaces and written communication protocols.

2. **Individualized Daily Schedules**
   Visual schedules that communicate the sequence, timing, and transitions of activities. Schedules are deliberately varied to build flexibility while maintaining predictability. Formats range from objects and photographs for concrete learners to written lists and digital planners for higher-functioning individuals. Adults benefit from consistent daily routines with tools such as timers, checklists, and color-coded organizational systems.

3. **Work or Activity Systems**
   Structured systems that tell individuals *what* work to do, *how much*, *when they are finished*, and *what comes next*. These promote independent task completion and reduce reliance on adult prompting. In adult vocational contexts, job coaching and structured work environments serve analogous functions.

4. **Visual Structure Within Tasks**
   Visual supports embedded in learning materials themselves—including visual instructions, organization of materials, and visual clarity about task expectations. Supports comprehension and independence across all skill areas and life domains.

## Twin Goals

- **Increasing independence**: Reducing the need for adult prompting and supervision
- **Promoting spontaneous, client-initiated communication**: Enabling individuals to act on their environment and express their needs effectively

These goals address the social and communicative deficits central to [[concepts/autism-spectrum-disorder-clinical-features]] and are directly linked to improved quality of life across all developmental stages.

## Relationship to CARS2 Assessment

In the [[summaries/CARS2-Manual_extracted]], Structured Teaching is described as the scaffold upon which other autism-specific interventions are built. The CARS2-ST and CARS2-HF rating forms map item scores onto five intervention domains (see Tables 12 and 13 in the manual):

| Intervention Domain | Related CARS2 Items |
|---|---|
| Social Interaction | Relating to People, Imitation, Visual Response, Listening Response, Verbal/Nonverbal Communication |
| Communication | Object Use, Adaptation to Change, Verbal/Nonverbal Communication |
| Restricted Interests & Stereotyped Behavior | Body Use, Object Use, Adaptation to Change, Verbal Communication, Activity Level/Thinking Skills |
| Sensory Issues & Associated Features | Emotional Response, Body Use, Visual/Listening Response, Taste-Smell-Touch, Fear/Nervousness |
| Thinking Style & Cognitive Issues | Relating to People, Visual Response, Fear, Level/Consistency of Intellectual Response, Thinking/Cognitive Integration Skills |

An elevated score on a given CARS2 item cluster points directly to the intervention domain requiring attention, making the CARS2 a practical bridge between [[concepts/autism-diagnostic-rating-scales]] and treatment planning.

## Evidence-Based Intervention Strategies by Domain

### Social Interaction
- **Early intervention**: Joint attention approaches (Kasari), DIR/FloorTime (Greenspan & Wieder), pivotal response training
- **Older/higher functioning**: Social Stories (Gray), Comic Strip Conversations, perspective-taking techniques (Garcia-Winner), social skills picture books
- Peer education to create supportive social environments
- **Adults**: Structured social skills groups focused on workplace interactions, relationship navigation, and community participation; interest-based groups and community involvement to build social networks

### Communication
- TEACCH communication curriculum emphasizing the *context*, *form*, and *function* of communication
- Picture Exchange Communication System (PECS) for concrete-level communicators
- Hanen Centre programs (*More Than Words*, *It Takes Two to Talk*)
- SCERTS model (Social Communication, Emotional Regulation, Transactional Support)
- Augmentative and alternative communication (AAC) devices for nonverbal individuals
- For higher-functioning individuals and adults: visual supports for conversation initiation, scripting, topic lists, social narrative techniques, and explicit workplace communication strategies

### Restricted Interests and Stereotyped Behaviors
- Visually individualized activity schedules with deliberately changing content to build flexibility
- "Surprise" or "change" cards embedded in schedules
- Scheduling access to preferred activities so individuals know when they will occur
- Power Cards and special-interest-based assignments to leverage motivational strengths
- Positive behavior support (PBS) procedures to replace problematic routines
- Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA) principles adapted for adult skill-building, including Pivotal Response Treatment (see [[concepts/autism-intervention-strategies]])

### Sensory Issues
- Reduction of auditory and visual overstimulation through physical structure
- Sensory integration evaluation and intervention by occupational therapists (sensory diets, weighted vests, vestibular stimulation)
- Aerobic exercise to reduce self-stimulatory behavior
- Calm rooms and sanctuary spaces
- Application of Dunn's sensory processing model (low registration, sensory sensitivity, sensation seeking, sensation avoidance)
- For adults: occupational therapy targeting sensory sensitivities in workplace and community contexts; noise-canceling headphones and other environmental modifications

### Thinking Style and Cognitive Issues
- Rule-based teaching phrased positively to clarify what to do
- Visual techniques to build meaning and connections between thoughts, actions, and others' perspectives
- Cognitive-behavioral approaches for higher-functioning individuals and adults (Gaus, 2007); CBT adapted for autism (CBT-A) and Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT) for autism are recommended for adults
- Mind/thought mapping, visual worksheets, organized schedules to externalize [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]]
- Theory-of-mind training through social narrative and perspective-taking tools
- Mindfulness and stress management techniques for adult emotional regulation

## Adult-Specific Extensions of TEACCH Principles

While Structured Teaching was initially developed for children, its core principles scale naturally to adult life domains. The [[summaries/autism_recommendations_for_adults_summary]] identifies several adult-specific areas where TEACCH-aligned strategies apply:

### Vocational and Workplace Support
- Structured work environments with clear written instructions and expectations
- Flexible scheduling to accommodate sensory and attentional needs
- Job coaching services for workplace skill development
- Vocational rehabilitation services and workplace accommodations under disability rights frameworks
- Resources: Disability Rights California ([www.disabilityrightsca.org](https://www.disabilityrightsca.org))

### Independent Living Skills
- Life skills training covering budgeting, cooking, household management, and daily self-care
- Visual supports (checklists, color-coded systems, posted schedules) adapted for adult home environments
- Supportive housing options providing varying levels of assistance
- Occupational therapy for daily living skill development

### Mental Health and Wellness for Adults
- Regular therapy addressing emotional and psychological well-being
- CBT-A and ACT for autism for anxiety, depression, and social challenges
- Relationship counseling and interpersonal support
- Structured social and recreational participation aligned with individual interests

### Community-Based Services
- Regional Center services (California) covering in-home behavioral support, speech/language therapy, occupational therapy, respite, and social skills treatment
- Autism Speaks resource guide: [www.autismspeaks.org/resource-guide](https://www.autismspeaks.org/resource-guide)
- BACB-certified behavior analysts: [www.bacb.com](https://www.bacb.com)

## Behavioral Management Principles Across Ages

Both the TEACCH framework and the broader [[concepts/autism-intervention-strategies]] literature emphasize consistent behavioral principles:

- Written rules and expectations posted visibly
- Systematic reward structures using immediate, relational, or experiential reinforcers (preferred over purely tangible rewards)
- Consequences that address behavior rather than the individual
- Consistency across settings and caregivers/supporters
- Family members and support persons trained to reinforce skills and participate in program planning
- Team meetings (therapists, supervisors, family) to maintain consistency and review data

For adults, these principles translate to collaborative support planning involving the individual, family, employers, and service providers.

## Case Applications

### Michael (CARS2-ST case, age 4)
Michael's high ratings in Relating to People, Object Use, and Adaptation to Change indicated a need for:
- High physical structure to regulate emotions and manage sensory input
- Visual schedule and work system to address transition difficulties
- Visual structure in play activities to build pretend and interactive play skills
- Naturalistic communication interventions targeting joint attention, turn-taking, and imitation

### Daniel (CARS2-HF case, age 8)
Daniel's elevated scores in Social-Emotional Understanding, Verbal Communication, and Thinking/Cognitive Integration Skills indicated a need for:
- Written schedules and social rules to make expectations explicit
- Social Stories and Comic Strip Conversations to build perspective-taking
- Cognitive-behavioral strategies for emotional regulation with visual supports
- Educational accommodations for fine motor difficulties, abstract thinking, and processing speed
