# Anxiety: Clinical Features and Assessment

Anxiety is one of the most commonly evaluated dimensions in clinical psychological assessment. It manifests across three primary domains — **affective**, **physiological/somatic**, and **cognitive** — and each domain may present with differing severity in any given individual. Understanding the profile of anxiety expression is essential for accurate diagnosis, treatment planning, and distinguishing anxiety from overlapping conditions such as trauma-related disorders, mood disorders, and substance use disorders.

---

## Domains of Anxiety Expression

### Affective Domain
- Persistent tension and inability to relax
- Emotional fatigue from chronic perceived stress
- Moodiness and emotional sensitivity
- Fluctuating self-confidence and self-esteem under stress
- Dissatisfaction with important relationships and uncertainty about major life goals

### Physiological / Somatic Domain
- Sweaty palms, trembling hands
- Complaints of irregular heartbeat (palpitations)
- Shortness of breath
- Sleep disturbance (difficulty falling asleep)
- These somatic features can overlap with presentations assessed in neurobehavioral status examinations

### Cognitive Domain
- Excessive worry and negative expectancies
- Concentration difficulties and diminished attention span
- Cognitive symptoms are **dissociable** from somatic and affective symptoms — a patient may score high on affective/somatic subscales while showing minimal cognitive anxiety

## Anxiety and Trauma

Anxiety presentations frequently co-occur with trauma history. Traumatic stress can produce recurrent anxiety episodes, intrusive memories, and avoidance behaviors. On the [[concepts/personality-assessment-inventory]], the **ARD-T** (Anxiety-Related Disorders — Trauma) subscale captures these features:

- Reliving a horrible past experience
- Long-standing troubling memories of a bad experience
- Loss of interest in previously enjoyed activities following trauma

In [[summaries/pai_100]], the respondent endorsed two critical ARD-T items at meaningful levels: reliving something horrible that happened (MT, score=2) and loss of interest in previously enjoyed activities since a bad experience (ST, score=1). These endorsements, combined with a coefficient of fit of 0.677 with PTSD diagnostic profiles, underscore the frequency with which trauma-driven anxiety appears in clinical PAI presentations.

In [[summaries/pai_16]], Annette Malan (age 19) endorsed three ARD-T critical items: reliving something horrible (ST, score=1), long-standing troubling memories of a bad experience (VT, score=3), and loss of interest in previously enjoyed activities following a bad experience (ST, score=1). Her profile fit with PTSD was 0.407, and PTSD was listed as the primary diagnostic consideration.

In [[summaries/pai_197]], Lilith Mo (age 30) endorsed two ARD-T critical items: reliving something horrible that happened (ST, score=1) and being troubled by memories of a bad experience for a long time (MT, score=2). Despite the overall profile showing only mild clinical elevations consistent with an adjustment disorder presentation, these trauma-related endorsements suggest a history of adverse experience warranting clinical follow-up — illustrating that trauma symptoms can be present even when global anxiety severity is subclinical.

In [[summaries/pai_26]], Alex Shirley (age 27) endorsed three ARD-T critical items: reliving something horrible (ST, score=1), being troubled by memories of a bad experience for a long time (MT, score=2), and loss of interest in previously enjoyed activities since a bad experience (MT, score=2). His profile coefficient of fit with PTSD was 0.883 — the third highest diagnostic group fit — making trauma-driven anxiety a prominent clinical concern alongside his broadly elevated profile.

In [[summaries/pai_30]], Krishan Patel (age 21) endorsed one ARD-T critical item: troubled by memories of a bad experience for a long time (ST, score=1). This trauma-related endorsement occurred in the context of a profile dominated by manic features rather than classical anxiety, underscoring that trauma-related anxiety can be present as a secondary feature even when mania is the primary clinical picture.

In [[summaries/pai_316]], Eric Roizman (age 55) presents a severe clinical profile that includes extreme anxiety characterized specifically by **phobias and somatic preoccupation**. Critical items flagged for serious pathology across multiple domains — including trauma-related content — alongside a history of trauma and potential conduct disorder, make this one of the more complex anxiety-comorbid presentations in the case series. The report notes PTSD-related features as part of a broad and highly distressed clinical picture.

In [[summaries/pai_35]], Leslie Moreno (age 19) endorsed three ARD-T critical items: reliving something horrible that happened (ST, score=1), being troubled by memories of a bad experience for a long time (VT, score=3 — highest severity), and no longer being interested in previously enjoyed things since a bad experience (MT, score=2). These endorsements occurred in the context of a profile where ARD was the most significantly elevated scale, and PTSD showed a coefficient of fit of 0.371, positioning trauma-driven anxiety as a clinically meaningful feature even while the primary diagnostic formulation centered on adjustment disorder and obsessive-compulsive personality features.

In [[summaries/pai_96]], Corey Ho (age 22) endorsed two ARD-T critical items: reliving something horrible that happened (ST, score=1) and being troubled by memories of a bad experience for a long time (ST, score=1). These trauma endorsements occurred in the context of a broadly elevated clinical profile dominated by depressive and anxiety features, and PTSD showed a coefficient of fit of 0.689 — statistically significant — making trauma-driven anxiety a clinically meaningful feature alongside the primary major depressive disorder formulation.

See [[concepts/trauma-informed-clinical-assessment]] for assessment considerations when trauma underlies an anxiety presentation.

## Anxiety in Standardized Assessment

The **PAI ANX scale** is one of the primary instruments for quantifying anxiety severity in a normed clinical context. It includes subscales for:
- **ANX-A** (Affective anxiety)
- **ANX-C** (Cognitive anxiety)
- **ANX-P** (Physiological anxiety)

In the case documented in [[summaries/pai_02]], the respondent showed significant elevation on the ANX scale with primary manifestations in the **affective and physiological domains**, but notably **without** prominent cognitive symptoms. This differential profile has implications for treatment targeting.

In [[summaries/pai_11]], the respondent presented with anxiety spanning all three domains simultaneously — plagued by worry compromising concentration and attention (cognitive), experiencing fatigue from high perceived stress (affective), and showing overt physical signs including sweaty palms, trembling hands, irregular heartbeats, and shortness of breath (physiological/somatic). The profile fit coefficient with anxiety disorders was 0.781, and generalized anxiety disorder (F41.1) was listed as a primary diagnostic consideration.

In [[summaries/pai_53]], AJ Zhang (age 19, female) similarly presented with anxiety and tension spanning all three domains. She was described as plagued by worry to the degree that concentration and attention were significantly compromised (cognitive), experiencing tension, difficulty relaxing, and fatigue from high perceived stress (affective), and showing overt physical signs including sweaty palms, trembling hands, irregular heartbeats, and shortness of breath (physiological/somatic). The profile fit coefficient with anxiety disorders was 0.781 — identical to [[summaries/pai_11]] — and generalized anxiety disorder (F41.1) was listed as a primary diagnostic consideration alongside major depressive disorder and persistent depressive disorder. Phobic features were also noted: maladaptive behavior patterns aimed at controlling anxiety, with phobic behaviors likely interfering significantly in daily life. The presentation was judged more consistent with multiple phobias or agoraphobia than a simple phobia, and social anxiety disorder (social phobia, F40.10) was listed as a rule-out diagnosis.

In [[summaries/pai_96]], Corey Ho (age 22) showed a significant anxiety presentation spanning all three domains. He was described as plagued by worry to the degree that his ability to concentrate and attend were significantly compromised (cognitive), experiencing a great deal of tension, difficulty relaxing, and fatigue as a result of high perceived stress (affective), and showing overt physical signs including sweaty palms, trembling hands, irregular heartbeats, and shortness of breath (physiological/somatic). The profile fit coefficient with anxiety disorders was 0.725 — statistically significant — and panic disorder (F41.0) was listed as a rule-out diagnosis. He also endorsed maladaptive behavior patterns aimed at controlling anxiety, though only occasionally and to a mild degree.

In [[summaries/pai_100]], the respondent (age 55) showed **no marked elevations** indicating clinical psychopathology, yet described some degree of anxiety and stress within the normal range, along with mild maladaptive anxiety-control behaviors. The profile fit coefficient with anxiety disorders was 0.701.

In [[summaries/pai_16]], Annette Malan (age 19) showed anxiety with primary manifestations in the **affective and physiological domains**, explicitly without prominent cognitive symptoms. Her anxiety fit coefficient was 0.474.

In [[summaries/pai_197]], Lilith Mo (age 30) showed **no marked elevations** on clinical scales. The report indicated she occasionally experiences maladaptive behavior patterns aimed at controlling anxiety, at a mild degree. Her profile fit coefficient with anxiety disorders was 0.554 — statistically significant — making anxiety disorders the second highest diagnostic group fit. The DSM-5 diagnostic consideration was **adjustment disorder, with anxiety** (F43.22), representing the mildest end of the anxiety severity spectrum captured by PAI profiles.

In [[summaries/pai_26]], Alex Shirley (age 27) presented with a broadly elevated clinical profile marked by significant anxiety and tension across affective and physiological domains. He was described as plagued by worry to the degree that concentration and attention were significantly compromised, experiencing fatigue from high perceived stress, and showing overt physical signs including sweaty palms, trembling hands, irregular heartbeats, and shortness of breath. The profile fit coefficient with anxiety disorders was 0.893 — the highest diagnostic group fit in his profile — and the primary diagnostic consideration was persistent depressive disorder, with anxiety disorders and PTSD as additional high-fit conditions.

In [[summaries/pai_30]], Krishan Patel (age 21) described a discomforting level of anxiety and tension with significant worry impairing concentration. Overt physical signs were present (sweaty palms, trembling, irregular heartbeat, shortness of breath), placing the presentation primarily in the **cognitive and physiological domains**. Notably, he did not report a strong subjective experience of tension or major difficulties relaxing, suggesting possible minimization of the affective component. The profile fit coefficient with anxiety disorders was 0.426 — at the significance threshold — with panic disorder listed as a formal diagnostic consideration (DSM-5 300.01, ICD-10 F41.0).

In [[summaries/pai_312]], Elea Zhong (age 20, female) showed **no marked elevations** indicating clinical psychopathology. The report noted she experiences some degree of anxiety and stress described as within the normal range. The profile fit coefficient with anxiety disorders was 0.051 — well below statistical significance — and generalized anxiety disorder (F41.1) was listed only as a rule-out diagnosis. No ARD-T critical items were endorsed. This case represents the mildest end of the anxiety spectrum captured in the current PAI case series, with anxiety as a minor contributory feature rather than a primary clinical concern.

In [[summaries/pai_316]], Eric Roizman (age 55, divorced) presents at the **severe end of the anxiety continuum**. The clinical profile describes extreme anxiety characterized by **phobias and somatic preoccupation** — placing his presentation in multiple anxiety domains simultaneously. This severe anxiety occurs in the context of a highly distressed clinical picture that includes major depressive symptoms, polysubstance dependence, and significant personality pathology, illustrating how extreme anxiety can be a cross-cutting feature rather than an isolated primary diagnosis.

In [[summaries/pai_35]], Leslie Moreno (age 19, female) showed a **significant ARD elevation** — the primary clinical scale elevation in her profile — with a profile fit coefficient with anxiety disorders of 0.384. The report described her as experiencing specific fears or anxiety surrounding some situations, likely to display a variety of maladaptive behavior patterns aimed at controlling anxiety, and exhibiting **obsessive-compulsive features**: perfectionism, rigidity, rumination, and difficulty making decisions. Changes in routine, unexpected events, and contradictory information were noted as generating untoward stress. She may fear her own impulses and doubt her ability to control them. Despite these ARD features, she self-reported no significant anxiety problems — a dissociation between objective profile elevation and subjective symptom denial that warrants clinical attention. The primary diagnostic formulation was adjustment disorder, unspecified, with obsessive-compulsive personality disorder and adjustment disorder with anxiety as rule-outs.

See also [[concepts/pai-assessment]] and [[concepts/personality-assessment-inventory]] for broader context on PAI scale interpretation.

## Adjustment Disorder with Anxiety: The Subclinical End of the Spectrum

Not all clinically significant anxiety presentations involve severe scale elevations. The PAI can identify anxiety-related impairment even when individual scale T-scores remain moderate. [[summaries/pai_197]] (Lilith Mo) and [[summaries/pai_100]] both illustrate this pattern: profile fit coefficients with anxiety disorders exceeded the significance threshold (0.42), ARD-T critical items were endorsed, and mild stress from difficulties in major life areas was reported — yet neither case showed marked clinical scale elevations.

In [[summaries/pai_197]], the primary diagnostic formulation was adjustment disorder with anxiety, characterized by:
- Mild stress from difficulties in some major life area
- Intact social support system serving as a buffer
- Positive self-concept and above-average treatment motivation
- Possible substance use denial (personality characteristics suggesting greater typical alcohol/drug involvement than reported)
- No acute suicidal ideation or violence risk

[[summaries/pai_312]] (Elea Zhong, age 20) pushes even further toward the subclinical end: her anxiety fit coefficient (0.051) did not approach significance, the clinical picture was described as within the normal range, and generalized anxiety disorder appeared only as a rule-out.

[[summaries/pai_35]] (Leslie Moreno, age 19) occupies a similar mild-to-moderate zone: adjustment disorder, unspecified was the primary diagnostic formulation, with a profile fit coefficient for anxiety disorders (0.384) below statistical significance but ARD clearly elevated. Like [[summaries/pai_197]], she reported a mild degree of life stress, adequate social support, and possible substance use denial. Unlike [[summaries/pai_197]], her low treatment motivation and possible disorganization/overwhelm were flagged as treatment complications.

This progression — from frank generalized anxiety ([[summaries/pai_11]], [[summaries/pai_53]]) to adjustment disorder ([[summaries/pai_197]]) to subclinical worry ([[summaries/pai_312]]) — illustrates how the same diagnostic label (generalized anxiety disorder as rule-out) can mark very different positions on the severity continuum. [[summaries/pai_316]] anchors the severe end of this continuum, with extreme anxiety forming one of several major clinical concerns in a highly distressed individual.

## Obsessive-Compulsive Features Within Anxiety Presentations

A distinct subtype of anxiety presentation centers on **obsessive-compulsive features**: perfectionism, rigidity, rumination, difficulty tolerating ambiguity, and fear of one's own impulses. On the PAI, this cluster is primarily captured through the ARD-O (Obsessive-Compulsive) subscale and reflected in DSM-5 rule-outs of obsessive-compulsive personality disorder.

In [[summaries/pai_35]], Leslie Moreno's ARD elevation was substantially driven by obsessive-compulsive features. She was described as:
- Probably seen by others as a perfectionist
- A fairly rigid individual who follows personal guidelines for conduct in an inflexible and unyielding manner
- Ruminating about matters to the degree of difficulty making decisions and perceiving the larger significance of decisions
- Generating untoward stress in response to changes in routine, unexpected events, and contradictory information
- Possibly fearing her own impulses and doubting her ability to control them

Obsessive-compulsive personality disorder (DSM-5 301.4, ICD-10 F60.5) was listed as a rule-out diagnosis. This profile — where ARD elevation is primarily OC-driven rather than phobic, panic, or trauma-driven — represents a distinct anxiety subtype with implications for treatment approach.

## Phobic Features Within Anxiety Presentations

Anxiety does not always present as generalized worry. In [[summaries/pai_53]], AJ Zhang (age 19) showed phobic features alongside the broader anxiety picture: maladaptive behavior patterns aimed at controlling anxiety, monitoring of the environment to avoid feared objects or situations, and a presentation judged more consistent with multiple phobias or agoraphobia than a simple phobia. Social anxiety disorder (F40.10) was listed as a rule-out diagnosis.

In [[summaries/pai_11]], the respondent's anxiety similarly included phobic features — maladaptive behavior patterns aimed at controlling anxiety, with phobic behaviors interfering significantly in daily life. The presentation was characterized as more consistent with multiple phobias or a more distressing phobia such as agoraphobia, rather than a simple phobia. Rule-out diagnoses included social anxiety disorder (social phobia, F40.10), highlighting the overlap between phobic avoidance and social withdrawal.

This same phobic pattern appeared in [[summaries/pai_16]], where Annette Malan was described as likely to display a variety of maladaptive behavior patterns aimed at controlling anxiety, monitoring her environment vigilantly to avoid feared objects or situations. Her presentation was judged more consistent with multiple phobias or agoraphobia than a simple phobia, and specific phobia (unspecified, F40.2xx) was listed as a rule-out diagnosis.

In [[summaries/pai_26]], Alex Shirley similarly endorsed significant phobic symptoms. The report described him as likely to display significant phobic behaviors interfering in daily life, monitoring his environment vigilantly to avoid feared objects or situations, and more likely to have multiple phobias or a more distressing phobia such as agoraphobia than a simple phobia. Specific phobia (unspecified, F40.2xx) was listed as a rule-out diagnosis.

In [[summaries/pai_30]], panic disorder was listed as a formal diagnostic consideration rather than a phobic condition, reflecting the somatic intensity of the anxiety presentation — prominent cardiovascular and respiratory symptoms — in the context of a manic-spectrum clinical picture. This illustrates how panic-spectrum features can be identified by the PAI even when a mood disorder is the primary diagnosis.

In [[summaries/pai_35]], specific phobia (unspecified, F40.2xx) was listed as a rule-out diagnosis, though the primary ARD-driven features were obsessive-compulsive rather than phobic in character. The coexistence of OC features, trauma-related endorsements, and a phobia rule-out illustrates how multiple anxiety subtypes can co-occur within a single clinical profile.

In [[summaries/pai_316]], Eric Roizman's anxiety is specifically described as characterized by **phobias and somatic preoccupation**, positioning his profile within the phobic-somatic cluster rather than the generalized worry cluster.

In [[summaries/pai_96]], Corey Ho's anxiety was described as including maladaptive behavior patterns aimed at controlling anxiety, though only occasionally and to a mild degree. Panic disorder (F41.0) was listed as a rule-out diagnosis, pointing toward the panic-somatic cluster alongside his prominent affective and cognitive anxiety features.

This underscores the importance of assessing the specific structural form of anxiety (generalized, phobic, panic-spectrum, obsessive-compulsive, trauma-related, adjustment-based) when interpreting ANX and ARD scale configurations on the PAI.

## Paranoia and Mistrust as Anxiety-Adjacent Features

In some PAI profiles, elevated anxiety is accompanied by paranoid ideation — a vigilant monitoring of the environment for threats, mistrust of others' motives, and hyperalertness to potential harm. This paranoid-anxious constellation represents a specific clinical subtype.

In [[summaries/pai_35]], Leslie Moreno described herself as tending to closely monitor her environment for evidence that others are trying to harm or discredit her in some way. She likely questions and mistrusts the motives of those around her, despite the nature or history of her relationships with them. This paranoid monitoring occurred alongside her ARD elevation and obsessive-compulsive features, suggesting a hypervigilant anxiety pattern rather than frank paranoid psychopathology.

Similar paranoid-anxious constellations appear across other cases. See [[concepts/paranoia-and-suspiciousness]] for further discussion of how mistrust and hypervigilance interact with anxiety presentations.

## Anxiety and Comorbidity

Anxiety rarely presents in isolation. Common comorbid considerations include:

- **Mood disorders** — anxiety overlaps substantially with depressive presentations. In [[summaries/pai_53]], AJ Zhang showed diagnostic fit coefficients of 0.830 (major depressive disorder), 0.793 (persistent depressive disorder), and 0.781 (anxiety disorders), with all three as primary diagnostic considerations — closely mirroring the profile in [[summaries/pai_11]]. In [[summaries/pai_100]], persistent depressive disorder (0.675) and major depressive disorder (0.636) were among the top fits alongside anxiety. In [[summaries/pai_197]], persistent depressive disorder (0.477) and major depressive disorder (0.467) were present but below the significance threshold. In [[summaries/pai_26]], persistent depressive disorder was the primary diagnostic consideration (fit = 0.860) with major depressive disorder (0.828) also prominent. In [[summaries/pai_312]], persistent depressive disorder (0.069) and major depressive disorder (0.055) were among the higher-fit diagnostic groups but well below significance. In [[summaries/pai_316]], **major depressive symptoms** are explicitly noted as a primary clinical feature alongside extreme anxiety. In [[summaries/pai_35]], persistent depressive disorder (fit = 0.286) and major depressive disorder (fit = 0.271) appeared in the diagnostic group profile but below significance, consistent with the mild-to-moderate overall presentation. In [[summaries/pai_96]], major depressive disorder (fit = 0.757) was the highest diagnostic group fit and the primary diagnostic formulation, with anxiety disorders (0.725) also statistically significant. See [[concepts/depression-clinical-features]] and [[concepts/major-depressive-disorder-clinical-features]].
- **Manic-spectrum presentations** — in [[summaries/pai_30]], anxiety appeared as a secondary feature within a profile dominated by manic features (bipolar I disorder mania fit = 0.562, highest diagnostic group fit). In [[summaries/pai_35]], the respondent described certain problems associated with elevated and variable mood, with others possibly perceiving her as impatient and somewhat demanding, though bipolar I fit was only 0.209. In [[summaries/pai_316]], symptoms consistent with manic or hypomanic episodes are noted alongside anxiety. In [[summaries/pai_96]], bipolar I disorder (mania) showed a very low fit (0.244), making manic features unlikely. See [[concepts/manic-episode-presentation]].
- **PTSD / trauma-related disorders** — ARD-T elevation alongside ANX elevation suggests trauma-driven anxiety; prominent in [[summaries/pai_16]] (PTSD as primary diagnosis, fit = 0.407), [[summaries/pai_100]], [[summaries/pai_11]], [[summaries/pai_26]] (fit = 0.883), and subclinically in [[summaries/pai_197]] (PTSD fit = 0.527, significant); also present as a secondary feature in [[summaries/pai_30]] (PTSD fit = 0.402); not a significant feature in [[summaries/pai_312]] (PTSD fit = 0.005); a prominent feature in [[summaries/pai_316]]; present in [[summaries/pai_35]] (PTSD fit = 0.371) with three ARD-T critical items endorsed; in [[summaries/pai_53]] (PTSD fit = 0.752) — a statistically significant fit; in [[summaries/pai_96]] (PTSD fit = 0.689) — statistically significant, with two ARD-T critical items endorsed.

- **Attentional difficulties** — in [[summaries/pai_53]], the Inattention (INATTN) Index was T=66; in [[summaries/pai_30]], the Inattention Index reached T=99; in [[summaries/pai_312]], mildly elevated at T=56; in [[summaries/pai_35]], Inattention Index was T=66; in [[summaries/pai_96]], the Inattention Index was T=77 — notably elevated — consistent with reported difficulties concentrating and making decisions; see [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]]
- **Personality disorder features** — [[summaries/pai_53]] carried a rule-out for other specified personality disorder with mixed borderline and avoidant features; [[summaries/pai_11]] carried the same rule-out; [[summaries/pai_100]] included a rule-out for mixed borderline, paranoid, and passive-aggressive features; [[summaries/pai_26]] carried borderline personality disorder as a formal rule-out; [[summaries/pai_30]] showed paranoid ideation as a clinical feature; [[summaries/pai_312]] carried other specified personality disorder with mixed borderline features as a rule-out; [[summaries/pai_316]] presents rigid perfectionism, impulsivity, antisocial tendencies, and paranoid mistrust; [[summaries/pai_35]] carries obsessive-compulsive personality disorder as a formal rule-out; [[summaries/pai_96]] lists borderline personality disorder as a rule-out alongside cyclothymic disorder and panic disorder
- **Antisocial features** — in [[summaries/pai_316]], antisocial tendencies and a history suggesting potential conduct disorder are noted alongside anxiety; in [[summaries/pai_96]], no significant antisocial behavior was self-reported (antisocial personality disorder fit = 0.177); see [[concepts/antisocial-personality-features]]

## Social Avoidance and Withdrawal in Anxiety

A recurring interpersonal pattern in anxiety presentations is passive withdrawal from social engagement. In [[summaries/pai_53]], AJ Zhang's interpersonal style was characterized as very uncomfortable and passive in social situations, submissive and withdrawn, with little confidence when interacting with others and unlikely to initiate most relationships. Her passivity was accompanied by anxiety when interactions required unconditional acceptance, and she was expected to avoid social interactions rather than take relational risks. Perceived social support was somewhat lower than average, with relatively few close relationships or dissatisfaction with their quality.

This mirrors the pattern in [[summaries/pai_11]], where the respondent's interpersonal style was similarly characterized as very uncomfortable and passive in social situations, submissive and withdrawn, with little confidence when interacting with others.

In [[summaries/pai_96]], Corey Ho's interpersonal style was described as **modest, unpretentious, and retiring** — self-conscious in social interactions, probably not skilled or comfortable in asserting himself, and likely viewed by others as passive, humble, and unassuming. Both his recent level of stress and his perceived level of social support were described as about average in comparison to normal adults — a favorable prognostic sign.

In contrast, [[summaries/pai_16]] illustrates that anxiety can co-occur with an opposite interpersonal pattern: Annette Malan's interpersonal style was characterized as friendly and extraverted, cheerful and positive in the presence of others, comfortable in most social situations.

In [[summaries/pai_197]], Lilith Mo's interpersonal style was characterized as one of autonomy and balance — assertiveness, friendliness, and concern for others all in the average range, typical of normal adults. Her mild stress from difficulties in some major life area was buffered by multiple supportive relationships, which was noted as a favorable prognostic sign.

In [[summaries/pai_26]], Alex Shirley's interpersonal style was characterized as **withdrawn and introverted** — appearing to others as having little interest in socializing, not inviting social interaction, making no special effort to appear friendly, and deriving little enjoyment from social interactions.

In [[summaries/pai_30]], Krishan Patel's interpersonal style represents yet another pattern: **strong needs for affiliation and positive regard**, resulting in uninhibited, attention-seeking, and dramatic social behavior. The quantity of social contact was prioritized over quality.

## Validity Considerations in Anxiety Assessment

Accurate anxiety assessment depends on valid self-report. Key validity issues include:

- **Negative impression management** — exaggerating symptoms, which may inflate anxiety scores
- **Defensiveness / positive impression management** — minimizing symptoms, leading to underestimation
- **Inconsistent responding** — careless or random responses that invalidate scale scores
- **Substance denial** — personality characteristics associated with anxiety can correlate with alcohol/drug involvement

The PAI addresses these directly through its validity scale structure. See [[concepts/validity-language]] for the clinical language used to communicate validity findings, and [[concepts/clinical-report-structure]] for how validity findings are sequenced in reports. See [[concepts/validity-and-response-styles]] for a broader overview of distortion indices.

In [[summaries/pai_53]], AJ Zhang showed an elevated Back Random Responding score (T=87) — identical in magnitude to the elevation noted in [[summaries/pai_11]] — indicating inconsistency between responses at the beginning and end of the test. The report flagged this as suggesting her approach to the test may have changed over time, possibly becoming less attentive toward the end. All interpretive hypotheses were noted as requiring cautious review. Despite this, response style indicators were within the normal range: negative and positive distortion indicators did not suggest malingering or defensiveness, and the scores on NIM and PIM fell below clinical thresholds.

In both [[summaries/pai_11]] and [[summaries/pai_16]], a notable validity concern was an elevated Back Random Responding score (T=87 and T=92, respectively). The [[summaries/pai_16]] case also showed a highly elevated Inattention Index (T=88). All interpretive hypotheses in these cases were flagged as requiring cautious review.

In [[summaries/pai_197]], all validity indicators were within normal or average ranges. However, the report flagged possible **denial of alcohol/drug use** based on personality characteristics typically associated with greater substance involvement.

In [[summaries/pai_26]], all validity indicators were within normal limits. The profile showed the highest coefficient of fit with the NIM predicted profile (0.902), which reflects high overall clinical severity rather than response distortion per se.

In [[summaries/pai_30]], validity indicators were largely within acceptable limits, but the Cashel Discriminant Function was elevated (T=74) and the profile showed the highest coefficient of fit with the PIM predicted profile (0.656), suggesting possible subtle positive impression management. Additionally, substance use denial was specifically flagged: ALC and DRG estimated scores were 22T and 17T higher than actual scale scores, respectively.

In [[summaries/pai_312]], all validity indicators were within normal or acceptable limits, with no evidence of negative or positive impression management globally. However, substance denial was specifically flagged: ALC Estimated Score was 10T higher than the actual ALC score and DRG Estimated Score was 8T higher than the actual DRG score.

In [[summaries/pai_316]], NIM and PIM scores both fell below clinical thresholds, and the report confirms the results are **valid** — the respondent completed an acceptable number of items, demonstrated consistent attention, and provided a forthright self-report without significant distortion.

In [[summaries/pai_35]], Leslie Moreno's validity profile showed no missing items and consistent responding. No evidence of negative impression management was present. However, the report specifically flagged possible **denial of alcohol/drug use** (ALC Estimated Score 13T higher than ALC; DRG Estimated Score 9T higher than DRG). The response set group profile fit was highest for the PIM predicted profile (0.447, statistically significant), adding a nuance: while global positive impression management was not flagged, the profile's resemblance to a PIM pattern warrants interpretive caution regarding underreported symptoms.

In [[summaries/pai_96]], all validity indicators were within the normal range. NIM and PIM scores fell below clinical thresholds (no NIM/PIM-specific profiles were generated). In [[summaries/pai_02]] and [[summaries/pai_100]], all validity indicators were within normal limits, supporting interpretive confidence in those elevated ANX findings.
