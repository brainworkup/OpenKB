# IEP and School Accommodations for Neurodevelopmental Disorders

Individualized Education Programs (IEPs) and related school accommodations provide structured, legally-backed frameworks for supporting students with [[concepts/adhd-clinical-features]], [[concepts/dyslexia]], [[concepts/dyscalculia]], [[concepts/dysgraphia]], [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]], and other neurodevelopmental conditions in the educational setting. These supports address the academic, behavioral, and motor dimensions of a student's needs. While IEPs are most common in K–12 settings, analogous accommodation frameworks (e.g., disability services offices) extend into college and young adult educational environments.

## What Is an IEP?

An IEP (Individualized Education Program) is a formal document developed collaboratively by parents, teachers, and school specialists. It outlines a student's present levels of performance, specific learning goals, and the accommodations and services the school will provide. Initiating an IEP evaluation typically requires sharing a neuropsychological or psychoeducational report with school personnel.

For children with co-occurring conditions — such as ADHD alongside dyslexia, math learning disability, or writing difficulties — the IEP must address each component as a necessary, but not sufficient, part of the overall intervention plan. See [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.adhd.child]] for a clinical example. Similarly, for a child with co-occurring dyslexia and ADHD, reading tutors must pay careful attention to the student's capacity for focused and sustained attention, and consideration should be given to the potential benefits of a stimulant medication trial to support reading fluency.

## Understanding Attention Problems in the School Context

Attention problems are defined as chronic and severe inconsistencies in the ability to maintain and regulate focus to tasks for more than short periods of time. They are characterized by distractibility, inability to concentrate, inability to maintain attention for long periods, disorganization, failure to complete tasks, and a lack of study skills. Children with attention problems are frequently distracted by both internal and external irrelevant stimuli, even in relatively quiet classroom environments.

These difficulties are distinct from — though often co-occurring with — ADHD, and require their own targeted intervention planning. Behaviorally based interventions are especially effective, involving learning new behaviors and learning how to periodically monitor existing behavior. Specific evidence-based approaches include classwide peer tutoring, computer-assisted instruction, contingency management, daily behavior report cards (DBRCs), modified task-presentation strategies, multimodal interventions, parent training, and self-management programs.

Key attention-related strategies in the classroom include:
- Schedule the most demanding attention tasks in the morning, as focus tends to diminish as the day progresses.
- Allow frequent breaks to move around inside and outside the classroom.
- Vary the instructional medium and pace to sustain interest; emphasize hands-on activities.
- Keep sustained attention demands balanced with more active learning.

## Tiered Intervention: T-Score Thresholds

The D-REF (Diagnostic Rating and Evaluation Form) provides a psychometric basis for activating specific classroom interventions based on T-score severity. This tiered model — with strategies triggered at T ≥ 60 (mildly elevated) and T ≥ 70 (clinically significant) — maps directly onto IEP planning for five key domains:

| Domain | T ≥ 60 Strategies | T ≥ 70 Additions |
|---|---|---|
| Attention/Working Memory | Shorten tasks, cue signals, seating, timers | — |
| Activity/Impulse Control | Praise compliance, ignore minor behavior | Formal behavior management system, calendar reinforcement |
| Compliance/Anger Control | Reduce workload, reinforce early, parent contact | Structured emotional expression program |
| Abstract/Conceptual Reasoning | Sticky notes, planners, ordered steps | — |

This framework, drawn from [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.adhd.dref-intervention-strategies]], complements the broader IEP by providing empirically keyed entry points for intervention intensity.

## Core Accommodation Categories

### Extended Time
- Students with ADHD, reading disabilities, or math learning difficulties typically require extended time (1.5× or 2×) for tests, in-class assignments, and standardized exams.
- Extended time accommodations for high-stakes exams are particularly important for individuals with processing speed weaknesses, enabling them to demonstrate competencies more effectively.
- Testing should occur in a **small, distraction-free environment** outside the regular classroom.
- For timed tasks where real-time extension is infeasible, grading should not penalize incomplete work; students should be given time after class to finish.
- Students with reading difficulties specifically require additional time to complete tests, quizzes, and assignments requiring reading.
- Students with math difficulties specifically require extended time on math tests and quizzes to demonstrate procedural knowledge adequately.

### Assistive Tools and Technology
- When completing math tests, quizzes, and assignments designed to assess procedural knowledge rather than computational skills, students with math learning difficulties should be allowed to use a calculator.
- Graph paper should be provided to help students with poor visual-organizational skills align rows and columns of multi-digit numbers more easily.
- Visual cues should be used to teach operations or skills involving sequencing or serialization.
- **Recorded textbooks** are an appropriate accommodation for students with reading difficulties; students can listen to the textbook while following along with the text.
- Digital resources and assistive technology (e.g., text-to-speech, speech-to-text, digital word logs) should be made available for students with [[concepts/dyslexia]], reading difficulties, or fine motor challenges.
- Dictation software (e.g., Dragon) can be used both at home and at school for students with co-occurring writing difficulties.
- Laptop computers, iPads, or similar devices with spell check, word prediction, read-back, speech recognition, and graphic organization software should be considered for written assignments.

### Classroom Environment
- **Seating placement** should minimize peripheral distractions; this is not always the front row — each environment should be reviewed individually to maximize the student's ability to focus.
- A study buddy can provide copies of lecture notes and serve as a homework contact.
- Provide anticipatory guidance for upcoming events, assignments, and projects; help students use a day planner or calendar.
- Cue students ten minutes before the end of class to finish work, record assignments, and complete other tasks.
- Ensure the student establishes eye contact when receiving directions, and ask the student to paraphrase directions to check comprehension.

### Reducing Task Load and Managing Overwhelm
- For students with reading or attention difficulties, reduce the number of questions or problems to be completed at one time; indicate essential items and offer bonus points for additional completed items.
- Highlighting key words, instructions, and main ideas in texts or handouts can help students manage reading demands.
- For students with fine motor or writing difficulties, typed responses should be permitted on assignments not directly assessing handwriting.
- Break large assignments into smaller, sequentially organized steps; provide feedback and reinforcement at each step.
- Give assignments one sheet at a time or assign every third problem rather than every one; schedule breaks after an optimal attention period.
- Reduce homework loads where possible; limit homework to guided practice on material the student has begun to master.
- Break down long-term assignments into steps to lessen feelings of being overwhelmed.

### Accuracy, Proofreading, and Work Habits
- Emphasize accuracy over speed by providing separate grades for these skills on assignments.
- Provide ample time so the student does not feel rushed; teach strategies for checking work before and after completion.
- Encourage proofreading several hours or days after completing a draft, when possible.
- Consider allowing partial credit for self-corrected errors on assignments and tests.
- Provide instruction in how to check work and practice it with the student regularly.

### Organization and Executive Support
- Assist the student in appreciating the "big picture" before getting caught up in details; ask the student to restate what a task is asking and develop a plan before beginning.
- Provide external organizational scaffolding: someone previews what the student is to do, provides commentary during the task, and reviews actions after completion.
- Help the student develop a well-organized notebook and filing system.
- A skills trainer/monitor serving as liaison between the student and teachers can coordinate organizational support across settings.

## Reading Accommodations and Interventions

Students with [[concepts/dyslexia]] or reading difficulties benefit from a structured set of accommodations and evidence-based interventions embedded within the IEP. See [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.build-reading-skills]] and [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.dyslexia]] for full clinical examples.

### Understanding Dyslexia in the Classroom
Dyslexia means the student is not phonetically decoding words accurately and is processing letter sounds in isolation. As a result, the student may struggle to recognize and decode unfamiliar, low-frequency words as whole words, which slows down reading speed. An educational therapist, reading specialist, or related tutor can help the student manage school demands, attention/executive difficulties, and develop reading skills. The Association of Educational Therapists (AET) is a resource for finding educational therapists.

### Evidence-Based Reading Intervention
- Students should receive an evidence-based intervention targeting reading remediation, with progress monitored daily or weekly using data collection and graphing.
- Intervention should be continuously tailored based on monitored progress.
- Recommended approaches include **Orton-Gillingham**, **Reading Recovery**, and **Lindamood-Bell**. See [[concepts/orton-gillingham-reading-intervention]] for details.
- The **Seeing Stars Program** is recommended specifically for orthographic processing.
- **PALS-R** (Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies in Reading; Fuchs & Fuchs) is recommended for early reading comprehension work.

### Phonological Processing
- Students with weaknesses in [[concepts/phonological-processing]] benefit from structured practice with rhyming, blending sounds, segmenting words, and removing sounds to form new words.
- Incorporating letters alongside phonological tasks (e.g., using letter cards to build and transform words) reinforces skill-building.
- An intensive phonics program is particularly important at early stages: concretely connecting letter sounds to orthography helps retain sight words in memory. Automaticity with phonemes typically requires at least 6 months of intensive intervention (3–4 times per week; daily is better).
- Once phoneme automaticity is established, focus shifts to oral reading fluency and comprehension.

### Phonological Awareness Activities
- **Sound blending games**: say individual sounds and push them together into a word.
- **Syllable segmentation**: clap out syllables, then break syllables into individual sounds.
- **Rhyming**: generating rhyming words (including nonsense/pseudo words) builds sensitivity to word-ending sounds.
- **Sounding out whole words**: encourage students to put all sounds together into a word that makes sense, rather than stopping at partial decoding.

### Orthographic Awareness
- Activities targeting orthographic awareness — such as spelling the same word multiple ways while preserving pronunciation — expand knowledge of grapheme-phoneme correspondences.
- Practice writing letters and words at least twice per week: letter sequences, family names, copying from books, writing messages.
- Writing strengthens the motor cortex to create automated allographic representations of letters, which must then be mapped onto phonemes.
- Explicit attention to capitalization rules (beginning of sentences, proper nouns) should be incorporated via awareness and repetition.

### Reading Fluency
- Teachers should emphasize [[concepts/reading-fluency]] over individual word analysis for students with reading disabilities.
- Recommended fluency techniques: imitative reading, repeated reading, radio reading, phrase reading, paired reading, and echo reading.
- These should be combined with sight-word recognition, decoding, [[concepts/vocabulary-development]], and comprehension instruction.
- Teachers and families can record brief passages from stories the student is interested in but cannot yet read independently; the student follows along while listening, repeating until able to read the passage independently.

### Scaffolding and Comprehension
- Teachers should scaffold reading activities by previewing subject matter, pre-reading end-of-chapter questions and boldfaced headings, and pausing to summarize after each sentence or paragraph.
- Dialogic reading (a shared reading strategy) encourages adults to ask specific questions to promote interest, comprehension, and enjoyment of reading.
- High-interest, low-readability books should be provided to allow students to read for pleasure at an appropriate level.
- Recorded passages from high-interest texts support fluency practice via listening and following along.

### Strategic Reading Routines
A core recommendation — applicable from secondary school through college — is to never begin a reading assignment linearly from the start without prior orientation. Instead, follow a structured sequence:

1. **Skim first**: Read the title, subtitles, graphs, introduction, and conclusion to get an overview before the lecture. For chapters without headings, read the first one or two paragraphs, randomly select a topic sentence per page, and read the final one or two paragraphs.
2. **Attend the lecture** and take notes.
3. **Review notes** after the lecture.
4. **Thoroughly read** the full assignment as soon as possible after the lecture.
5. **Annotate**: Take notes, write in the margins, or use color-coded highlighting for main ideas versus specific details. Supplement complex texts with Cliffs Notes or published critiques.

For class discussions (rather than lectures), complete skimming, reading, and note-taking *before* the discussion. See [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.dyslexia.young-adult]] for the college-level elaboration of this approach.

### Active Reading Strategies (Home and School)
- Use highlighting and underlining to draw attention to main points; use electronic highlighting tools for digital resources.
- Pause after 1–2 paragraphs to briefly summarize the material aloud; re-read (and read aloud) if unable to summarize.
- Answer in-chapter questions to check for comprehension.
- Use Post-It notes with question numbers after finding answers to study guide questions.

### Oral Reading in Class
- Of all in-class activities, reading aloud in front of classmates is the one feared most by students with reading difficulties.
- When a student must read aloud in class, notify them the **day before** so they can practice the passage with a tutor, resource room teacher, or parent at home.
- Allow the student to signal readiness by raising their hand rather than being called on unexpectedly.
- Students may gain confidence by recording themselves reading aloud and listening back.

### Spelling Accommodations
- Teachers should not count spelling errors against students on content-area tests (history, science, etc.); this allows demonstration of subject-matter knowledge without penalty for secondary academic skill deficits.
- Spelling errors should be noted and used as instructional opportunities rather than grade penalties.

### Vocabulary and Grammar
- Students should be encouraged to log unfamiliar words and create flashcards until words enter their sight-word vocabulary.
- Sentence-level activities (identifying correct/incorrect grammatical forms, sentence types, and parts of speech) build language awareness.
- Read complete and incomplete sentences to the student and ask them to identify each; model declarative, interrogative, compound, and negative sentences.

### Progress Monitoring and Motivation
- Reading fluency and comprehension progress should be monitored regularly, with data graphed to track growth and set appropriate goals.
- Maintaining a reading log and reward system can build motivation and a sense of accomplishment; reward effort during protracted periods of difficulty.
- Open communication with the student about reading difficulties is encouraged to support self-awareness and coping.
- Parents and school must do whatever it takes to preserve the student's interest in reading given the extreme effort required.

### External Resources
- Families and teachers are directed to the International Dyslexia Association (IDA) and Learning Disabilities of America (LDA) websites for information and resources.
- Recommended reading for families: *Overcoming Dyslexia* by Sally Shaywitz, M.D., and *Twice Exceptional* by Scott Barry Kaufman, Ph.D.

## Speech, Language, and Motor Accommodations

For students with co-occurring speech, expressive language, or motor difficulties:

- **Speech-Language Pathology (SLP)**: Address articulation and expressive language to ensure these do not impede reading development. Direct vocabulary instruction, sentence modeling, and grammar activities are recommended.
- **Oral expression**: Encourage use of language in naturalistic contexts (ordering food, asking store clerks questions); provide positive conversational feedback; model appropriate syntax and vocabulary without requiring the student to repeat corrections.
- **Occupational Therapy**: Recommended for students with graphomotor difficulties, poor hand/finger coordination, or right-left confusion, to bolster motor efficiency.
- Students who struggle with expressive language in social contexts should be encouraged to take more risks and make mistakes, especially at school, to build confidence in self-expression and class participation.

## Math Accommodations and Interventions

Students with [[concepts/dyscalculia]] or math learning difficulties require targeted IEP supports addressing both foundational numeracy and higher-order mathematical reasoning. See [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.build-math-skills]] and [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.dyscalculia]] for full clinical examples.

### Foundational Counting and Number Sense
- Use concrete activities (e.g., placing objects into egg carton holes) to build rote counting and one-to-one correspondence.
- Practice skip-counting by 2s, 3s, 4s, 5s, 6s, etc. to reinforce multiplication as repeated addition and support math fact automaticity.

### Visual-Spatial Organization
- Provide **graph paper** to assist with alignment of multi-digit numbers.
- Use visual cues to teach operations involving sequencing or serialization.

### Math Fact Automaticity
- Teach memory strategies and tricks (e.g., the finger trick for multiplying by 9).
- Use mnemonics, rhymes, flash cards, and digital games with immediate feedback and reinforcement.

### Hands-On and Manipulative Instruction
- Incorporate manipulatives, drawing, and hands-on activities when teaching mathematical concepts.
- Use physical demonstrations for relational concepts such as mass.
- Attach coins to index cards with illustrative pictures to support coin identification and recall for money skills.

### Real-World Generalization
- Encourage generalization of math skills to real-world contexts: shopping, making change, cooking, and estimating travel time.
- Parents should model their own use of mathematical concepts in everyday life.

### Math Accommodations
- Allow use of a **calculator** on tests/assignments assessing procedural knowledge rather than computational skill.
- Provide **extended time** on math tests and quizzes.
- Monitor math development and explicitly remind students to double-check their math work; teach the routine of checking work as part of every assignment.

## Writing Accommodations and Interventions

Students with [[concepts/dysgraphia]] or persistent difficulties in spelling, written language, visual-motor coordination, and fine motor skills require dedicated IEP supports. See [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.build-writing-skills]] for a full clinical example.

### Evidence-Based Writing Intervention
- Students should participate in an evidence-based writing intervention targeted at their specific areas of weakness (e.g., spelling, written expression, sentence construction).
- Progress must be carefully monitored throughout the intervention, with instruction tailored accordingly.

### Spelling and Vocabulary Development
- Spelling practice should use **multiple modalities**: computer, chalkboard, plastic magnetic letters, and other tactile or visual approaches.
- Letter-manipulation games (forming real or made-up words from a group of letters) support spelling skill acquisition.
- Students should maintain a **personal problem-word list** of commonly misspelled words, updating it as mastery is achieved.

### Written Language Complexity
- Sentence-combining activities build language complexity.
- Creative writing tasks (e.g., rewriting a story by substituting synonyms) enhance language development and vocabulary flexibility.

### Handwriting and Fine Motor Accommodations
- Teachers should **not penalize** students for poor handwriting when fine motor difficulties are documented.
- Students should be allowed to **type responses** on assignments not assessing handwriting, freeing cognitive resources for content.
- Students may be permitted to **dictate responses** to an adult on tests not measuring writing skills directly.

### Assistive Technology for Writing
- **Speech-to-text software** (e.g., Dragon) should be considered to allow students to speak their thoughts; this should supplement, not replace, ongoing writing instruction.
- Laptop computers or iPads with spell check, word prediction, read-back, and graphic organization software are strongly recommended.

### Occupational Therapy Referral
- An occupational therapy evaluation is strongly recommended for students with documented fine motor and gross motor challenges.

## College-Level Accommodations

The same accommodation framework extends to post-secondary education, with additional strategies suited to the independent demands of college coursework. Students diagnosed with ADHD and/or a reading disorder should apply for disability accommodations at the start of college. See [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.dyslexia.young-adult]] for a full elaboration of young-adult-specific recommendations.

### Formal Accommodations
- **Extended time** (1.5× or 2×) on all tests.
- **Low-stimulus or private testing rooms**; option to sub-vocalize multiple-choice questions during tests.
- Professors should provide outlines or PowerPoint copies of lectures prior to class, allowing students to annotate materials during the lecture rather than creating notes from scratch.
- Permission to **audio record lectures** for later review, reducing the cognitive burden of simultaneous listening and writing.
- Access to **recorded/audio textbooks** for all courses.
- Use of dictation software (e.g., Dragon Naturally Speaking) at home and at school.
- Students are encouraged to share copies of their neuropsychological evaluation with their institution's disability services office.

### Strategic Reading in College
Never simply begin a reading assignment cold. The recommended pre/during/post sequence is:
1. **Skim** — title, subtitles, graphs, introduction, conclusion (or first/last paragraphs and random topic sentences for non-textbook chapters).
2. **Attend the lecture** and take notes.
3. **Review lecture notes** immediately after class; stay after to add any ideas not adequately captured.
4. **Thoroughly read** the full assignment as soon as possible after the lecture.
5. **Annotate with highlights, margin notes, or color-coded markers** to distinguish main ideas from specific details. Go to the library or bookstore for Cliffs Notes or critiques of complex books.

For class discussions (rather than lectures), complete all skimming, reading, and note-taking before the discussion.

### Lecture and Note-Taking Strategies
- Experiment with taking lecture notes on a computer versus by hand.
- Stay after class to review and supplement notes with ideas not adequately recorded during the lecture.
- Recording lectures reduces the cognitive burden of simultaneous listening and writing — a particular challenge for individuals with ADHD and reading disorders.
- Annotated outlines or PowerPoint slides provided by professors can serve as a scaffold on which students write their own notes, improving memory encoding.

### College Writing Process
A structured writing process is particularly beneficial for students with reading disorders and ADHD:
1. **Brainstorm** by dictating initial thoughts into an audio recorder or smartphone.
2. **Outline** by listing the thesis and sub-topics before drafting.
3. **Plan paragraphs**: list on one page per paragraph the ideas, facts, and information needed to make each point — this helps determine whether sufficient material has been gathered.
4. **Draft on a computer**.
5. **Proofread in three separate passes**:
   - Content and logical sequencing: ask for each paragraph — What is its purpose? Why does it follow the previous paragraph? How does it relate to the next?
   - Eliminate redundancy; simplify awkward or wordy sentences by reading them aloud.
   - Correct spelling and grammar using software tools.

### Test-Taking Strategies in College
- Survey the entire test first to budget time appropriately.
- Read questions carefully and attend to details.
- Answer easiest or immediately known questions first.
- Prioritize high-point-value items (e.g., essays).
- Skip questions where stuck and return after completing the rest — other parts of the test may provide helpful information.
- For essays, quickly outline main ideas in the test margins using bullet points before writing.
