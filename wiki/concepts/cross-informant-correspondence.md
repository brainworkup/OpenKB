# Cross-Informant Correspondence

Cross-informant correspondence refers to the degree of agreement or disagreement among different reporters describing the same person's symptoms, behavior, or functioning. In clinical work, these informants may include parents, teachers, patients, caregivers, and clinicians. This concept is central to [[summaries/clinical-assessment]] and closely related to [[concepts/multi-informant-assessment]].

## Why It Matters

In psychological and neuropsychological assessment, different informants often observe the patient in different contexts. As a result, discrepancies are common and should not automatically be treated as error. Instead, agreement and disagreement can each carry clinically useful information.

Cross-informant correspondence matters because it affects:
- diagnostic formulation
- treatment planning
- interpretation of rating scales
- understanding of context-specific symptom expression
- decisions about whether more than one informant adds meaningful value

This is especially important in child and adolescent assessment, where behavior may differ substantially across home, school, and clinic settings.

## Core Idea

A low level of agreement across informants does not necessarily mean the data are invalid. It may reflect real differences in how symptoms appear across environments, relationships, or task demands. For example, attention or behavior problems may be much more visible in structured school settings than at home.

Thus, cross-informant correspondence is best understood as a clinical interpretation problem rather than a simple measurement problem.

## Patterns of Correspondence

Three broad patterns are useful when interpreting multi-informant data:

1. **High correspondence**  
   Multiple informants report similar concerns. This suggests symptoms may be stable across settings or readily observable by different people.

2. **Low but meaningful correspondence**  
   Informants disagree because the individual genuinely behaves differently across contexts. This can highlight setting-specific stressors, demands, or supports.

3. **Low correspondence due to artifact**  
   Disagreement may partly reflect differences in measurement method, rater expectations, response style, limited observation opportunity, or bias.

These distinctions connect directly to [[concepts/clinical-data-management]], [[concepts/behavioral-rating-scales]], and [[concepts/validity-and-response-styles]].

## Operations Triad Interpretation

As discussed in [[summaries/clinical-assessment]], the Operations Triad Model provides a useful framework for understanding cross-informant correspondence:

- **Converging operations** — informants agree, suggesting similar symptom expression across settings
- **Diverging operations** — informants disagree because behavior differs meaningfully by context
- **Compensating operations** — discrepancies arise from methodological issues, bias, or measurement artifact

This framework is important because it treats discrepancies as potentially informative rather than automatically problematic.

## Typical Findings

The source synthesis notes that cross-informant agreement is often modest. It highlights findings summarized in research cited within [[summaries/clinical-assessment]] showing overall correspondence around **r = .28**, with stronger agreement for externalizing problems than for internalizing symptoms.

A practical implication is that:
- disruptive or observable behaviors often generate more agreement
- internal states such as anxiety, sadness, or worry often generate less agreement

This links correspondence to [[concepts/anxiety-clinical-features]], [[concepts/depression-clinical-features]], and other domains where symptom visibility varies by observer.

## Clinical Interpretation

When informants disagree, clinicians should consider several competing explanations:
- true context-dependent behavior differences
- variation in opportunities to observe the behavior
- differences in thresholds for concern
- measurement limitations
- response bias or distortion
- developmental factors affecting self-report accuracy

The key point is that disagreement should prompt hypothesis generation, not immediate dismissal of one source.

## Incremental Value of Additional Informants

Cross-informant correspondence is also tied to the question of incremental validity. Additional informants are most useful when they contribute unique information that cannot be obtained from existing sources alone.

For example:
- a teacher may clarify classroom attention demands
- a parent may describe home routines and developmental history
- a patient may report internal distress invisible to others
- a clinician may provide structured observational data

This makes cross-informant correspondence highly relevant to [[concepts/neuropsychological-assessment-workflow]], [[concepts/staged-clinical-intake]], and [[concepts/trauma-informed-clinical-assessment]].

## Relevance to Attention and Neuropsychological Assessment

In [[summaries/clinical-assessment]], attention difficulties are presented as a common referral concern, especially when longstanding symptoms may only receive formal attention after a triggering event. Cross-informant correspondence helps determine whether attention problems:
- appear across settings or only in specific contexts
- reflect longstanding developmental patterns or more recent change
- are better explained by anxiety, mood, environmental demands, or neurological change

This is closely related to [[concepts/premorbid-vs-acquired-attention-difficulties]], [[concepts/developmental-vs-acquired-cognitive-symptoms]], [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]], and [[concepts/cognitive-domains]].

## Best-Practice Implications

Based on the source document, good clinical use of cross-informant correspondence includes:
- planning assessments around likely areas of agreement and disagreement
- interpreting discrepancies as data rather than noise
- using context-sensitive formulations
- selecting instruments that capture dimensional variation when appropriate
- avoiding overconfidence when validity evidence is contaminated by informant overlap

These practices fit naturally within [[concepts/neuropsychological-assessment-workflow]], [[concepts/neurobehavioral-status-exam]], and [[concepts/clinical-guidelines]].

## Takeaway

Cross-informant correspondence is the pattern of agreement and disagreement across reporters in clinical assessment. Its main significance is interpretive: discrepancies often reveal clinically meaningful context effects, while agreement can support conclusions about symptom consistency across settings.
