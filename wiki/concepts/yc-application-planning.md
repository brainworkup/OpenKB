# YC Application Planning

## Overview
YC application planning is the process of identifying unanswered application prompts, organizing them by section, and using that inventory to guide completion of the submission. In this wiki, the concept is grounded by [[summaries/YC Application Questions_20260413233900]] and [[summaries/YC Application Questions_20260416091345]], which capture checkpoints on remaining YC application work and confirm the same core inventory of open questions.

## Core Idea
The main function of YC application planning is to turn a large application into a manageable task list. Rather than treating the application as a single writing task, this approach breaks it into discrete prompts across progress, strategy, market, and legal sections. This supports clearer sequencing, faster drafting, and better coordination with related work such as [[concepts/application-question-tracking]], [[concepts/application-preparation]], and [[concepts/startup-application-materials]].

The newer source also reinforces that this is not just a writing exercise but a planning checkpoint: the application status can be summarized as a finite set of required answers plus a small set of optional supporting materials. That makes YC application planning a practical system for measuring completion, not just brainstorming responses.

## Structure of Remaining Work
Based on [[summaries/YC Application Questions_20260413233900]] and [[summaries/YC Application Questions_20260416091345]], the remaining work was organized into four groups:

### Progress
The sources identify four unanswered questions in the progress section:
- How far along are you?
- How long have the founders been working on this, and how much of that time has been full-time?
- Are people using the product?
- What tech stack is being used or planned?

These questions connect product status, founder commitment, user evidence, and implementation credibility. They also overlap with founder narrative and execution evidence, making them relevant to [[concepts/founder-narrative]], [[concepts/founder-track-record]], and [[concepts/ai-code-generation]] when technical implementation is part of the story.

### Strategy and Market
The sources identify four grouped questions in strategy and market:
- Why this idea?
- Do the founders have domain expertise?
- How do they know people need it?
- Who are the competitors, and what is uniquely understood?
- How will the company make money, and how much could it make?
- Which category best fits the company?

Although counted as four higher-level YC prompts, these include several subparts. Together they define the application's market logic and differentiation case. This area relates closely to [[concepts/application-strategy]], [[concepts/competitive-landscape-analysis]], [[concepts/startup-differentiation]], [[concepts/market-sizing]], [[concepts/founder-evaluation]], and [[concepts/solo-founder-strategy]] where relevant.

The newer checkpoint especially clarifies that this section is where the application must connect founder insight to market proof: not just what the company does, but why this team chose the idea, what they understand about the market, and how that understanding supports revenue potential.

### Legal
The sources identify three unanswered legal questions:
- Whether any legal entity has been formed
- Whether any investment has been taken
- Whether the company is currently fundraising

These questions are operationally simple but strategically important because they frame company maturity and financing status. They connect to [[concepts/startup-fundraising]].

### Optional Items
The sources also note two optional items:
- Coding agent session attachment
- Other ideas considered for applying

These items are not required, but they can strengthen the overall application if they add evidence of technical capability or thoughtful founder judgment. The coding session attachment is especially related to [[concepts/coding-agent-session]].

## Key Takeaway from the Source
The main planning value across both source documents is the explicit count of remaining work:
- 11 required unanswered questions
- 2 optional items

That count creates a concrete planning baseline. It converts vague application progress into a measurable completion problem, which is the practical heart of YC application planning. The newer source also confirms that this inventory was derived by reviewing both the application structure and the separate to-do list, reinforcing the usefulness of maintaining both a canonical form view and a working checklist.

## Why This Concept Matters
YC applications reward clarity, compression, and evidence. Planning helps ensure that:
- every required prompt is answered,
- high-leverage sections are recognized early,
- the founder story stays consistent across sections, and
- optional materials are evaluated strategically rather than added impulsively.

In practice, this concept sits at the intersection of task management and narrative design. It is not only about finishing forms; it is about assembling a coherent startup case across progress, product, market, and financing. The repeated checkpoint structure seen in the source summaries suggests that good YC application planning is iterative: review the open questions, recategorize what remains, and use the count of unanswered prompts to drive the next drafting pass.
