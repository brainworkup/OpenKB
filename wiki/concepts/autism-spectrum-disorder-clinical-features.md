# Autism Spectrum Disorder: Clinical Features, Diagnostic Domains, and Interventions

Autism Spectrum Disorder (ASD) is a neurodevelopmental condition characterized by persistent differences in social communication, the presence of restricted and repetitive behaviors, and sensory processing variations. In contemporary clinical practice, ASD is understood as a spectrum — ranging from individuals who are largely non-verbal with significant support needs to highly verbal, intellectually capable adults whose presentation may be subtle enough to go undiagnosed for decades.

This page synthesizes clinical features across the major diagnostic domains used in structured ASD evaluation, drawing on the semi-structured intake assessment documented in [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]], the CARS2 manual ([[summaries/CARS2-Manual_extracted]]), and autism recommendation resources documented in [[summaries/autism_recommendations_for_adults_summary]]. It also addresses evidence-based interventions across the lifespan.

---

## Standardized Rating Instruments

The Childhood Autism Rating Scale, Second Edition (CARS2) is one of the most widely used and empirically validated instruments for ASD evaluation. It includes two rating forms:

- **CARS2-ST (Standard Version)**: For individuals under age 6, or age 6+ with IQ ≤ 79 or notably impaired communication. Internal consistency α = .93; interrater reliability r = .84.
- **CARS2-HF (High-Functioning Version)**: For individuals age 6+ with IQ ≥ 80 and fluent spontaneous speech. Internal consistency α = .96; interrater reliability r = .95.

Both forms contain 15 items rated on a 1–4 scale (with half-point increments). Ratings assess behavior relative to typically developing peers of the same age, weighting **peculiarity, frequency, intensity, and duration** — not merely presence or absence. Total raw scores convert to T-scores (mean = 50, SD = 10) calibrated on a clinical sample of over 2,000 individuals with ASD diagnoses, and to severity categories (Minimal-to-No Symptoms; Mild-to-Moderate; Severe).

A key property of these instruments is that they are not screeners for the general population. Their primary value is as brief yet comprehensively grounded summary tools within referred clinical populations, contributing to diagnostic hypotheses alongside developmental history, formal cognitive testing, and collateral informant data. Ratings alone are insufficient for diagnosis.

See also: [[concepts/autism-diagnostic-rating-scales]], [[concepts/behavioral-rating-scales]].

## Three Core Diagnostic Clusters

### 1. Social-Emotional Understanding

This domain addresses a person's **cognitive** understanding of others' communication, behaviors, and differing perspectives. It is distinct from emotional empathy per se — it concerns the ability to perceive and articulate how another person may feel or what their perspective may be. Key dimensions include:

- **Reading nonverbal cues**: facial expressions, gestures, body language, tone of voice
- **Perspective-taking** (theory of mind): understanding that others hold different beliefs, intentions, and feelings
- **Responding to emotional context**: noticing and appropriately reacting to others' emotional states

Impairment is rated on a continuum from age-appropriate functioning (responds to subtle cues, consistent perspective-taking) to severe impairment (virtually no ability to read nonverbal signals; unable to recognize that others have differing perspectives). The CARS2-HF places this as Item 1 — Social-Emotional Understanding — reflecting the growing literature on theory of mind deficits in ASD. Research with children and adults consistently demonstrates that even high-functioning individuals who can pass simple theory of mind tasks often fail at more complex real-world applications.

In clinical practice, individuals with Asperger presentations may stand up for a child being bullied or report rule violations (indicating some moral-perspective awareness) while remaining naive targets for social manipulation — a characteristic tension between rule-based social understanding and fluid perspective-taking under ambiguous conditions.

In the intake transcript documented in [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]], the patient was rated approximately 1.5 out of 4 — he understands facial expressions and basic emotional tone, but occasionally misses subtle sarcasm and at times confuses light teasing with bullying. His mother noted a lifelong mild reduction in spontaneous empathic response, though his father offered a counterpoint: the patient is clearly emotionally invested in those around him and would be visibly distressed if someone were hurt. This tension — between observed flat affect and genuine internal emotional engagement — is a characteristic complexity of high-functioning ASD presentations.

The mother also noted that socially, the patient gravitates toward familiar, comfortable topics (especially sports) when meeting new people, using these as conversational scaffolding rather than as genuine restricted interests per se. This adaptive strategy partially masks underlying difficulty navigating the open-ended flow of conversation.

### 2. Emotional Expression and Regulation

This domain rates the capacity to express feelings appropriately and to regulate emotional responses. Key features include:

- **Affective range**: whether the person displays a full range of emotions (happy, sad, proud, angry, scared, anxious)
- **Congruence**: whether emotional expression matches verbal content and situational context
- **Regulation**: frequency and severity of emotional dysregulation events, and whether these occur across multiple settings

A common ASD presentation is **flat or monotone affect** — not because the person lacks internal emotional experience, but because outward expression is suppressed or underdeveloped. This is distinct from deliberate emotional concealment. The CARS2-HF treats emotional expression and regulation as a separate item from social-emotional understanding, recognizing that impaired outward expression and impaired cognitive understanding of others' emotions are related but dissociable.

Under the CARS2-HF rating system, a rating of 3 (moderately abnormal) in this domain reflects flat or excessively inconsistent emotional expression, limited ability to describe one's own emotional states, and serious dysregulation problems that occur frequently in at least one setting. A rating of 4 reflects extreme dysregulation across more than one setting, with extreme mood shifts that are difficult to change.

The patient in [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]] is described by his family as a "black box" — emotionally flat in both positive and negative directions, rarely expressing spontaneous excitement or distress. He answers the phone with a flat "hello" regardless of who is calling. Visiting natural landmarks like Bryce Canyon produces little visible reaction, though this does not appear to reflect genuine indifference. The mother explicitly distinguishes between inability to express and absence of feeling.

However, under extreme stress (losing driving privileges after a third car accident), the patient had an intense outburst involving thrown furniture — consistent with the pattern of long-compressed emotional expression punctuated by rare but intense dysregulation. This pattern — flat baseline with infrequent extreme outbursts — is clinically distinct from moodiness and from the erratic emotional volatility seen in some other conditions.

### 3. Relating to People

This domain focuses on the reciprocal, interactive quality of social relationships:

- **Initiation**: Does the person initiate interactions for social (not just instrumental) purposes?
- **Reciprocity**: Is there a back-and-forth, fluid quality to interactions, or is the exchange one-directional, topic-restricted, or awkward?

The CARS2-HF distinguishes between initiating to get obvious needs met (mildly abnormal), initiating almost totally around special interests with little attempt to engage others in those interests (moderately abnormal), and no directed initiation at all with minimal response to others' overtures (severely abnormal).

In the CARS2-ST (used for lower-functioning or younger individuals), relating to people is consistently one of the items most likely to receive high ratings among individuals with autism — appearing in the top three most elevated items regardless of age group or cognitive level.

The patient in [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]] was rated closer to mild impairment: people actively seek him out, and interactions are generally described as warm. His tennis community provides a structural context in which he is well-liked and socially competent. However, he is not a natural initiator — his mother summarizes this clearly: he is always game when others reach out but rarely reserves courts or organizes outings himself. Conversational reciprocity improved significantly between adolescence and his early thirties.

## Restricted and Repetitive Behaviors

This cluster encompasses:

- **Restricted special interests**: intense, narrow focus on specific topics or domains
- **Insistence on sameness**: resistance to changes in routine, distress when interrupted mid-task
- **Ritualistic behaviors**: actions that must be performed in a specific way
- **Repetitive motor mannerisms**: finger-cracking, hand-flapping, rocking, toe-walking, self-directed behaviors

The CARS2-HF combines adaptation to change and restricted interests into a single item (Item 6 — Adaptation to Change/Restricted Interests), rating based on the most severe difficulty in any one of three sub-areas: coping with change, ritualistic behaviors, or restricted special interests. This reflects the conceptual linkage between these features in higher-functioning presentations, where obvious motor stereotypies may be absent but intense, narrow interests and rigid routines are prominent.

In individuals with Asperger presentations specifically, special interests may be common childhood topics (trains, weather, statistics) pursued with unusual intensity and factual depth, or genuinely unusual topics (actuarial statistics, bus schedules). The individual may amass tremendous amounts of factual information and find the predictability of the interest self-soothing.

Early developmental markers often include lining up objects, arranging items identically, and later compulsive collections in adulthood. These behaviors are often self-soothing — ordering the environment to reduce anxiety. The clinician must also consider overlap with **OCD / Just Right OCD**, in which intrusive thoughts about perceived disorder are relieved only by corrective action — a pattern that may co-occur with ASD rigidity rather than being identical to it.

The patient in [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]] showed early signs of this domain (lining up toy cars, arranging Weeble figures identically from approximately age 2), and adult manifestations include compulsive apologizing after every missed tennis shot, specific food-ordering rituals, and workplace behavior of completing other employees' invoices without authorization because incomplete or disordered work felt intolerable. The last is interpreted as potentially OCD-spectrum rather than purely ASD rigidity.

## Body Use and Motor Features

ASD evaluations include assessment of:

- **Gross motor coordination**: gait, balance, agility
- **Fine motor skills**: handwriting, fastening buttons, using scissors, tying shoes
- **Repetitive motor behaviors**: rocking, spinning, hand-flapping, toe-walking, self-directed behaviors
- **Sensory-motor integration**: how motor output is coordinated with sensory input

Under the CARS2 system, any current obvious deviant motor behaviors (posturing, spinning, rocking, toe-walking, self-directed aggression) automatically warrant a rating of 3 or higher on the CARS2-HF Body Use item, depending on persistence. Fine motor difficulties severe enough that the person actively resists certain tasks also support higher ratings.

Motor difficulties and sensory sensitivities are considered associated features of both autism and Asperger's Disorder in DSM-IV-TR. Motor clumsiness and fine motor difficulties are specifically identified as associated features of Asperger's Disorder. Gait and posture abnormalities may become more noticeable with age.

The patient in [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]] showed early signs of low muscle tone (noted as "placid" at Easter Seals at age 3–4), fine motor difficulties (pencil grip, scissors use, button fastening), and a compulsive finger-cracking habit under stress or deep thought. Paradoxically, he demonstrates excellent proprioceptive control in sports — precise topspin and slice control in tennis, accurate disc golf throws over long distances — illustrating domain-specific motor learning rather than global motor impairment.

## Sensory Processing Differences

Sensory atypicalities are a core feature of ASD, typically more pronounced in childhood and often improving with age:

- **Auditory**: over- or under-responsiveness to sounds; failure to orient to one's own name (an early red flag); preference for or aversion to specific sounds
- **Visual**: unusual visual fascinations; peripheral rather than direct gaze; difficulty shifting visual attention from high-interest items; clear preference for visual learning
- **Touch**: rigidity to physical contact; under- or over-reaction to pain
- **Taste/smell/food texture**: restricted diet, food texture aversions, unusual smell-seeking or smell-avoiding behaviors

The CARS2-HF addresses near-receptor sensory issues (taste, smell, touch) with particular attention to pervasiveness across settings. For higher-functioning individuals, the most common sensory manifestations tend to be clothing texture preferences, food selectivity, and unusual pain thresholds rather than the more obvious mouthing and rubbing behaviors seen in lower-functioning presentations. To receive a rating of 3 or higher on the CARS2-HF sensory item, the unusual preferences must be obvious across multiple settings **and** the individual self-reports these difficulties or they are obvious on direct observation.

Failure to orient to one's own name as a toddler — selectively non-responsive rather than globally non-responsive — is characteristic of ASD-related auditory processing differences rather than peripheral hearing impairment, and has been documented in retrospective video studies as early as 12 months. The patient in [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]] showed this pattern, leading initially to suspicion of hearing loss. He also never went through a babbling stage — a cluster of early developmental markers now recognized as potential early ASD indicators.

Other sensory features noted in the intake: effortful and obligatory hugging since childhood, restricted palate with texture aversions (somewhat expanded in adulthood), and extensive side-eye observation throughout elementary school when assessing social situations.

## Verbal and Nonverbal Communication

### Verbal Communication

The CARS2 rates verbal communication across a broad range — from total muteness through bizarre or meaningless language. Key dimensions include:

- Conversational reciprocity and length
- Unusual vocal intonation, rate, or volume
- Echolalia, pronoun reversal, or made-up/repetitive phrases
- Pragmatic language use
- Presence of overly formal or pedantic speech style

The CARS2-HF is particularly sensitive to the Asperger phenotype: pedantic language, one-sided communication with verbosity on special interests, failure to appreciate conventional conversational rules, and overly formal vocabulary that seems more sophisticated than expected for age. Individuals with Asperger presentations have been described as speaking like "little adults."

For the CARS2-ST (used with lower-functioning individuals), verbal communication is consistently the item most likely to receive high ratings across nearly all comparison groups. In the CARS2-HF, verbal communication ratings differentiate between individuals who have problems with reciprocal conversation only (mildly abnormal), those who have both reciprocal conversation difficulties and verbal oddities (moderately abnormal), and those unable to sustain a back-and-forth conversation at all (severely abnormal).

The patient in [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]] had severe early language delay — non-verbal until approximately age 4, never babbled, and was putting together two-word sentences only around age 4. Language developed through intensive speech therapy (including two years at the Callier Center). Adult speech remains dysfluent with word-finding difficulties, false starts, and flat/monotone prosody. Topic restriction — defaulting to sports in social situations — persists as a residual feature. Disentangling this from a developmental language disorder is a key diagnostic challenge. See also [[concepts/speech-language-development-disorders]].

### Nonverbal Communication

The CARS2-HF treats nonverbal communication (Item 12) as including gaze to regulate and understand interactions, facial expressions, and gestures in combination with verbalizations across instrumental, descriptive, and emphatic functions. Greater emphasis is placed on the individual's use of nonverbal signals than on their response to others' signals.

Eye contact in ASD may be avoided persistently, inconsistently integrated with speech, or effortful rather than automatic. In higher-functioning presentations, the clinician attends to whether eye contact is genuinely integrated with verbalizations or whether it is present but mechanically managed — disconnected from the communicative act.

The patient's eye contact is effortful — he must consciously manage it while simultaneously handling word-finding and formulation. The mother and father disagreed somewhat on severity: the father views the pattern as within the normal range for speakers managing cognitive load; the mother believes it is more significant. The patient used side-eye observation extensively in childhood — assessing social situations through peripheral vision rather than direct gaze.

## Thinking Style and Cognitive Integration

The CARS2-HF introduces a dedicated item (Item 13 — Thinking/Cognitive Integration Skills) not present in the original CARS, reflecting the growing literature on **weak central coherence** in ASD. This domain assesses:

- The ability to understand the meaning of larger concepts (not just accumulate details)
- The ability to discriminate relevant from irrelevant details
- The ability to integrate information into a meaningful overview rather than a piecemeal, disjointed understanding

Weak central coherence — the tendency to focus on details while missing the larger meaning — impacts all areas of learning and translates into a piecemeal, detail-focused understanding of the social world. Individuals may show great attention to specific concrete facts while requiring persistent prompting from others to grasp the overall conceptualization.

This feature is directly related to [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]] — particularly the flexibility, planning, and organization difficulties documented across studies of both autism and Asperger presentations. Over 80% of executive function studies have documented deficits in this population, with flexibility, planning, and organization most affected. Difficulties with rapid attention switching are also well-documented and are hypothesized to interfere with the rapidly changing demands of reciprocal social interaction.

In clinical practice, this domain manifests as: discipline that doesn't seem to change behavior because the child cannot abstract cause-effect relationships; difficulty understanding why social interactions went wrong despite knowing all the facts; and a thinking pattern that is very detail-focused yet struggles to integrate details into conceptual meaning.

## Knowledge Translation and the Thought-Action Gap

One distinctive feature discussed at length in [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]] is what the father describes as a fundamental disconnect between knowing and doing — observed across speech, tennis strategy, and daily judgment. The patient knows intellectually what he should do (be more aggressive at the net; stop driving when tired; complete only his assigned work), but cannot reliably translate this knowledge into spontaneous action under real-world conditions.

This connects to [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]] and may reflect difficulties with proceduralization of learned behaviors: skills that have been explicitly learned but not fully automatized remain vulnerable to breakdown under fatigue, stress, or competing demands. The father's dancing analogy is apt: if you have to think about every step, you cannot dance well. The patient must consciously manage things — eye contact, social initiation, work boundaries, driving decisions — that others perform automatically.

This pattern is also visible in the CARS2-HF framework, which notes that for high-functioning individuals, the challenge is often not knowledge deficits but execution deficits — knowing the social rule while being unable to apply it fluidly in real-time.

## Severity Dimensions: Autism vs. Cognitive-Language Functioning

A clinically important distinction, explicitly addressed in the CARS2 manual, is that ASD severity and cognitive-language functioning are **two separate dimensions** that must be evaluated independently:

- **Cognitive/language dimension**: ranges from severe intellectual disability with no verbal skills through average or above-average intelligence with well-developed academic skills
- **Autism dimension**: ranges from mild (poor social interactions but generally enjoys them; has special interests but is relatively flexible) to severe (difficulty tolerating even brief social interactions; extreme repetitive behaviors; extreme rigidity)

A widely held assumption is that high-functioning autism or Asperger's Disorder means the person's autism is mild. This is not always the case — someone may have average or above-average intelligence and fluent speech while still showing severe social inflexibility, extreme rigidity, or pervasive dysregulation. Conversely, a person with mild intellectual disability who is relatively social and flexible may function more successfully than someone with intact cognition but extreme anxiety in social settings.

High-functioning autism (HFA) is used for individuals with verbal communication skills and intellectual abilities above the intellectually disabled range who nonetheless meet full criteria for Autistic Disorder. Asperger's Disorder (as defined in DSM-IV) additionally requires no clinically significant early language or cognitive delay. As of 2005 data from Division TEACCH, 53% of individuals with an ASD diagnosis had IQ scores above 80 — reflecting the substantial proportion of the spectrum at the higher end.

## Diagnostic Thresholds and Functional Impairment

A diagnosis of ASD requires that features be:
1. **Pervasive** — present across multiple settings, not just one context
2. **Impairing** — causing real-world difficulty in home, school, work, or social functioning
3. **Developmental** — symptoms were present early in life (prior to age 4 or 5), even if not identified at the time
4. **Not better explained** by another condition (though comorbidities are common)

The CARS2 cutoff scores provide clinical benchmarks: CARS2-ST scores of 30+ (children) or 28+ (adolescents/adults) suggest likely ASD and warrant further comprehensive evaluation. CARS2-HF scores of 28+ similarly support ASD as a strong diagnostic hypothesis. These cutoffs are not endpoints — they are starting points for individualized assessment.

The clinician in [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]] explicitly notes that while the patient shows features across nearly all diagnostic domains, many have improved substantially from a severe early presentation — a trajectory attributed to intensive early intervention (Callier Center, speech therapy, occupational therapy), heavy parental involvement, and structured educational support. The key clinical question is whether the residual features constitute a diagnosable ASD profile or represent a related neurodevelopmental pattern.

## Evidence-Based Interventions

Intervention recommendations differ substantially by age and presenting profile. The following synthesizes guidance from [[summaries/autism_recommendations_for_adults_summary]] and [[concepts/autism-intervention-strategies]].

### Applied Behavior Analysis (ABA)

ABA is the most empirically validated intervention approach for ASD. Key features of strong ABA programs include:

- Designed and monitored by a Board-Certified Behavior Analyst (BCBA)
- Individualized after detailed assessment; tailored to specific deficits and functional goals
- Pivotal Response Treatment uses individual interests to increase motivation for social and communicative engagement
- Generalization tasks built in so skills transfer across settings
- Data on skills and behavior problems recorded and analyzed regularly
- Family members trained to reinforce skills and involved in planning and review

ABA principles can be adapted for adult skill-building, not just pediatric intervention. For adults, ABA-derived strategies support workplace skill development, independent living tasks, and communication goals.

Provider resources: BACB certified analysts at [www.bacb.com](https://www.bacb.com); Regional Centers in California; [Autism Speaks resource guide](https://www.autismspeaks.org/resource-guide).

### Social Skills Training

Structured social skills groups — run by speech-language therapists or psychologists — are recommended across the lifespan. These groups emphasize:

- Social conversation and turn-taking
- Perspective-taking practice
- Navigating group interactions

For **children and adolescents**, social stories teach behavioral scripts for specific situations. For **adults**, structured groups focused on workplace relationships, friendship, and romantic partnerships address the distinct social demands of adult life. Interest-based community groups (clubs, volunteer activities) provide naturalistic contexts for social engagement.

See [[concepts/autism-adult-support]] for additional adult-specific social guidance.

### Structure and Routine

Predictable daily schedules reduce anxiety and support behavioral regulation for individuals with ASD across the lifespan. Practical tools include:

- Visual schedules posted in home or workspace
- Timers for activity transitions
- Color-coded labels and designated areas for belongings
- Written chore charts and responsibility lists

Routine and structure remain important for adults — particularly when managing workplace demands, independent living, or sensory regulation needs.

### Behavioral and Emotional Support

- **Clear rules and expectations**: Written rules posted visibly; predictable consequences explained in advance
- **Reward systems**: For children, immediate relational rewards (shared activities with a caregiver) are preferred over tangible treats; for adults, self-monitoring and structured feedback serve similar functions
- **Cognitive Behavioral Therapy (CBT)**: CBT adapted for ASD (sometimes called CBT-A) is recommended for adults to address anxiety, depression, social skills, and emotional regulation
- **Acceptance and Commitment Therapy (ACT)**: An emerging approach for autism in adults, supporting psychological flexibility
- **Mindfulness and stress management**: Practical techniques for managing sensory overload and interpersonal stress

### Parent Training and Family Support

For families of children with ASD:

- **Parent Training**: Enrollment with a child psychologist or ABA provider; teaches strategies to manage specific challenging behaviors based on their function
- **Parent-Child Interaction Therapy (PCIT)**: Evidence-based; reduces challenging behavior; improves parent-child communication; includes in-the-moment coaching
- Family members trained to reinforce skills and maintain consistency across settings

Recommended books for caregivers:
- *Autism Spectrum Disorders: What Every Parent Needs to Know* — Rosenblatt & Carbone (AAP)
- *An Early Start for Your Child with Autism* — Rogers, Dawson & Vismara
- *Raising a Child with Autism: A Guide to ABA for Parents* — Shira Richman
- *Educating Children with Autism* — National Academy of Science

### Occupational Therapy

Occupational therapy addresses:
- Fine motor skill development
- Sensory processing difficulties (noise sensitivity, texture aversions, tactile defensiveness)
- Daily living skills across the lifespan

For adults, OT can support workplace sensory accommodations (noise-canceling headphones, modified lighting), independent living skill development, and management of sensory environments.

### Adult-Specific Interventions

The primary clinical literature on ASD interventions has historically focused on children. However, adult-specific recommendations include:

| Domain | Recommended Approach |
|---|---|
| Therapy | CBT adapted for autism (CBT-A), ACT for autism, individual or group therapy |
| Workplace | Structured work environment, written instructions, quiet workspace, flexible scheduling, job coaching |
| Independent Living | Life skills training (budgeting, cooking, time management), supportive housing if needed |
| Social | Adult interest-based social groups, community involvement, volunteer opportunities |
| Sensory | Occupational therapy, workplace sensory accommodations |
| Vocational | Vocational rehabilitation services, workplace accommodations, career coaching |
| Mental Health | Regular therapy for anxiety and depression, relationship counseling, mindfulness |

See [[concepts/autism-adult-support]] for cross-document synthesis of adult-specific guidance.

## Differential Considerations

- **OCD / Just Right OCD**: compulsive correction of perceived disorder; intrusive thoughts relieved only by action — may co-occur with or mimic ASD rigidity
- **Developmental language disorder / speech dyspraxia**: may account for some social and cognitive features without a primary ASD diagnosis; see [[concepts/speech-language-development-disorders]]
- **Executive function deficits**: poor planning, judgment under fatigue, difficulty shifting cognitive set; see [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]]
- **ADHD**: attentional variability, sequencing difficulties; frequently comorbid with ASD; higher-functioning individuals with ADHD show different CARS2-HF item patterns
- **Learning Disorders**: may share features with ASD in social-emotional understanding and anxiety domains but differ markedly on General Impressions and social reciprocity items
- **Social anxiety**: avoidance of social situations could be attributed to speech-related self-consciousness, but breadth of features across domains makes primary anxiety explanation less parsimonious
- **Asperger's Disorder (historical)**: ASD without early language delay — not strictly applicable where confirmed early language delay is present, though adult phenotype may resemble high-functioning ASD

## Clinical Assessment Approach

Best-practice ASD evaluation is collaborative and multi-informant:

- Patient self-report
- Parent/caregiver report (using instruments like the CARS2-QPC)
- Teacher or other collateral informant report (especially for CARS2-HF, where multi-setting information is required for items 2, 8, 9, and 10)
- Clinician observation during session
- Structured rating across defined domains
- Formal neuropsychological testing
- Developmental history confirming early onset

The CARS2-HF specifically requires convergent evidence from multiple sources because the challenge of assessing autism-related behavior in relatively high-functioning individuals makes single-source evaluation insufficient. Information about early development — even if not formally rated on the instrument — is essential to establishing the developmental criterion.

Parents should not be asked to complete the rating booklets directly; their information is gathered through the CARS2-QPC and structured interview, then integrated by the professional making the ratings.

This aligns with best-practice methodology as documented in [[concepts/neuropsychological-assessment-pipeline]] and [[concepts/cognitive-domains]]. The [[concepts/staged-clinical-intake]] approach — extended history-taking before formal testing — allows the clinician to calibrate hypotheses and select appropriate measures.
