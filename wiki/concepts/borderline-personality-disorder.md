# Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD)

Borderline Personality Disorder (BPD) is a severe, pervasive pattern of instability affecting emotional regulation, self-image, interpersonal relationships, and impulse control. It is classified under DSM-5 code 301.83 (ICD-10: F60.3) and is among the most frequently encountered personality disorder diagnoses in clinical settings.

## Core Clinical Features

### Emotional Dysregulation
- Rapid and extreme mood swings (affective lability)
- Episodes of poorly controlled, explosive anger
- Intense emotional reactions disproportionate to precipitating events
- Chronic feelings of emptiness and lack of purpose

### Identity Disturbance
- Unstable and poorly established self-concept
- Wildly fluctuating self-esteem — oscillating between severe self-doubt and exaggerated confidence/grandiosity
- Positive self-regard that may be defensive in response to underlying feelings of emptiness
- Uncertainty about major life issues and sense of direction

### Interpersonal Instability
- History of intense, volatile relationships
- Pervasive fear of abandonment or rejection (real or imagined)
- Anxious ambivalence in close relationships: bitterness and resentment alternating with dependency
- Difficulty trusting others, including treating professionals
- Strong needs for attention and affiliation that may be perceived by others as controlling

### Impulsivity and Self-Harmful Behavior
- Proneness to impulsive, self-harmful, or self-destructive behaviors
- Risk domains include spending, sexual behavior, and substance use
- Elevated risk for self-mutilation and suicidal behavior

## BPD in the PAI Assessment Context

The [[concepts/personality-assessment-inventory]] (PAI) includes dedicated scales and subscales sensitive to borderline features. BPD-related features appear across multiple PAI clinical reports at varying levels of prominence.

### Primary BPD Presentation — Nat Lim ([[summaries/pai_01]])
BPD was among the primary diagnostic considerations, with a coefficient of fit of 0.746 against the borderline personality disorder profile — a statistically significant association. The PAI clinical narrative described a profile consistent with "personality functioning within the borderline range," noting:
- Emotional lability and episodes of poorly controlled anger
- Identity instability and lack of life direction
- A history of intense and volatile relationships with fears of abandonment
- Impulsivity with potential for self-harm or suicidal behavior
- Presentation "in a state of crisis"

### Borderline Features as a Secondary Finding — Itamar Cohen ([[summaries/pai_06]])
In the PAI report for Itamar Cohen (IC24), BPD was not a primary diagnostic consideration, but borderline-range interpersonal features were noted in the clinical narrative. Specifically, the report described:
- A history of involvement in intense and volatile relationships
- Preoccupation with fears of being abandoned or rejected by important others
- A coefficient of fit of .469 against the BPD clinical profile (approaching but below the .42 significance threshold)
- The primary diagnostic possibilities were obsessive-compulsive disorder, obsessive-compulsive personality disorder, somatic symptom disorder, and specific phobia

This case illustrates how borderline features — particularly relational instability and abandonment fears — can appear as secondary dimensions within profiles primarily driven by paranoid, somatic, and obsessive-compulsive pathology. The [[concepts/validity-and-response-styles]] section of that report also noted mixed impression management (both positive and negative distortion), complicating interpretation.

### Borderline Features as a Rule-Out — AJ Zhang ([[summaries/pai_11]])
In the PAI report for AJ Zhang (npsych230112), a 19-year-old female, BPD appeared in the **Rule Out** section of DSM-5 diagnostic possibilities as "Other specified personality disorder with mixed borderline, avoidant features" (301.89 / F60.89). The BPD coefficient of fit was 0.769, making it the fourth highest-fitting diagnostic group overall — a statistically significant association.

The clinical picture for AJ Zhang overlapped with BPD in several key domains:
- Harsh, negative self-evaluation with chronic self-doubt, pessimism, and identity uncertainty
- Passive, submissive, and socially withdrawn interpersonal style with fears of non-acceptance
- Suicidal ideation with endorsement of self-harm behavior (BOR-S item 183: "When I'm upset, I typically do something to hurt myself" — Mainly True)
- Critical item endorsement of "I have no interest in life" (DEP-A item 206 — Very True)
- Elevated Suicide Potential Index (T = 68) and Level of Care Index (T = 72)

However, the profile was more prominently characterized by depressive and anxious features (major depressive disorder fit = 0.830; anxiety disorders = 0.781) with relative absence of the anger dyscontrol, impulsivity, and overt relational volatility that typify primary BPD presentations. The avoidant personality dimension — marked withdrawal, passivity, and social anxiety — was equally prominent, yielding the mixed borderline/avoidant rule-out formulation.

The validity profile for AJ Zhang also noted elevated Back Random Responding (T = 87), indicating inconsistency between early and late test responses, which required cautious interpretation of the overall profile.

### BPD as a Rule-Out — Aria Dewey ([[summaries/pai_15]])
In the PAI report for Aria Dewey (npsych230323), a 23-year-old female, BPD appeared in the **Rule Out** section of DSM-5 diagnostic possibilities (301.83 / F60.3). The primary diagnostic consideration was Adjustment Disorder, Unspecified. The coefficient of fit against the BPD clinical profile was 0.462, which is a statistically significant association.

The clinical picture for Aria Dewey showed substantial overlap with BPD across multiple domains:
- **Identity disturbance**: poorly established self-concept that fluctuates between harsh self-criticism and relative self-confidence; self-esteem tied to the status of close relationships; sense of incompleteness and inadequacy apart from relational anchoring
- **Interpersonal instability**: anxious ambivalence in close relationships marked by bitterness and resentment on one hand and dependency and anxiety about possible rejection on the other; strong needs for attention and affiliation perceived by others as controlling; belief that social relationships offer little support
- **Impulsivity**: reported proneness to self-harmful behaviors including spending, sex, and substance use; antisocial character features including marked need for stimulation, reckless behavior, and thrill-seeking
- **Affective lability**: emotional lability, unhappiness, and anger; elevated and variable mood; viewed by others as impatient and demanding
- **Suicidal ideation**: periodic and possibly transient thoughts of self-harm; Suicide Potential Index T = 71; Chronic Suicide Risk Index T = 68; profile fit to self-mutilation behavior group = 0.432 (statistically significant)

Notably, the symptom behavior group coefficients of fit for Aria Dewey also showed statistically significant associations with self-mutilation (0.432), current aggression (0.431), suicide history (0.422), and current suicide (0.420). Her Treatment Process Index was markedly elevated (T = 81), indicating a difficult treatment course with likelihood of reversals. The therapeutic relationship was flagged as carrying special significance given her limited social support.

The validity profile for Aria Dewey noted some inconsistent responses to similar items and possible denial of substance use (ALC Estimated Score 19T higher than ALC; DRG Estimated Score 17T higher than DRG), complicating interpretation of substance-related findings.

Trauma-related critical items were endorsed — including reliving a horrible experience (ARD-T items 34, 114) and loss of interest following a bad experience (item 274: Very True) — suggesting possible adverse history consistent with trauma features, though the ARD scale was not among the highest elevations overall.

### BPD as a Rule-Out — Annette Malan ([[summaries/pai_16]])
In the PAI report for Annette Malan (npsych230209), a 19-year-old female, BPD appeared in the **Rule Out** section of DSM-5 diagnostic possibilities (301.83 / F60.3). The primary diagnostic considerations were posttraumatic stress disorder and alcohol use disorder (mild). The coefficient of fit against the BPD clinical profile was 0.442 — at the threshold of statistical significance.

The clinical picture for Annette Malan showed meaningful overlap with BPD features:
- **Identity uncertainty**: described as uncertain about major life issues with little sense of direction or purpose in her life as currently structured
- **Impulsivity**: reported as quite impulsive and prone to behaviors likely to be self-harmful or self-destructive, including spending, sex, and/or substance abuse
- **Mood variability**: elevated and variable mood; irritability toward others perceived as thwarting her plans; relationship strain
- **Risk-taking and recklessness**: adventurousness, tendency toward risky or dangerous behavior, low boredom tolerance, impulsive excitement-seeking

However, unlike primary BPD presentations, Annette Malan's profile was dominated by trauma-related (PTSD) and alcohol use features, with the BPD features appearing as personality dimensions within a broader clinical picture. Her interpersonal style was notably characterized as friendly and extraverted rather than volatile or conflicted, and she denied significant problems with antisocial behavior, empathy deficits, undue suspiciousness, or depression.

A notable validity concern was her Back Random Responding score (T = 92), indicating considerable inconsistency in responses between early and late portions of the test. The Inattention Index was also elevated (T = 88), suggesting attentional factors may have influenced her responses toward the end of testing.

### BPD as a Rule-Out — Summer Young ([[summaries/pai_17]])
In the PAI report for Summer Young (npsych230105), a 24-year-old female, BPD appeared in the **Rule Out** section of DSM-5 diagnostic possibilities (301.83 / F60.3). The primary diagnostic considerations were Bipolar I disorder (current or most recent episode manic, unspecified) and Cyclothymic disorder. The coefficient of fit against the BPD clinical profile was **0.729** — the highest-fitting diagnostic group in the entire profile — a strongly statistically significant association.

The clinical picture for Summer Young showed substantial and broad overlap with BPD features:
- **Interpersonal instability**: a history of involvement in intense and volatile relationships; preoccupation with consistent fears of being abandoned or rejected; difficulty trusting treating professionals as part of more general difficulties in close relationships
- **Affective lability and impulsivity**: very labile mood, impulsivity, and heightened activity levels, accompanied by prominent hostility and irritability; combination of impulsivity, resentment, and high energy described as placing her at risk for lashing out at those close to her and for acting-out behaviors generally
- **Identity disturbance**: generally positive but fluctuating self-evaluation; positive self-esteem as a defense against underlying feelings of uncertainty and self-doubt; fragile self-esteem prone to dramatic drops in response to scrutiny or criticism; tendency to externalize blame to preserve self-esteem
- **Suicidal ideation**: recurrent thoughts related to a suicidal act; Suicide Potential Index T = 74; critical item 100 (SUI) endorsed as "Mainly True" ("I've made plans about how to kill myself"); Chronic Suicide Risk Index T = 63
- **Anger dyscontrol**: critical items endorse explosive temper (item 61: "Sometimes my temper explodes and I completely lose control" — Very True), threats toward others (item 181 — Slightly True), and others being afraid of her temper (item 21 — Mainly True); Reactive Aggression Scale T = 70
- **Trauma features**: critical items endorse troubled memories of a bad experience (ARD-T item 114 — Mainly True) and diminished interest in previously enjoyed activities following a traumatic event (item 274 — Slightly True); symptom behavior group profile fit with self-mutilation = 0.630, auditory hallucinations = 0.609, and current suicide = 0.645

The primary diagnoses suggested — Bipolar I disorder and Cyclothymic disorder — reflect that her profile's grandiosity and mood elevation features were sufficiently prominent to shift the primary diagnostic consideration toward a mood disorder framework, with BPD as an important rule-out rather than the leading formulation. This mirrors the known clinical challenge of distinguishing BPD affective lability from bipolar spectrum mood disorders, particularly when grandiosity is present.

Validity considerations complicated interpretation. The report noted mixed response styles: while NIM and PIM were below threshold, the Cashel Discriminant Function was elevated (T = 76), suggesting possible positive distortion in some areas, and subtle negative impression management patterns were also present. Denial of substance use was flagged, with ALC Estimated Score 21T higher than ALC and DRG Estimated Score 19T higher than DRG — a pattern suggesting the respondent described personality characteristics typically associated with alcohol or drug involvement while denying such problems.

### BPD as a Rule-Out — Dylan Kay ([[summaries/pai_199]])
In the PAI report for Dylan Kay (npsych230209), a 20-year-old female, BPD appeared in the **Rule Out** section of DSM-5 diagnostic possibilities (301.83 / F60.3). The primary diagnostic consideration was persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia). The coefficient of fit against the BPD clinical profile was **0.724** — the sixth highest-fitting diagnostic group overall, a statistically significant association.

The clinical picture for Dylan Kay showed meaningful overlap with BPD across several domains:
- **Interpersonal instability and abandonment fears**: described as possibly in a state of crisis associated with difficulties or rejection (perceived or actual) in interpersonal relationships; pattern of anxious ambivalence in close relationships marked by bitterness and resentment alternating with dependency and anxiety about possible rejection
- **Emotional lability**: the PAI narrative specifically noted emotional lability, fairly rapid and extreme mood swings, and episodes of poorly controlled anger
- **Identity disturbance**: uncertain about major life issues with little sense of direction or purpose; pattern consistent with [[concepts/identity-disturbance-clinical-features]]
- **Interpersonal withdrawal**: interpersonal style characterized as withdrawn and introverted, with passive style in relationships discouraging social contact — a feature more consistent with the depressive and avoidant dimensions of her profile than primary BPD
- **Suicidal ideation**: periodic, possibly transient thoughts of self-harm; Suicide Potential Index T = 62; follow-up on suicidal thoughts warranted
- **Trauma features**: ARD-T critical item 114 endorsed as Very True ("I've been troubled by memories of a bad experience for a long time")
- **Difficulty trusting**: flagged as a potential early treatment complication — initial difficulty placing trust in a treating professional as part of more general problems in close relationships

Negative self-evaluation was prominent: self-critical, difficulty handling setbacks, blaming herself for past failures, attributing successes to others — consistent with the depressive coloring of her primary diagnostic consideration. Unlike primary BPD presentations, Dylan Kay's profile was characterized by affective anxiety and depression rather than explosive anger or overt interpersonal volatility, and she denied significant problems with antisocial behavior, undue suspiciousness, or unusual mood elevation.

Validity indicators were within normal limits for Dylan Kay. NIM and PIM scores were below thresholds (84 and 57), no NIM/PIM-specific profiles were generated, all distortion indicators fell in the normal range, and the respondent was judged to have answered in a reasonably forthright manner without attempting to present an unrealistically positive or negative impression.

The symptom behavior group coefficients of fit showed statistically significant associations with antipsychotic medications (0.735), auditory hallucinations (0.731), and current suicide (0.708) — a pattern that warrants clinical attention despite the absence of reported psychotic symptoms or significant psychosis-range elevations in the clinical narrative.

### BPD as Primary Diagnostic Consideration — Raven Colbert ([[summaries/pai_202]])
In the PAI report for Raven Colbert (npsych230419), a 20-year-old female, BPD was the **highest-fitting diagnostic group** with a coefficient of fit of **0.894** — the strongest BPD profile fit observed across the entire case series reviewed to date. BPD appeared as a primary diagnostic consideration (301.83 / F60.3) in the DSM-5 diagnostic possibilities section.

The clinical picture for Raven Colbert showed pervasive and prominent BPD features across all core domains:
- **Interpersonal instability**: history of involvement in intense and volatile relationships; preoccupation with consistent fears of being abandoned or rejected; passive and submissive interpersonal stance in social situations with underlying resentment when pressed
- **Identity disturbance**: poorly established, fluctuating self-concept oscillating between harsh self-criticism and relative self-confidence; self-esteem explicitly dependent on close relationship status; sense of incompleteness, unfulfillment, and inadequacy apart from relational anchoring; little sense of direction or purpose
- **Impulsivity and self-harm**: quite impulsive and prone to self-harmful and self-destructive behaviors including spending, sex, and substance use; critical item endorsement of self-harm when upset (BOR-S item 183 — Mainly True); elevated risk for self-mutilation and suicidal behavior
- **Suicidal ideation**: Suicide Potential Index T = **90** — at the level typical of individuals placed on suicide precautions, requiring immediate evaluation; Chronic Suicide Risk Index T = 83; critical items include plans to kill self (item 100 — Very True) and currently considering suicide (item 340 — Slightly True)
- **Affective features**: significant depressive experience alongside borderline personality features; anhedonia, worthlessness, hopelessness; relatively few physiological signs of depression; drug use and depression described as bidirectionally related

The symptom behavior group coefficients of fit were exceptionally high, with self-mutilation at **0.919** (the highest across all behavior groups), followed by auditory hallucinations (0.886), antipsychotic medications (0.886), suicide history (0.868), current suicide (0.867), and persecutory delusions (0.855). This pattern represents a severe, high-risk clinical profile.

The overall clinical narrative described multiple co-occurring problems consistent with both BPD and comorbid conditions: substance dependence with above-average impairment even relative to specialized treatment populations, significant depression, trauma-related symptoms (PTSD features endorsed on three ARD-T critical items at MT level), anxiety with phobic avoidance (possibly agoraphobia), somatic preoccupation, thought disorganization with social isolation and detachment, and paranoid features. The configuration resembles a complex, multi-morbid presentation in which BPD features are primary and pervasive rather than secondary or emergent.

Validity was complicated by a **dual distortion pattern**: both positive distortion (denial of substance use — ALC Estimated Score 12T higher than ALC; DRG Estimated Score 22T lower than DRG) and negative distortion (endorsement of bizarre/unlikely symptoms; NIM predicted profile fit = 0.895; fake bad fit = 0.852). Clinical elevations were interpreted as likely overrepresenting the actual degree of psychopathology in some areas, but profile patterns of this type are typically associated with marked distress and severe functional impairment.

The Inattention Index was T = **99**, indicating significant attentional difficulties during testing, and Mean Clinical Elevation was T = 78, reflecting broad-based pathology across the profile.

### BPD as a Primary Rule-Out — Alex Shirley ([[summaries/pai_26]])
In the PAI report for Alex Shirley (npsych220217), a 27-year-old male, BPD appeared in the **Rule Out** section of DSM-5 diagnostic possibilities (301.83 / F60.3). The primary diagnostic consideration was Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia). The coefficient of fit against the BPD clinical profile was **0.838** — the sixth highest-fitting diagnostic group overall, a strongly statistically significant association.

The clinical picture for Alex Shirley showed broad and prominent overlap with BPD features across multiple core domains:
- **Emotional lability and anger**: described as emotionally labile with fairly rapid and extreme mood swings and, in particular, episodes of poorly controlled anger; easily irritated and quick-tempered; critical items endorse fear in others due to temper (item 21 — Slightly True), explosive loss of control (item 61 — Slightly True), and threats to hurt people (item 181 — Slightly True)
- **Identity disturbance**: uncertain about major life issues with little sense of direction or purpose; self-concept imperfectly established; self-esteem fragile and particularly vulnerable to perceived slights or oversights by other people; self-image depends unduly upon the current status of close relationships
- **Interpersonal instability and abandonment fears**: possibly presenting in a state of crisis associated with difficulties or rejection (perceived or actual) in interpersonal relationships; pattern of anxious ambivalence in close relationships marked by bitterness and resentment on one hand and dependency and anxiety about possible rejection on the other
- **Phobic and anxiety features**: significant phobic symptoms and behaviors, possibly agoraphobia or multiple phobias; vigilant environmental monitoring to avoid feared stimuli; discomforting anxiety and tension with marked concentration difficulties
- **Impulsivity and adventurousness**: adventurousness, risk-taking, tendency toward impulsivity; others may view him as unsympathetic and pragmatic
- **Substance use**: drug use described as a source of interpersonal, vocational, and/or legal problems; ALC Estimated Score 9T higher than ALC; DRG Estimated Score 5T higher than DRG
- **Withdrawal**: interpersonal style characterized as withdrawn and introverted, passive and distant; little enjoyment from social interaction

Alex Shirley's Suicide Potential Index was **T = 90** — notably high despite the primary diagnostic formulation being dysthymia and the narrative noting he is "not reporting distress from thoughts of self-harm." The Chronic Suicide Risk Index (T = 68) and Level of Care Index (T = 69) were also elevated, warranting careful monitoring. The symptom behavior group coefficients of fit showed significant associations with auditory hallucinations (0.898), antipsychotic medications (0.873), persecutory delusions (0.837), self-mutilation (0.821), and assault history (0.801) — a severity pattern disproportionate to the dysthymia primary formulation.

Trauma-related critical items were endorsed: reliving a horrible experience (ARD-T item 34 — Slightly True), troubled memories of a bad experience (item 114 — Mainly True), and loss of interest following a bad experience (item 274 — Mainly True) — indicating possible traumatic history consistent with [[concepts/trauma-informed-clinical-assessment]] considerations.

Validity indicators were within normal limits. NIM and PIM were below thresholds, all distortion indicators fell in the normal range, and the respondent was judged to have answered in a reasonably forthright manner. The RXR Estimated Score was notably low (T = 32), suggesting possible resistance to behavior change that may complicate treatment despite above-average surface motivation.

### BPD as a Rule-Out — Krishan Patel ([[summaries/pai_30]])
In the PAI report for Krishan Patel (npsych220428), a 21-year-old male college student, BPD appeared in the **Rule Out** section of DSM-5 diagnostic possibilities (301.83 / F60.3). The primary diagnostic consideration was Panic disorder (300.01 / F41.0), and Bipolar I disorder, current or most recent episode manic, unspecified (296.40 / F31.9) was also listed as a rule-out. The coefficient of fit against the BPD clinical profile was **0.380** — below the threshold of statistical significance (.42) but present in the differential.

The clinical picture for Krishan Patel showed partial overlap with BPD features, principally in the domains of impulsivity, interpersonal dynamics, and irritability:
- **Impulsivity**: a major self-reported problem; proneness to behaviors likely to be self-harmful or self-destructive, such as those involving spending, sex, and/or substance abuse — a core BPD-range feature
- **Interpersonal dynamics**: strong needs for affiliation and positive regard from others; uninhibited social behavior perceived as attention-seeking and dramatic; quantity of social contact prioritized over quality — features that share surface similarity with the attention-seeking and relational intensity of BPD
- **Irritability and mood lability**: agitated, irritable, and affectively labile; moody and demanding presentation causing strain in close relationships; quick-tempered and easily provoked
- **Paranoid ideation**: quick to feel treated inequitably; easily believes others are making a concerted effort to undermine his interests — a feature present in BPD but more prominent in paranoid presentations
- **Self-concept**: mixed self-evaluation oscillating between self-doubt and self-confidence; fluctuations not extreme; comparable to those experienced by most adults — notably less severe than primary BPD identity disturbance

However, the clinical picture for Krishan Patel was dominated by features more consistent with a **manic episode** than with BPD: heightened energy levels at severity uncommon even in clinical samples, accelerated thought processes, overcommitted and disorganized activity levels, and an irritability-driven rather than abandonment-driven interpersonal style. The profile fit against Bipolar I disorder (mania) was 0.562 — the highest-fitting diagnostic group — and fit against college students as a context-specific norm group was also high (0.573). Grandiosity was notably absent, which distinguishes this manic-range picture from a more classic bipolar presentation and allows BPD to remain in the differential.

The Inattention Index (T = 99) and Reactive Aggression Scale (T = 80) were notably elevated. The Violence Potential Index (T = 70) and Suicide Potential Index (T = 68) were in elevated ranges. Critical item endorsement included explosive loss of control (item 61 — Very True) and troubled memories of a bad experience (ARD-T item 114 — Slightly True).

A key validity concern was possible denial of alcohol/drug use, with ALC Estimated Score 22T higher than ALC and DRG Estimated Score 17T higher than DRG — a pattern flagged for caution in interpreting substance-related self-report.

### BPD as a Primary Diagnostic Consideration — Max Gosey ([[summaries/pai_34]])
In the PAI report for Max Gosey (npsych220219), a 34-year-old married male, BPD was listed as a **primary diagnostic consideration** (301.83 / F60.3) alongside Persistent Depressive Disorder (Dysthymia). The coefficient of fit against the BPD clinical profile was **0.875** — the fourth highest-fitting diagnostic group overall — a strongly statistically significant association. Notably, anxiety disorders (0.889), persistent depressive disorder (0.876), and PTSD (0.876) each fit slightly higher than BPD, but BPD's fit was sufficient for primary diagnostic consideration alongside dysthymia.

The clinical picture for Max Gosey showed prominent BPD features across multiple core domains:
- **Interpersonal instability and abandonment fears**: the PAI narrative described a profile of a person "tense, angry, unhappy, and emotionally labile," possibly presenting in a state of crisis associated with difficulties or rejection (perceived or actual) in interpersonal relationships; pattern of anxious ambivalence in close relationships marked by bitterness and resentment on one hand and dependency and anxiety about possible rejection on the other
- **Affective lability**: quite emotionally labile with fairly rapid and extreme mood swings and, in particular, episodes of poorly controlled anger; some capacity to restrain extreme verbal or physical demonstrations of anger
- **Impulsivity**: prone to behaviors likely to be self-harmful or self-destructive, such as those involving spending, sex, and/or substance abuse; increased risk for self-mutilation or suicidal behavior during times of affective turmoil
- **Suicidal ideation**: recurrent thoughts related to a suicidal act; Suicide Potential Index T = **84**; Chronic Suicide Risk Index T = 65; critical item 340 endorsed as Slightly True ("I'm considering suicide"); risk heightened by situational stresses, poor impulse control, and social isolation
- **Identity disturbance**: generally negative self-evaluation fluctuating between harsh self-criticism/self-doubt and periods of relative self-confidence; self-criticism more prominent during stressful periods; blames himself for setbacks and sees future success as dependent upon actions of others
- **Interpersonal social discomfort**: interpersonal style characterized as very uncomfortable in social situations; little interest in or need for interacting with others; passive, submissive stance; tendency toward social isolation to avoid forced relational commitments
- **Trauma features**: ARD-T critical items 34 and 114 endorsed as Slightly True (reliving something horrible; troubled by memories of a bad experience); item 274 (loss of interest following a bad experience) also endorsed — suggesting possible traumatic history
- **Depressive features**: pessimism, worthlessness, hopelessness, personal failure (mild/transient); primary diagnostic consideration of persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia)
- **Thought disorganization**: describes thought processes as marked by confusion, distractibility, and difficulty concentrating; possible tangential or circumstantial speech

Symptom behavior group coefficients of fit included current suicide (0.847), auditory hallucinations (0.848), antipsychotic medications (0.832), suicide history (0.823), self-mutilation (0.811), and persecutory delusions (0.809) — a high-severity pattern consistent with the elevated Suicide Potential Index and multi-domain clinical elevation. Mean Clinical Elevation was T = 59, suggesting moderate broad-based pathology.

A key validity concern was **possible denial of alcohol/drug use**, with ALC Estimated Score 18T higher than ALC and DRG Estimated Score 16T higher than DRG — a pattern appearing consistently across BPD-range cases in this series. The Reactive Aggression Scale (T = 81) and Inattention Index (T = 77) were also notably elevated. Treatment Process Index was T = 76, predicting a challenging treatment course with probability of reversals. A rule-out differential included Cyclothymic disorder and Major Depressive Disorder, single episode, unspecified.

Max Gosey's profile resembles that of Alex Shirley in its combination of BPD features within a depression-dominant primary formulation and social withdrawal, but differs in its markedly stronger BPD primary diagnostic consideration (explicit DSM-5 primary listing vs. rule-out), higher profile fit (.875 vs. .838), and the absence of prominent phobic features. ## Differential Diagnosis and Comorbidity

BPD frequently co-occurs with or must be differentiated from:
- Posttraumatic stress disorder (PTSD) — shared features of hyperreactivity, dissociation, and relational instability; see [[concepts/trauma-informed-clinical-assessment]]
- Bipolar disorder — affective lability and impulsivity overlap; manic episodes must be distinguished from BPD mood shifts; this boundary is particularly salient in the Summer Young case, where Bipolar I and Cyclothymic disorder were the primary diagnostic considerations and BPD was a rule-out; and in Krishan Patel's case, where manic presentation was primary and BPD a secondary rule-out; Cyclothymic disorder was also listed as a rule-out for Corey Ho
- Major depressive disorder — chronic dysphoria and suicidality common to both; see [[concepts/major-depressive-disorder-clinical-features]]; Corey Ho exemplifies how a depression-dominant profile with identity instability and relational self-esteem fragility can generate a statistically significant BPD coefficient of fit while remaining primarily organized around MDD
- Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia) — as in Dylan Kay and Alex Shirley, pervasive low mood and negative self-evaluation can dominate the clinical picture while BPD features remain secondary rule-outs; Tiffany Cha carries dysthymia as a primary consideration alongside BPD as the highest-fitting diagnostic group; Max Gosey carries dysthymia as a co-primary diagnostic consideration alongside BPD; Woody Chiang carries dysthymia as a rule-out alongside BPD and relational problems; Benjamin Chiu's highest-fitting diagnostic group was major depressive disorder (0.910), with BPD third (0.878); see [[concepts/persistent-depressive-disorder]]
- Intermittent explosive disorder — as in Tiffany Cha, explosive anger and impulsivity may yield this as a primary diagnostic consideration even when BPD profile fit is the highest in the profile
- Schizotypal personality disorder — identity disturbance and transient psychotic-like symptoms can overlap; subclinical psychosis-like features were present in Tiffany Cha's profile; social isolation, detachment, and difficulty interpreting interpersonal nuances are also prominent in Benjamin Chiu's profile
- Antisocial personality disorder — impulsivity and risk-taking are shared features; Tiffany Cha endorsed a history of antisocial behavior and possible conduct disorder during adolescence; notably, Woody Chiang's highest profile fit was with antisocial personality disorder (0.759), reflecting aggression and unreliability item endorsements that partially overlap with BPD impulsivity
- Paranoid personality disorder — as in Woody Chiang, interpersonal wariness, hostility, and suspiciousness can co-occur with BPD-range relational instability, warranting both as rule-outs within the same diagnostic formulation; in Benjamin Chiu, paranoid personality disorder is a primary diagnostic consideration rather than a rule-out, with BPD a significant secondary rule-out
- Somatic symptom disorder — in cases like Itamar Cohen, somatic preoccupation may dominate the clinical picture while borderline relational features remain secondary; in Raven Colbert, somatic symptoms co-occur with BPD as a primary finding; in Benjamin Chiu, somatic preoccupation is a prominent secondary feature within a multi-morbid formulation
- Avoidant personality features — as in AJ Zhang, passivity and social withdrawal can co-occur with borderline self-harm and identity features, warranting a mixed personality formulation; Max Gosey also shows prominent social avoidance and passive interpersonal withdrawal
- Adjustment disorder — as in Aria Dewey, acute crisis presentation with borderline features may meet criteria for adjustment disorder as a primary diagnosis while BPD warrants rule-out
- Cyclothymic disorder and Bipolar II — mood variability features may overlap with BPD affective lability, as seen in Aria Dewey's differential, most prominently in Summer Young's differential where Cyclothymic disorder was a co-primary diagnostic consideration, and also as a rule-out in Corey Ho's differential alongside the primary MDD formulation
- Narcissistic personality disorder — grandiosity and attention-seeking overlap with some BPD features; both appeared as rule-outs in Aria Dewey's differential; grandiosity was a prominent feature in Summer Young's profile
- Alcohol use disorder and substance use disorder — as in Annette Malan, substance-related pathology and trauma can be the primary clinical picture while impulsivity and identity uncertainty present as secondary BPD-range features; in Raven Colbert, substance dependence co-exists with BPD as a primary diagnostic formulation; in Alex Shirley, drug-related problems appear alongside dysthymia as the primary diagnosis with BPD as a rule-out; alcohol use disorder (mild) is also a rule-out for Woody Chiang
- Anxiety disorders and specific phobia — as in Alex Shirley, phobic symptoms and pervasive anxiety can be the dominant presenting complaint within a BPD rule-out context; in Tiffany Cha, anxiety disorders had the third-highest profile fit (0.863) alongside BPD's highest fit (0.880); in Max Gosey, anxiety disorders had the highest profile fit (0.889) while BPD was still listed as a primary diagnostic consideration; in Benjamin Chiu, anxiety disorders had the fourth-highest fit (0.878, tied with BPD) within a profile dominated by depression and PTSD; anxiety disorders were the fourth-highest fit in Corey Ho's profile (0.725)
- Panic disorder — as in Krishan Patel, panic-range anxiety and somatic symptoms can co-present with manic irritability and impulsivity in a young adult where BPD is a distant rule-out; Panic disorder was also listed as a rule-out for Corey Ho
- Obsessive-compulsive disorder — as in Tiffany Cha, perfectionism, rumination, and rigid behavior may appear in the Rule Out section alongside BPD
- Relational problems — as in Woody Chiang, crisis-level interpersonal difficulties may be designated as the primary clinical consideration when the overall profile does not fully consolidate around a single personality disorder formulation, with BPD and paranoid personality disorder as active rule-outs
- Schizoaffective disorder — appears as a rule-out in cases with prominent BPD features combined with thought disorganization and psychosis-proximate symptom profiles; was the second-highest fit in Tiffany Cha's profile (0.863) and the third-highest in Corey Ho's profile (0.729)

In the Nat Lim case, the full DSM-5 differential also included schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, paranoid personality disorder, and persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia). In Raven Colbert's case, schizoaffective disorder and schizophrenia were also listed as rule-outs, reflecting the degree of thought disorganization and social isolation in her profile. Schizoaffective disorder also had the second-highest profile fit (0.863) in Tiffany Cha's report, alongside prominent low-level psychosis-like critical item endorsements. In Benjamin Chiu's case, schizophrenia had a notably high profile fit (0.840), reflecting paranoid features, social isolation, and somatic preoccupation.

## Self-Concept and Identity in BPD

A hallmark feature is the poorly established, fragile self-concept. Self-esteem is highly reactive to perceived interpersonal slights or oversights. Identity shifts correspond to emotional state, making stable long-term goals and commitments difficult. This instability is central to understanding the broader clinical picture and treatment challenges.

Across cases, self-concept disturbance manifests with different coloring:
- **Primary BPD** (Nat Lim): chaotic oscillation between severe self-doubt and exaggerated self-confidence
- **Primary BPD, high severity** (Raven Colbert): poorly established self-concept fluctuating between harsh self-criticism and relative self-confidence; self-esteem explicitly dependent on close relationships; feels incomplete and inadequate apart from relational anchoring; identity shifts correspond to relationship status
- **Primary BPD, multi-morbid** (Tiffany Cha): poorly established, fluctuating self-concept; attitude about herself likely to fluctuate between harsh self-criticism and relative self-confidence; self-perception tied to status of close relationships; feels incomplete, unfulfilled, and inadequate apart from relational anchoring; self-esteem fragile and responsive to slights
- **Primary BPD co-occurring with dysthymia** (Max Gosey): generally negative self-evaluation fluctuating between harsh self-criticism/self-doubt and periods of relative self-confidence; self-criticism more prominent during stressful periods; blames himself for setbacks; sees future success as dependent upon actions of others; lacks direction and certainty about goals
- **Mixed borderline/avoidant** (AJ Zhang): uniformly negative and self-critical coloring without extreme reactive lability
- **BPD rule-out with adjustment disorder** (Aria Dewey): fluctuation between harsh self-criticism and relative self-confidence, with self-esteem explicitly tied to relational status; feels incomplete and inadequate apart from close relationships
- **Secondary borderline features** (Itamar Cohen): more modest or uniformly negative coloring within a paranoid/somatic/OC profile
- **BPD rule-out with PTSD and alcohol use disorder** (Annette Malan): uncertainty about major life issues and lack of direction, mixed self-evaluation with both positive and negative aspects — but without the extreme volatility of primary BPD
- **BPD rule-out with Bipolar I and Cyclothymic disorder** (Summer Young): generally positive but fragile and fluctuating self-evaluation functioning as a defense against underlying self-doubt; prone to dramatic drops under criticism; external attribution of failure to preserve self-esteem; positive self-regard co-existing with grandiosity features
- **BPD rule-out with persistent depressive disorder** (Dylan Kay): negative self-evaluation with self-criticism, difficulty handling setbacks, and attribution of successes to others; inwardly troubled by self-doubt and misgivings about adequacy; lacks the extreme reactive lability of primary BPD but shares the core negativity and abandonment preoccupation
- **BPD rule-out with dysthymia** (Alex Shirley): imperfectly established self-concept with fragile self-esteem particularly vulnerable to relational slights; self-image unduly dependent on status of close relationships; outward appearance of adequate self-esteem masking internal uncertainty; withdrawn and introverted interpersonal style alongside the relational ambivalence characteristic of BPD
- **BPD as non-significant rule-out with manic presentation** (Krishan Patel): mixed self-evaluation with both positive and negative aspects; fluctuations not extreme and comparable to most adults; during stressful times prone to self-criticism, uncertainty, and indecisiveness — a relatively mild identity picture consistent with the manic-dominant rather than borderline-dominant formulation
- **BPD rule-out with relational problems and paranoid personality features** (Woody Chiang): self-evaluation with both positive and negative aspects; fluctuates between pessimism/self-doubt and relative self-confidence; fluctuations not extreme and comparable to most adults; self-criticism more pronounced during stressful periods — a self-concept picture of moderate instability, less severe than primary BPD but situated within a broader clinical profile marked by interpersonal wariness and crisis-level relational difficulties
- **BPD rule-out with paranoid personality, PTSD, and MDD** (Benjamin Chiu): generally harsh, negative self-evaluation; self-critical and pessimistic; dwelling on past failures and lost opportunities; considerable uncertainty and indecision about plans and goals for the future; tends to blame himself for setbacks; sees prospects for future success as dependent upon actions of others — a uniformly negative self-concept pattern without extreme reactive lability, situated within a paranoid/trauma/depressive clinical configuration
- **BPD rule-out with MDD primary** (Corey Ho): poorly established self-concept with harsh self-criticism and severe self-doubt; self-perception varying as a function of the status of close relationships; feels incomplete, unfulfilled, and inadequate apart from relational anchoring; self-esteem quite fragile and likely to plummet in response to slights or oversights; associated shifts in identity and attitudes about major life issues — a picture closely resembling relational-dependent BPD identity instability within a predominantly depressive clinical presentation

See [[concepts/identity-disturbance-clinical-features]] for a broader discussion of identity instability across diagnostic categories.

- Tiffany Cha: acceptable validity — no evidence of general positive or negative impression management, consistent responding; Inattention Index T = 88; primary concern is denial of substance use (ALC Estimated Score 20T higher than ALC; DRG Estimated Score 12T higher than DRG)
- Max Gosey: acceptable validity — no evidence of general positive or negative impression management; consistent responding confirmed; primary concern is denial of alcohol/drug use (ALC Estimated Score 18T higher than ALC; DRG Estimated Score 16T higher than DRG); RXR Estimated Score 14T higher than actual RXR, suggesting possible underreporting of treatment reluctance
- Woody Chiang: subtle negative impression management indicators present — patterns or combinations of features unusual in clinical populations but relatively common among individuals feigning mental disorder; NIM and PIM below thresholds; no NIM/PIM-specific profiles generated; Inattention Index T = 88; NIM predicted profile fit was the highest response set coefficient (0.769); clinical elevations may overrepresent the extent and degree of significant pathology in certain areas
- **Benjamin Chiu**: mixed distortion pattern — negative impression management present across multiple indices (Malingering Index T = 84; Rogers Discriminant Function T = 73; Negative Distortion Scale T = 78; Hong Malingering Index T = 83; Multiscale Feigning Index T = 77); concurrent denial of substance use despite personality characteristics associated with alcohol/drug involvement; profile fit with motor vehicle accident claimants context norm (0.726) adds a potential external motivation consideration; clinical elevations interpreted as likely overrepresenting symptoms in particular areas, though severe functional impairment is probable given profile configuration
- **Corey Ho**: clean validity profile — zero missing items, consistent responding, all distortion indicators within normal limits, NIM and PIM below thresholds; no NIM/PIM-specific profiles generated; respondent judged to have answered in a reasonably forthright manner; RXR Estimated Score (T = 32, 7T higher than actual RXR) is a treatment consideration, suggesting possible resistance to behavior change; no substance use denial pattern (ALC Estimated Score only 7T higher than ALC; DRG Estimated Score 1T lower than DRG)

The substance use denial pattern appeared in Summer Young, Aria Dewey, Annette Malan, Raven Colbert, Krishan Patel, Tiffany Cha, Max Gosey, and Benjamin Chiu — cutting across cases with significant BPD-range impulsivity features — and was notably absent in Dylan Kay, Alex Shirley, and Corey Ho, whose profiles were more tractable in terms of impression management. Raven Colbert's case is distinctive in showing concurrent negative distortion alongside substance use denial. Woody Chiang presents a distinctive pattern in which negative impression management (rather than substance use denial) is the primary validity concern. Benjamin Chiu's profile is notable in combining clinically elevated negative impression management indices with concurrent substance use denial and a context-specific norm fit with motor vehicle accident claimants, creating a complex validity picture in a case with the highest Suicide Potential Index in the series. Corey Ho's profile stands out as unusually clean among this series, with no impression management concerns and no substance use denial.

These validity patterns underscore the importance of examining response consistency indices and impression management scales as part of any personality assessment involving complex, multi-scale instruments. See [[concepts/validity-and-response-styles]] and [[concepts/random-responding]] for broader discussion.

## Treatment Considerations

BPD presents significant treatment challenges:
- The therapeutic relationship carries heightened emotional significance
- Patients may have profound difficulty trusting treating clinicians
- Hostility or derogatory reactions toward therapists as authority figures are common
- Early sessions may be destabilized by relational sensitivity and impasses
- Disorganization and overwhelm may interfere with structured treatment modalities
- Motivation for change may coexist with strong ambivalence and resistance

When borderline features appear as secondary dimensions (as in Itamar Cohen), treatment challenges may include emotional constriction rather than emotional overwhelm — the patient may have difficulty expressing emotional material at all, with the therapeutic relationship carrying special weight given limited social support.

When mixed borderline/avoidant features are present (as in AJ Zhang), the passive and withdrawn interpersonal style can make initial engagement difficult. The therapeutic alliance must be carefully managed, as limited social support amplifies the significance of the therapeutic relationship.

When BPD is a rule-out within an acute crisis presentation (as in Aria Dewey), the Treatment Process Index may be markedly elevated (T = 81), signaling a difficult treatment course with probable reversals. Current deficits in social support give special significance to the therapeutic relationship.

When BPD is a rule-out within a trauma- and substance-dominated profile (as in Annette Malan), the patient may currently be too disorganized or overwhelmed to participate meaningfully in some treatment modalities. The combination of PTSD, alcohol use disorder, and impulsive personality features suggests that integrated treatment addressing trauma and substance use simultaneously may be necessary.

When BPD is a rule-out within a bipolar spectrum presentation (as in Summer Young), treatment is complicated by mixed response style validity concerns, defensive disclosure style, and difficulty trusting treating professionals. Treatment motivation is above average but the nature of problems suggests a challenging course with probability of reversals. The impulsivity, anger dyscontrol, suicidal planning, and grandiosity create a multi-domain risk profile that requires careful safety planning alongside engagement efforts.

When BPD is a rule-out within a depression-dominant presentation (as in Dylan Kay), treatment motivation is above average and the clinical picture is more tractable than in primary BPD presentations. The primary treatment complication is initial difficulty trusting a treating professional as part of broader interpersonal problems. Her withdrawn and introverted interpersonal style may make early engagement challenging, and the treatment relationship assumes particular importance given the passive and avoidant quality of her social functioning.

When BPD is a rule-out within a dysthymia presentation alongside impulsivity and phobic features (as in Alex Shirley), treatment motivation appears above average but the low RXR Estimated Score (T = 32) signals possible resistance to actual behavior change. The therapeutic relationship carries heightened significance given poor social support. The patient may be too disorganized or overwhelmed to engage meaningfully in some treatment modalities early on. Anger management and the monitoring of suicidal risk (despite denial of current distress from self-harm thoughts) are important early treatment considerations.

When BPD is a non-significant rule-out within a manic presentation (as in Krishan Patel), treatment motivation appears typical of clinical settings and is above average relative to non-treatment-seeking adults. However, the nature of manic features — disorganization, overcommitment, overwhelm — suggests the patient may currently be too disorganized to participate meaningfully in some forms of treatment. Anger management (Reactive Aggression Scale T = 80) and monitoring of substance use denial are important early-stage considerations. The reasonably intact social support and average stress level are favorable prognostic signs.

When BPD is the highest-fitting diagnostic group alongside intermittent explosive disorder as a primary consideration (as in Tiffany Cha), the Treatment Process Index is markedly elevated (T = 81), predicting a difficult treatment course with reversals. Key treatment complications include: current deficits in social support giving special significance to the therapeutic relationship; initial difficulty placing trust in a treating professional as part of more general problems in close relationships; emotional constriction making early expression of emotional material difficult; and the need for specific follow-up on suicidal ideation and risk factors including poor impulse control and situational stress. Anger management is a prominent area of concern given potential for property damage and assault threats.

When BPD is a co-primary diagnosis alongside persistent depressive disorder (as in Max Gosey), the Treatment Process Index is elevated (T = 76), predicting a challenging treatment course with probability of reversals. Key early-stage complications include: current disorganization and overwhelm potentially interfering with meaningful participation in some treatment modalities; difficulty trusting treating professionals as part of broader interpersonal discomfort; and the need for specific follow-up on suicidal ideation given poor impulse control, situational stresses, and social isolation. The RXR Estimated Score (14T higher than actual RXR) suggests possible underreporting of treatment reluctance. Anger management (Reactive Aggression Scale T = 81) warrants early attention. A favorable prognostic sign is his report of several supportive relationships, suggesting an intact social support buffer.

When BPD is a primary diagnosis alongside substance dependence and severe suicidal ideation (as in Raven Colbert), the treatment picture is the most complex in the series. Immediate safety evaluation is required. The Treatment Process Index (T = 86) is among the highest recorded, indicating a predicted difficult treatment course with reversals. Key early-stage complications include: disorganization and overwhelm interfering with structured treatment participation; difficulty trusting treating professionals; and special significance of the therapeutic relationship given profound social isolation and limited support. The dual distortion validity pattern also suggests that initial treatment engagement may be complicated by her mixed presentation style.

When BPD is a rule-out within a profile primarily characterized by relational problems and paranoid personality features (as in Woody Chiang), the Treatment Process Index is elevated (T = 76), signaling a challenging treatment course with probability of reversals. Treatment motivation is below average relative to clinical populations, with possible resistance to the idea that personal change is needed. Key treatment complications include: defensiveness and risk of early termination; limited social support giving special significance to the therapeutic relationship; and current disorganization or overwhelm potentially interfering with meaningful engagement in some treatment modalities. The anger management domain warrants attention given her quick temper and easy irritability, and the discrepancy between the elevated Suicide Potential Index and denial of self-harm distress warrants careful follow-up.

When BPD is a rule-out within a paranoid personality, PTSD, and MDD formulation (as in Benjamin Chiu), the Treatment Process Index is elevated (T = 81), predicting a difficult treatment course with probability of reversals. Key early-stage complications include: current social support deficits giving special significance to the therapeutic relationship; profound difficulty placing trust in a treating professional as part of broader paranoid interpersonal style; current disorganization and overwhelm potentially interfering with meaningful participation in some treatment modalities; and resistance to considering psychological origins of problems. Immediate safety evaluation is required given the Suicide Potential Index of T = 93 — the highest in the entire series.

When BPD is a rule-out within a predominantly depressive presentation without anger dyscontrol (as in Corey Ho), treatment motivation is above average and the respondent acknowledges important problems with a positive attitude toward personal change and therapy. The Treatment Process Index is mildly elevated (T = 60). Key early-stage considerations include: current disorganization and overwhelm potentially interfering with meaningful participation in some treatment modalities; monitoring of suicidal ideation given Suicide Potential Index T = 68 and critical item endorsement of having made plans to kill himself; and attention to his fragile, relationally-dependent self-esteem as a treatment relationship variable. Favorable prognostic signs include reasonably intact social support, average stress level, and a forthright, undistorted self-report. The low RXR Estimated Score (T = 32) warrants monitoring for possible resistance to behavior change despite surface motivation.

Evidence-based treatments for primary BPD include Dialectical Behavior Therapy (DBT), Mentalization-Based Treatment (MBT), and Transference-Focused Psychotherapy (TFP), though these are not discussed in the source documents.

See [[concepts/treatment-motivation-and-compliance]] for broader discussion of treatment engagement across PAI cases.

## PAI Scale Sensitivity

The PAI's Borderline Features (BOR) scale and its subscales (affective instability, identity problems, negative relationships, self-harm) are the primary instruments for detecting BPD-related pathology within the [[concepts/personality-assessment-inventory]] framework. The [[concepts/clinical-report-structure]] of the PAI integrates these subscale findings into narrative clinical hypotheses.

Coefficients of fit with the BPD clinical profile across the full case series:
- **.894** — Raven Colbert (primary BPD, highest severity; co-occurring substance dependence, PTSD, major depression, anxiety)
- **.880** — Tiffany Cha (highest-fitting diagnostic group; primary diagnoses intermittent explosive disorder and persistent depressive disorder; co-occurring PTSD features, anxiety, subclinical psychosis-like symptoms, antisocial history, drug use)
- **.878** — Benjamin Chiu (third-highest fitting; primary diagnoses MDD, PTSD, paranoid personality disorder; co-occurring somatic preoccupation, severe anxiety, social isolation; highest Suicide Potential Index in series at T = 93; mixed negative impression management; fits context norm for motor vehicle accident claimants)
- **.875** — Max Gosey (co-primary BPD and persistent depressive disorder; co-occurring anxiety, PTSD features, social isolation, thought disorganization)
- **.838** — Alex Shirley (BPD rule-out, dysthymia primary; anxiety disorders, PTSD features, substance use)
- **.769** — AJ Zhang (mixed borderline/avoidant rule-out, depression-dominant profile)
- **.746** — Nat Lim (primary BPD presentation)
- **.729** — Summer Young (BPD rule-out, Bipolar I and Cyclothymic disorder primary)
- **.724** — Dylan Kay (BPD rule-out, persistent depressive disorder primary)
- **.711** — Woody Chiang (BPD rule-out, relational problems primary; paranoid personality disorder and alcohol use disorder also as rule-outs; highest fit was antisocial personality disorder at 0.759)
- **.664** — Corey Ho (BPD rule-out, MDD primary; co-occurring anxiety, identity instability, fragile relational self-esteem, suicidal ideation without anger dyscontrol; clean validity profile)
- **.469** — Itamar Cohen (secondary borderline features within a paranoid/somatic/OC profile)
- **.462** — Aria Dewey (BPD rule-out within adjustment disorder primary presentation)
- **.442** — Annette Malan (BPD rule-out within PTSD and alcohol use disorder primary presentation)
- **.380** — Krishan Patel (BPD rule-out, manic presentation primary; below statistical significance threshold)

Corey Ho's coefficient (.664) falls in the middle of the series, reflecting a presentation in which identity instability and relational self-esteem fragility generate a meaningful BPD profile fit even in the absence of anger dyscontrol, overt impulsivity, or relational volatility. His case illustrates how the identity disturbance and abandonment sensitivity dimensions of BPD can be activated within a predominantly depressive clinical picture while the affective lability and anger dyscontrol dimensions remain absent or subclinical.

Krishan Patel's coefficient (.380) is the lowest in the series and falls below the .42 statistical significance threshold, consistent with BPD appearing only as a distant rule-out in a profile dominated by manic features. Raven Colbert's coefficient (.894) is the highest in the series and was simultaneously the highest-fitting diagnostic group within her own profile, yielding BPD as a primary diagnostic consideration rather than a rule-out. Max Gosey's coefficient (.875) is the fourth highest in the series and also yielded a primary diagnostic consideration — illustrating that BPD can reach primary diagnostic status even when anxiety disorders and PTSD have slightly higher profile fits, if the overall clinical configuration is sufficiently consistent with borderline features. Tiffany Cha's coefficient (.880) is the second highest in the series; like Raven Colbert, it was the highest-fitting group in her profile, yet primary diagnostic formulations were intermittent explosive disorder and persistent depressive disorder — illustrating how the anger-dyscontrol and depressive features can shape primary diagnosis even when BPD profile fit is highest. Alex Shirley's coefficient (.838) is notably high, yet BPD remained a rule-out rather than a primary formulation — illustrating how the primacy of dysthymia features and the absence of overt extreme interpersonal volatility can constrain the diagnostic formulation even when profile fit with BPD is very high. Summer Young's coefficient of fit (.729) was the highest-fitting diagnostic group within her own profile yet still yielded a rule-out formulation — illustrating how mood disorder features (Bipolar I, Cyclothymic disorder) can supersede BPD in the primary diagnostic formulation even when BPD profile fit is high. Woody Chiang's coefficient (.711) was the second highest-fitting diagnostic group in her profile (behind antisocial personality disorder at .759), illustrating how the antisocial dimension — when co-elevated alongside borderline features — can shift the diagnostic center of gravity away from BPD even at relatively high profile fit values.
