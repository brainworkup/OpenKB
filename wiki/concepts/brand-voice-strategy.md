# Brand Voice Strategy

A **brand voice strategy** defines the stable personality, tonal range, terminology rules, and stylistic conventions that govern how an organization communicates — regardless of channel, audience, or content type. It separates what never changes (voice attributes) from what flexes by context (tone), and provides concrete rules so that any writer can produce on-brand content.

See [[summaries/brainworkup-brand-voice-guide]] for the primary source document and [[summaries/brainworkup-branding-concepts]] for the visual identity directions that work in parallel with voice.

---

## Voice vs. Tone

The most important structural distinction in brand voice strategy:

- **Voice** — the consistent personality of the brand. It does not change. For brainworkup, this means always being authoritative, empathic, clear, and evidence-based.
- **Tone** — the emotional inflection applied in a specific context. It shifts along multiple dials (warmth, formality, precision, encouragement) depending on audience and channel.

This separation allows a brand to feel consistent across wildly different contexts — a parent-facing evaluation summary and a forensic expert witness report — without sounding identical. The branding concepts document makes this explicit: the same clinician and logomark can read as warm and reassuring (pediatric path) or precise and institutional (forensic path) depending on how tone is calibrated.

## Core Components of a Brand Voice Strategy

### 1. Brand Personality Statement
A concise characterization of the brand as a person or archetype. For brainworkup: a *trusted authority figure* who shifts to *meticulous expert witness* in forensic contexts. Personality statements anchor all downstream decisions — including naming, visual identity, and whether the brand reads as a solo practitioner or an "Institute."

### 2. Voice Attributes
A small set of named attributes (typically 3–5), each defined by:
- What it means in practice ("We are...")
- What it explicitly excludes ("We are not...")
- A positive example ("This sounds like...")
- A negative example ("This does NOT sound like...")

brainworkup's four attributes: **Authoritative**, **Empathic**, **Clear**, **Evidence-Based**.

### 3. Audience Segmentation
Different audiences require different tonal calibration. A well-formed voice strategy identifies each audience's:
- Knowledge level and clinical familiarity
- Emotional state when encountering the content
- Information needs (actionable next steps vs. evidentiary precision)
- Trust signals they respond to

brainworkup segments audiences as: parents/families, referring providers, attorneys/courts, and academic colleagues. See [[concepts/clinical-communication-register]] for how register shifts across these groups.

The branding concepts document reinforces this: worried parents and attorneys are both "looking for someone who can make sense of complexity," but the framing that earns trust differs markedly. Parents respond to warmth and plain language; attorneys and courts respond to credentials, methodology, and institutional gravitas.

### 4. Tone Spectrum
A tone spectrum maps each audience or channel to specific dial settings. For brainworkup:

| Context | Key Dials |
|---|---|
| Pediatric / Family | Warmth ↑, formality ↓ |
| Forensic / Legal | Precision ↑, warmth ↓, formality ↑ |
| Professional / Referral | Efficiency ↑, clinical precision ↑ |
| Public / Marketing | Approachability ↑, authority ↑ |
| Academic / Research | Rigor ↑, precision ↑, warmth low |

The seven branding concepts map directly onto this spectrum. Concepts 1 and 2 ("Growing Minds," "The Lighthouse") occupy the warm/family end. Concepts 4 and 6 ("The Institute," "Academic Authority") occupy the authoritative/forensic end. Concept 7 ("Dual Identity") operationalizes the full spectrum simultaneously through sub-brands.

### 5. Core Messaging Pillars
The 3–5 ideas the brand returns to repeatedly, in priority order. These ensure that across all content, the same themes are reinforced. For brainworkup:
1. Clinical expertise grounded in science
2. Evaluations that lead to action
3. Respect for the developing mind
4. Dual clinical and forensic expertise

### 6. Terminology Guide
A controlled vocabulary specifying preferred terms, avoided terms, and audience-specific translations. This is especially critical in clinical and legal contexts where word choice carries evidentiary or emotional weight.

Example: "differences" (parent-facing) vs. "deficits" (acceptable in clinical/forensic reports); "neuropsychological evaluation" vs. "brain test."

Naming decisions are also part of the terminology guide. The choice between "Brainworkup Neuropsychology," "Brainworkup Institute," or "Brainworkup Institute of Neuropsychology" is itself a voice decision — each signals a different level of institutional authority and warmth. "Institute" signals research mission and depth; "Neuropsychology" is more accessible to lay audiences.

### 7. Style Rules
Mechanical conventions (punctuation, capitalization, number formatting, link text) that ensure consistency at the sentence level. Includes brand wordmark rules — e.g., brainworkup is always lowercase, one word, even at the start of a sentence.

### 8. Channel-Specific Guidance
A channel matrix maps tone and format expectations to specific surfaces: website, blog, LinkedIn, Instagram, referral letters, clinical reports, conference presentations. This operationalizes the tone spectrum into day-to-day writing decisions.

Website design choices reinforce voice: the branding concepts document notes that a clinical practice should use light/airy backgrounds, humanist or serif typography, and clear calls to action — rather than the dark themes, code blocks, and monospace fonts associated with a data science aesthetic. Visual and verbal voice must be consistent.

## The Naming Decision as a Voice Strategy Choice

One of the more concrete applications of brand voice strategy is the practice name itself. The branding concepts document frames the "Institute" question as a voice and positioning decision:

**Use "Institute" framing when:**
- The brand needs to signal more than a solo private practice
- USC faculty appointment and research publications are brand assets
- Forensic/legal work is a significant audience
- Future growth (associates, postdocs) is planned

**Keep "Neuropsychology" framing when:**
- Simplicity and personal connection are priorities
- The primary audience is parents who want to feel they're working with *a person*, not an institution
- Overhead of an institutional identity is unwanted

**A middle path:** Use the more formal name as the legal/brand entity while introducing as "Dr. [Name] at Brainworkup" in parent-facing contexts — maintaining warmth at the interpersonal level without sacrificing institutional credibility.

This is brand voice strategy in practice: the same underlying identity expressed at different levels of formality for different audiences.

## Why Brand Voice Strategy Matters in Clinical Contexts

In most consumer brands, voice strategy is primarily a marketing concern. In clinical and forensic contexts, it becomes a **communication safety system**:

- The wrong register with a family can undermine trust and impede care.
- The wrong register in a forensic report can be cross-examined and damage credibility.
- Inconsistent terminology confuses providers and misaligns treatment planning.
- Ableist or stigmatizing language causes harm, regardless of intent.

A well-formed brand voice strategy in healthcare explicitly addresses inclusive language, person-first defaults, and score interpretation framing — e.g., describing scores as a "snapshot of performance, not a fixed trait."

## Key Principle

> Voice is what you always are. Tone is how you show up in the room.

A robust brand voice strategy gives writers enough constraint to stay on-brand and enough flexibility to meet each audience where they are — without requiring case-by-case editorial judgment for every sentence. For practices serving both families and courts, this flexibility is not a luxury; it is a clinical and professional necessity.
