# Neurodevelopmental Clinical Intake and Family History Interview

A neurodevelopmental clinical intake is a structured, multi-informant interview process used to gather comprehensive developmental, behavioral, social, and family history in order to guide differential diagnosis — particularly for conditions on the autism spectrum, OCD spectrum, and related neurodevelopmental profiles. It is distinct from formal psychometric testing: the intake precedes and contextualizes standardized assessment by building a longitudinal picture of the patient across settings and developmental stages.

See [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]] for a detailed example of this process in practice.

---

## Purpose and Structure

The neurodevelopmental clinical intake serves several goals:

- **Establish developmental chronology**: When did symptoms or differences first appear? What was the trajectory (early delay, plateau, gradual improvement)?
- **Gather multi-informant perspectives**: Patient self-report alone is often insufficient; parents, partners, and caregivers provide crucial longitudinal data that the patient cannot.
- **Contextualize current functioning**: Current challenges (e.g., job loss, car accidents, social isolation) are mapped onto a developmental backdrop to distinguish neurodevelopmental features from situational or acquired ones.
- **Screen across diagnostic domains**: A single intake can simultaneously probe for autism spectrum disorder, OCD-spectrum features, executive function deficits, speech and language disorders, sensory processing differences, and emotional regulation difficulties.

---

## Key Informant Roles

In the [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]] example, three informants participate:

| Informant | Role |
|---|---|
| Patient (adult male, mid-30s) | Self-report; insight into current subjective experience |
| Father (in person) | Behavioral observations across childhood, adolescence, adulthood |
| Mother (by phone) | Primary childhood caregiver; detailed early developmental history |

This multi-voice structure is especially important when the patient has limited introspective access to early development or when affect is flat and self-report is sparse.

The CARS2-HF (High-Functioning Version of the Childhood Autism Rating Scale, Second Edition) formalizes this multi-informant requirement: completing that form requires information from direct observation *and* an informant such as a parent, spouse, or teacher who can describe behavior across settings. The CARS2-ST (Standard Version) can be completed from a single source, but multiple sources remain best practice.

## Core Domains Assessed

### 1. Early Developmental Milestones
- Age of first words, babbling onset, and language emergence
- Non-verbal communication (pointing, sign language use)
- Motor development (tone, coordination, fine vs. gross motor)
- Feeding history (failure to thrive, texture sensitivities)
- Response to name and early social orienting

The CARS2 framework emphasizes that symptoms of autism should have been present early in life — prior to age 4 or 5 — to support an ASD diagnosis. This makes gathering reliable early developmental history a critical component of any neurodevelopmental intake.

### 2. Speech and Language History
Severe early language delays are a central feature when present. In the transcript example, the patient was non-verbal until approximately age 4, never babbled, learned sign language before spoken words, and attended the Callier Center (a specialized speech/language preschool program). See [[concepts/speech-language-development-disorders]].

For high-functioning individuals, language may appear superficially intact, but the CARS2-HF specifically targets subtler verbal oddities: overly formal or pedantic speech, unusual intonation or volume, limited conversational reciprocity, and made-up or repetitive phrases. An individual may have fluent speech but still demonstrate significant pragmatic language impairment.

### 3. Social and Emotional Development
- Peer relationships across developmental stages
- Reciprocity and conversational give-and-take
- Theory of mind and perspective-taking ability (whether the individual can perceive and articulate how another person feels or what their perspective may be)
- Emotional expression and regulation (affect flatness, explosive episodes, meltdowns)
- Romantic relationship history and avoidance

The CARS2-HF separates social functioning into distinct rating areas: Social-Emotional Understanding (cognitive grasp of others' communication and perspectives), Emotional Expression and Regulation of Emotions (capacity to express and control one's own emotions), and Relating to People (initiation of and reciprocity in direct interactions). This tripartite structure mirrors the multi-layered social deficits clinicians probe in the intake.

See [[concepts/autism-spectrum-disorder-clinical-features]] and [[concepts/theory-of-mind]] for the core diagnostic dimensions explored.

### 4. Behavioral and Restricted-Interest Features
- Rigid ordering behaviors (lining up toys, requiring symmetry)
- Compulsive correction of disorder (filing others' invoices at work)
- Perfectionistic standards (white-out over crossing out, re-doing others' handwriting)
- Repetitive apologizing and compulsive social repair behaviors
- Special interests unusual in intensity or narrowness
- Insistence on sameness; ritualistic routines

The CARS2 Adaptation to Change / Restricted Interests item rates difficulty coping with change, ritualistic behaviors, and restricted special interests on a single continuum, using the most severe area as the basis for the rating. Clinicians probe these dimensions during intake by asking about routines, transitions, preferred topics, and responses to unexpected change.

### 5. Executive Function and Judgment
- Ability to translate knowledge into action (knowing vs. doing)
- Planning and anticipatory awareness (e.g., driving when fatigued)
- Multi-step instruction following
- Sequencing difficulties
- Attention shifting and cognitive flexibility

Research underlying the CARS2-HF identifies executive dysfunction — particularly impaired flexibility, planning, and attention shifting — as among the most consistently documented cognitive features of ASD across ages. See [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]].

### 6. Sensory Processing
- Auditory response (response to name, sound over- or under-reactivity)
- Visual response (eye contact integration, side-eye observation, visual fascination with lights or reflections)
- Tactile (rigidity to touch, fine motor grip, clothing texture sensitivity)
- Gustatory/olfactory (food texture sensitivities, picky eating, smelling objects)
- Pain reactivity (over- or under-response)

The CARS2 rates each sensory modality separately: Visual Response, Listening Response, and Taste/Smell/Touch Response and Use. For high-functioning individuals, the most common presentations are subtle: clothing or food preferences that resist change, aversion to particular ambient sounds, or selective visual fascination. To score 3 or higher on the CARS2-HF for sensory items, unusual responses must be pervasive across settings and self-reported or directly observed.

### 7. Thinking Style and Cognitive Integration
The CARS2-HF introduces a dedicated Thinking/Cognitive Integration Skills item that captures weak central coherence — the tendency to focus on details while missing the larger conceptual meaning. This is assessed by asking individuals to identify the main point of a passage or picture, and noting whether they report numerous details without synthesizing. The intake can probe this informally through conversation about life events and by observing whether the individual integrates context or remains surface-level and literal.

### 8. Adaptive Functioning and Independence
- Current daily living skills
- Financial independence
- Degree of family scaffolding still in place
- Future planning and anticipated support needs

The CARS2-HF Level and Consistency of Intellectual Response item explicitly weights adaptive skills: individuals whose adaptive skills are appropriate for their age and intellectual level receive lower scores, regardless of intellectual variability. This means adaptive functioning must be directly assessed during the intake, not inferred from IQ alone.

## Use of Semi-Structured Rating Instruments

Clinicians often anchor the interview to a semi-structured rating scale to ensure systematic coverage and generate quantifiable severity ratings. In [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]], the clinician uses a four-point scale across multiple behavioral domains.

The CARS2 instruments formalize this approach. Both the CARS2-ST and CARS2-HF use a 1–4 rating system (with half-point increments) across 15 domains, generating a Total raw score (15–60) and a T-score calibrated on a clinical sample of over 2,000 individuals with ASD diagnoses. The T-score reflects severity relative to the autism clinical population — not the general population — making it useful for characterizing functional profiles even when a diagnosis is already established.

The CARS2-QPC (Questionnaire for Parents or Caregivers) is an unscored companion form that systematically collects caregiver observations across the same domains. It is designed to be completed by a parent or caregiver before the clinical session and used as a framework for a follow-up interview. Importantly, parents should not complete the CARS2-ST or CARS2-HF themselves — only the QPC.

Ratings are gathered collaboratively — clinician, patient, and informants discuss each domain together, which also serves a psychoeducational function. See [[concepts/behavioral-rating-scales]] and [[concepts/autism-diagnostic-rating-scales]].

## Interpreting Caregiver Reports

The CARS2 manual identifies four key considerations when interpreting caregiver responses:

1. **Overall pattern of strengths and weaknesses**: Look for discrepant skill profiles — e.g., strong verbal skills paired with severe sensory preoccupations and poor nonverbal communication.
2. **Quality of examples**: A caregiver may endorse a symptom but describe a behavior not consistent with ASD phenomenology. Clarify whether the example reflects the specific type of behavior being rated.
3. **Cross-source consistency**: When sources give discrepant reports, further inquiry clarifies whether behavior genuinely differs across settings or is being described differently by different observers.
4. **Developmental onset**: ASD requires that symptoms were present early in life (before age 4–5). Other conditions (e.g., trauma, acquired social difficulties) can produce social problems without the same early developmental history.

These considerations map directly onto the clinical judgment exercised during the neurodevelopmental intake interview.

## The Role of Family Involvement

Parent and family involvement in adult neurodevelopmental intakes is particularly valuable when:

1. The patient has limited recall of early development
2. The patient's affect or communication style limits self-report richness
3. The family system itself is part of the clinical picture (e.g., degree of parental scaffolding, adaptive vs. enabling dynamics)

In [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]], the mother's involvement reveals early features (side-eye observation, no babbling, failure to thrive) that neither the patient nor the father could have reported accurately. The father provides behavioral observations from adolescence and adulthood that the patient underreports due to limited insight.

The CARS2-HF's mandatory multi-informant requirement formalizes what experienced clinicians know: for high-functioning individuals with ASD, the gap between self-presentation and real-world behavioral patterns is often substantial, and collateral information is not optional.

## Diagnostic Considerations Arising from the Intake

The intake does not typically yield a final diagnosis but narrows the differential and frames the formal testing that follows. Common considerations include:

- **High-functioning autism spectrum disorder** (historical Asperger's profile): restricted interests, early language delay without intellectual disability, flat affect, sensory sensitivities, social reciprocity difficulties
- **OCD-spectrum features**: intrusive thoughts about disorder compelling corrective action; "just right" phenomenology; compulsive social appeasement
- **Executive function deficits**: difficulty translating intention into action, poor real-time judgment
- **Speech dyspraxia**: severe early motor speech disorder now largely compensated

The CARS2 instruments provide quantitative anchoring for these differential considerations. Average CARS2-HF Total scores differ significantly across clinical groups: high-functioning autism (mean ~35), Asperger's Disorder (mean ~33), PDD-NOS (mean ~34), ADHD (mean ~20), learning disorders (mean ~19). Pattern differences in item ratings — not just total scores — help distinguish between these groups. For example, individuals with ADHD tend to receive high ratings on Level and Consistency of Intellectual Response and Emotional Expression at lower total score levels, while General Impressions only becomes elevated at much higher total scores compared to the ASD profile.

See [[concepts/staged-clinical-intake]] for how this type of interview fits into a broader assessment pipeline, and [[concepts/cognitive-domains]] for the domains subsequently tested.

## Providing Feedback After the Intake

A well-conducted intake also creates the foundation for diagnostic feedback. The CARS2 manual recommends discussing severity along two separate dimensions:

1. **Cognitive/language dimension**: ranging from severe intellectual disability with no verbal skills to average or above-average intelligence with well-developed academic skills
2. **Autism severity dimension**: ranging from mild social difficulties with flexibility to severe rigidity, extreme repetitive behaviors, and inability to tolerate social proximity

This two-dimensional framework helps families understand why "high-functioning autism" or "Asperger's" does not necessarily mean "mild autism" — a person may have strong cognitive skills but severe social-behavioral rigidity. It also allows the clinician to position the patient relative to peers with similar presentations and to prioritize intervention targets.

## Contextual and Environmental Factors

A well-conducted neurodevelopmental intake also captures situational moderators:

- **COVID-era remote work isolation**: In [[summaries/multi_patient_transcript]], the shift to working from home is identified as a plausible environmental trigger that removed the social scaffolding the patient had relied on in office settings.
- **Family support as compensatory infrastructure**: The patient's relative functioning is understood partly as a product of intensive early intervention and ongoing parental support — raising questions about what functioning looks like without that scaffold.
- **Cultural and contextual norms**: The clinician explicitly notes that some behaviors (e.g., deflecting eye contact while speaking, preferring familiar social topics) exist on a continuum and must be interpreted in context.
- **Environmental scaffolding and structured teaching**: Intervention histories matter. Patients who received early structured teaching (e.g., Division TEACCH-based approaches) may present with substantially compensated profiles that mask the underlying neurodevelopmental differences. See [[concepts/structured-teaching-teacch]].
