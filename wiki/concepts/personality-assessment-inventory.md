# Personality Assessment Inventory (PAI)

The **Personality Assessment Inventory (PAI)** is a standardized, self-report psychological test designed to assess a broad range of personality characteristics and psychopathology relevant to clinical diagnosis, treatment planning, and forensic evaluation. Developed by Leslie C. Morey, PhD, and published by PAR (Psychological Assessment Resources), the PAI consists of 344 items responded to on a 4-point scale: False (F), Slightly True (ST), Mainly True (MT), or Very True (VT).

Scores are plotted as T scores based on a U.S. Census-matched standardization sample of 1,000 normal adults. Elevations greater than two standard deviations above the mean for the PAI clinical sample (N = 1,246 clinical patients) are considered clinically significant.

---

## Structure

The PAI is organized into:
- **Full scales** covering validity, clinical, treatment, and interpersonal domains
- **Subscales** providing more granular measurement within each full scale
- **Supplemental indices** for suicide risk, violence risk, treatment process, malingering, defensiveness, and more
- **Alternative Model for Personality Disorders** profile

### Key Scale Domains
| Domain | Examples |
|---|---|
| Validity | Inconsistency (ICN), Infrequency (INF), Negative Impression (NIM), Positive Impression (PIM) |
| Clinical | Somatic Complaints (SOM), Anxiety (ANX), Anxiety-Related Disorders (ARD), Depression (DEP), Mania (MAN), Paranoia (PAR), Schizophrenia (SCZ), Borderline Features (BOR), Antisocial Features (ANT) |
| Treatment | Aggression (AGG), Suicidal Ideation (SUI), Stress (STR), Nonsupport (NON), Treatment Rejection (RXR) |
| Interpersonal | Dominance (DOM), Warmth (WRM) |

## Validity Assessment

The PAI includes multiple layers of validity evaluation:

1. **Item completion** — Flags scales with >20% missing items (indicated by ♦ on profile).
2. **Response consistency** — Back Random Responding; Hong Randomness Index.
3. **Negative impression management** — Malingering Index, Rogers Discriminant Function, Negative Distortion Scale, Hong Malingering Index, Multiscale Feigning Index, Malingered Pain-Related Disability Discriminant Function.
4. **Positive impression management** — Defensiveness Index, Cashel Discriminant Function, Positive Distortion Scale, Hong Defensiveness Index.

Thresholds: NIM ≥ 84 and PIM ≥ 57 trigger NIM/PIM-specific profile overlays. When both indices fall below threshold — as seen across multiple case examples — the protocol is considered valid and the respondent is judged to have answered in a reasonably forthright manner. When both positive and negative distortion indicators are present simultaneously, the profile reflects a mixed response style — for example, global defensiveness absent but denial of specific problems (e.g., substance use), combined with mild exaggeration of other complaints. Validity interpretation is central to [[concepts/validity-language]] used in clinical reports.

The ALC Estimated Score and DRG Estimated Score compare predicted substance involvement (based on personality characteristics) to self-reported scores; a large discrepancy flags likely under-reporting of alcohol or drug use. In the **Krishan Patel case (npsych220428, age 21)**, the ALC Estimated Score was 22T higher than ALC and the DRG Estimated Score was 17T higher than DRG — a substantial discrepancy flagging likely denial of alcohol and drug use problems despite the respondent reporting no significant substance abuse or dependence. In the **Elea Zhong case (npsych230518, age 20)**, ALC Estimated Score was 10T higher than ALC and DRG Estimated Score was 8T higher than DRG — a moderate discrepancy suggesting possible denial of substance use problems, even in the absence of global positive impression management. In the **Tianyu Wang case (npsych220505, age 24)**, ALC Estimated Score was 15T higher than ALC and DRG Estimated Score was 13T higher than DRG — a notable discrepancy indicating possible denial of alcohol and drug use that should be reviewed with caution despite an otherwise clean validity profile. In less severe cases, a 7–9T discrepancy may indicate mild minimization of substance-related problems.

In the **Ed Gonzalez case (EG24, age 62)**, the ALC Estimated Score was 30T lower than the actual ALC score — a notable discrepancy in the opposite direction, where the actual ALC elevation substantially exceeds what personality-based prediction would expect. This pattern contrasts sharply with cases where ALC/DRG estimated scores exceed actual scores (suggesting denial), and instead reflects frank acknowledgment of alcohol-related difficulties consistent with the client's self-reported problems. The DRG Estimated Score was 8T higher than DRG, suggesting some possible minimization of drug involvement.

In the **Alexander Jacobs case (AlJac, age 32)**, all validity indicators fall within normal limits. Zero items were missing, consistent responding was demonstrated, and all distortion indicators are in the normal range. NIM and PIM both fall below thresholds, so no NIM/PIM-specific profiles were generated. ALC Estimated Score was 9T higher than ALC and DRG Estimated Score was equal to DRG — a mild discrepancy for ALC but no drug-related concern. This profile is fully interpretable, representing a straightforward within-normal-limits validity picture.

Some protocols may exhibit idiosyncratic response patterns to particular items without exceeding formal validity thresholds. In such cases, interpretive hypotheses should be reviewed cautiously even when the overall response style appears forthright. One example is a protocol where Back Random Responding reached 76T — indicating some inconsistency in early-versus-late responses — combined with subtle negative impression management indicators that together suggest possible exaggeration in specific areas without rendering the test uninterpretable.

Another validity pattern involves simultaneous defensiveness in particular domains alongside subtle negative impression management: the respondent shows no evidence of global positive impression management, yet demonstrates possible denial of substance use problems (personality-based ALC/DRG estimated scores 18T and 6T higher than actual), while also presenting with patterns relatively common among individuals feigning mental disorder. The overall profile remains interpretable but clinical scale elevations may overrepresent symptom severity in certain areas.

**Substance use denial in a manic presentation** is illustrated in the Krishan Patel case (npsych220428, age 21). Despite no evidence of global positive or negative impression management, ALC and DRG estimated scores were 22T and 17T higher respectively than self-reported scale scores — among the largest discrepancies documented across cases in this wiki. The Cashel Discriminant Function was also elevated (T=74), adding a further signal of possible subtle positive impression management. No NIM/PIM-specific profiles were generated (NIM and PIM both below thresholds), and the Malingering Index (T=71) was modestly elevated. The overall profile fit the college students context-specific norm group most closely (coefficient = 0.573), consistent with the client's demographic.

**Substance use denial in a subclinical presentation** is illustrated in the **Tianyu Wang case (npsych220505, age 24)**. Despite no evidence of global positive or negative impression management (Malingering Index T=44, Defensiveness Index T=44, NIM and PIM both below thresholds), the ALC and DRG estimated scores were 15T and 13T higher than self-reported scores respectively. The Cashel Discriminant Function was mildly elevated (T=70). No NIM/PIM-specific profiles were generated. This is a clinically important pattern in an otherwise subclinical profile — substance-related interpretive hypotheses require cautious review.

**Mild positive impression management without invalidity** is illustrated in the Anna Finkel case (npsych230202, age 21). All validity indices fell within acceptable limits (NIM and PIM both below thresholds), with zero missing items and consistent responding. However, the Defensiveness Index (T=63) indicated a tendency to present in a favorable light and minimize common shortcomings — particularly regarding negative behavioral consequences.

**Mild positive impression management in an older adult** is also illustrated in the **Ed Gonzalez case (EG24, age 62)**. The Defensiveness Index (T=63) and Hong Defensiveness Index (T=67) are both mildly elevated, indicating a tendency to present favorably and minimize personal limitations. Results are not invalid but may underrepresent problem severity. All negative distortion indicators fell within normal limits (Malingering Index T=44, Rogers Discriminant Function T=40). The Cashel Discriminant Function (T=31) was notably low, and the profile showed statistically significant fit with the "Fake good" response set (coefficient = 0.460). The profile context-specific fit pattern is distinctive: statistically significant fit with potential kidney donors (0.486), law enforcement officer candidates (0.473), and egg donors and gestational carriers (0.460) — all healthy screening contexts — alongside a notably negative fit with motor vehicle accident claimants (-0.473), suggesting no evidence of symptom amplification in a medicolegal context.

**Normal/within-limits validity profiles** are also observed. In the Alexandra Stanley case (npsych230406, age 25), all validity indices fell within normal limits with zero missing items, appropriate attention to content, and consistent responding. All negative and positive distortion indicators had T scores in the 40–53 range. The **Alexander Jacobs case (AlJac, age 32)** similarly presents a fully clean validity profile with all indicators in the normal range and zero missing items.

**The Gorety Nguyen case (npsych230316, age 23)** similarly demonstrates a within-limits validity profile: NIM and PIM both below thresholds (no NIM/PIM-specific profiles generated), zero missing items, consistent responding, and all distortion indicators in the normal range (Malingering Index T=57, Defensiveness Index T=57). Notably, the NIM predicted profile coefficient of fit (0.540) is statistically significant despite the NIM score remaining below threshold — this warrants awareness when interpreting clinical scale elevations. A critical item endorsement of "I think I have three or four completely different personalities inside of me" (NIM item #129) as Very True is also flagged under potential malingering.

**The Elea Zhong case (npsych230518, age 20)** presents a largely clean validity profile: NIM and PIM both below thresholds, zero missing items, consistent responding, and no evidence of negative impression management. All negative distortion indicators fall within normal limits (Malingering Index T=44, Rogers Discriminant Function T=37). The sole validity concern is the ALC/DRG estimated score discrepancy described above, suggesting possible denial of substance involvement rather than global positive impression management. The Cashel Discriminant Function (T=63) is mildly elevated but does not constitute evidence of broad defensiveness.

**The Eric Roizman case (ER24, age 55)** presents a fully valid profile: an acceptable number of items were completed, consistent attention to content was demonstrated, and the respondent provided a forthright report of symptoms without significant distortion. NIM and PIM both fell below clinical thresholds, so no NIM/PIM-specific profiles were generated. This validity picture is notable given the complexity and severity of the clinical findings in this case.

See also [[concepts/clinical-report-structure]] for how validity findings are organized in narrative output, and [[concepts/malingering-detection]] for cross-document discussion of feigning detection.

## Supplemental Clinical Indices

The PAI Plus report includes a rich set of supplemental indices beyond the core scales:

- **Suicide Potential Index** — Composite indicator of suicide risk; see [[concepts/suicide-and-violence-risk-indices]]. Scores in the T=65–68 range indicate periodic, transient suicidal ideation warranting follow-up; elevated scores (T=74–81) may co-occur with a client not currently reporting distress from self-harm thoughts (as in Gorety Nguyen, T=74, where no active suicidal ideation was reported); extreme elevations (T=80+) indicate acute risk. Normal range (e.g., T=43–53) indicates no current suicidal ideation. The Krishan Patel case (T=68) illustrates a clinically significant elevation without current reported self-harm distress. The Elea Zhong case (T=50) illustrates a within-normal-limits score in a low-acuity profile. The Ed Gonzalez case (T=43) similarly reflects no significant suicidal ideation in a profile dominated by alcohol-related concerns. The **Tianyu Wang case (T=50)** also falls within normal limits, with no reported distress from thoughts of self-harm. The **Alexander Jacobs case (T=43)** similarly reflects no suicidal ideation in a within-normal-limits profile.
- **Chronic Suicide Risk (S_Chron)** — Long-term suicide risk beyond acute ideation; T=63 observed in the Gorety Nguyen case alongside a Suicide Potential Index of 74; T=65 in low-acuity presentations with primarily cognitive depression; T=60 in the Krishan Patel case; T=54 in the **Alexander Jacobs case**; T=51 in the Ed Gonzalez case; T=51 in the Tianyu Wang case; T=45 in the Elea Zhong case; T=42 in within-normal-limits profiles.
- **Violence Potential Index** — Composite of aggression risk; scores can reach extreme elevations (e.g., T=112) in high-risk profiles. T=75 is clinically significant even when anger self-report appears controlled. T=70 observed in the Krishan Patel case, consistent with the manic/irritable presentation and elevated reactive aggression. Normal range (e.g., T=43, T=47, T=52) seen in typical and within-normal-limits profiles; the Elea Zhong case returned T=47; the Ed Gonzalez case returned T=47; the **Tianyu Wang case returned T=52** — within normal limits; the **Alexander Jacobs case returned T=43** — within normal limits.
- **Treatment Process Index** — Predicts treatment difficulty and engagement; T=65 observed in the Gorety Nguyen case (above average motivation); T=70 in the Krishan Patel case (above average for treatment settings, despite anticipated treatment challenges); T=55 in the Ed Gonzalez case (comparable to adults not in treatment); T=55 in the **Tianyu Wang case** (within normal range, below typical clinical levels); T=44 seen in low-motivation, self-satisfied presentations including the Elea Zhong case and the **Alexander Jacobs case**.
- **Inattention (INATTN) Index** — Flags potential inattention during testing (experimental); T=77 in Gorety Nguyen is consistent with clinically reported concentration difficulties and thought disorganization; T=99 in Krishan Patel — an extreme elevation representing a notable outlier and consistent with the profile's prominent distractibility, confusion, and thought disorganization features; T=56 in the Elea Zhong case — mildly elevated but not clinically significant; T=56 in the **Tianyu Wang case** — similarly mildly elevated but not clinically significant; T=45 in the Ed Gonzalez case — within normal limits; T=45 in the **Alexander Jacobs case** — within normal limits.
- **Reactive and Instrumental Aggression Scales** — Distinguish reactive (affective, impulsive) from instrumental (planned, goal-directed) aggression subtypes; T=53/48 in Gorety Nguyen; T=80/72 in Krishan Patel; T=44/54 in Ed Gonzalez; T=42/40 in the Elea Zhong case; T=52/54 in the **Tianyu Wang case** — both within normal limits; T=46/46 in the **Alexander Jacobs case** — both within normal limits; T=41/40 seen in other within-normal-limits profiles.
- **Level of Care Index** — Guides level-of-care decisions; T=61 observed in both the Gorety Nguyen and Krishan Patel cases; T=64 in moderate presentations; T=44 in within-normal-limits profiles including the Elea Zhong case; T=44 in the **Tianyu Wang case**; T=42 in the **Alexander Jacobs case**; T=42 in the Ed Gonzalez case.
- **ALC/DRG Estimated Scores** — Detect under-reporting of substance use via personality-based prediction. In Gorety Nguyen, ALC Estimated Score was 2T lower than ALC and DRG Estimated Score was 3T higher than DRG — a modest discrepancy. In Krishan Patel, the discrepancies were 22T and 17T respectively. In Elea Zhong, the discrepancies were 10T and 8T respectively. In **Tianyu Wang**, the discrepancies were 15T and 13T respectively — notable and warranting cautious substance-related interpretation. In Ed Gonzalez, the ALC Estimated Score was 30T **lower** than the actual ALC — the largest documented reverse discrepancy, indicating frank self-acknowledgment of alcohol problems that substantially exceed personality-based prediction. In the **Alexander Jacobs case**, ALC Estimated Score was 9T higher than ALC and DRG Estimated Score was equal to DRG — a mild ALC discrepancy of limited clinical significance.
- **Mean Clinical Elevation** — Global psychopathology index; T=61 in both Gorety Nguyen and Krishan Patel indicates mild-to-moderate overall symptom burden; values around T=82 indicate broad severe impairment; T=51 in the **Tianyu Wang case** reflects a subclinical overall profile; T=48 in the **Alexander Jacobs case** reflects an entirely within-normal-limits clinical profile; T=47 in the Elea Zhong case reflects an entirely subclinical clinical profile; T=46 in the Ed Gonzalez case reflects a similarly low overall clinical burden despite the ALC elevation.
- **RXR Estimated Score** — Predicted treatment rejection based on clinical profile; when estimated RXR exceeds actual RXR, it suggests the client acknowledges more problems than they are willing to engage in treatment for. In Gorety Nguyen, RXR Estimated Score was 3T higher than actual RXR. In Krishan Patel, RXR Estimated Score was 8T higher than actual RXR. In **Tianyu Wang**, RXR Estimated Score was 4T **lower** than actual RXR — suggesting the client is even less invested in treatment than her clinical profile predicts, consistent with her self-satisfied, low-distress presentation. In Elea Zhong, RXR Estimated Score was 8T **lower** than actual RXR — the same pattern. In Ed Gonzalez, RXR Estimated Score was 2T higher than actual RXR — a minimal discrepancy consistent with his below-average treatment motivation. In the **Alexander Jacobs case**, RXR Estimated Score was 11T **lower** than actual RXR — the client's actual treatment rejection substantially exceeds what the clinical profile predicts, consistent with his self-satisfied, low-distress, within-normal-limits presentation.
- **Neuro-Item Sum** — Experimental composite of neurologically relevant item responses; T=60 in Gorety Nguyen, consistent with reported cognitive complaints; T=71 in Krishan Patel, consistent with prominent confusion, distractibility, and thought disorganization; T=57 in Ed Gonzalez — mildly elevated but not clinically significant; T=55 in **Tianyu Wang** — within normal limits; T=53 in Elea Zhong, within normal limits; T=48 in the **Alexander Jacobs case** — within normal limits.
- **Violence and Aggression Risk Index** — Additional experimental composite; T=46 in Gorety Nguyen; T=62 in Krishan Patel; T=46 in Ed Gonzalez; T=52 in **Tianyu Wang**; T=49 in Elea Zhong; T=46 in the **Alexander Jacobs case**; T=39 in within-normal-limits profiles.

Experimental indices are denoted with an asterisk (*) and italicized text in reports; they should be interpreted cautiously given limited cross-validation research.

## Profile Matching

The PAI Plus report computes **coefficients of fit** with profiles of known clinical groups, modal cluster profiles, symptom behavior groups, response set groups, and context-specific norm groups (e.g., motor vehicle accident claimants, chronic pain patients, law enforcement candidates, college students, child custody evaluations). Coefficients above **.42** represent statistically significant associations.

This enables the clinician to understand how closely the client's profile resembles established diagnostic groups or situational contexts, supporting differential diagnosis and broader neuropsychological score interpretation.

### Diagnostic Group Fit Patterns Observed Across Cases

Profile fit varies substantially across clinical presentations:

- **Manic/hypomanic presentations** (e.g., Krishan Patel, npsych220428) show highest fit with bipolar I disorder/mania (0.562), schizoaffective disorder (0.452), adjustment disorders (0.438), and anxiety disorders (0.426). Symptom behavior fit includes current aggression (0.422) as statistically significant. Context-specific fit is highest for college students (0.573). PAI Cluster 5 shows the highest cluster fit (0.579).
- **Antisocial/substance-use-predominant presentations** (e.g., Gorety Nguyen, npsych230316) show highest fit with alcohol use disorders (0.602), substance use disorders (0.596), bipolar I disorder/mania (0.539), and antisocial personality disorder (0.514). Symptom behavior fit is statistically significant for prisoners (0.532), perpetrators of rape (0.521), assault history (0.506), and self-mutilation (0.500). Context-specific fit with motor vehicle accident claimants is low (0.256).
- **High-acuity/complex cases** may show highest fit with PTSD, schizophrenia, and anxiety disorders, with significant fit also for borderline personality disorder, schizoaffective disorder, and bipolar I.
- **Moderate-acuity cases** (e.g., primary anxiety with trauma history and mild substance use) show highest fit with schizoaffective disorder (0.597), anxiety disorders (0.592), borderline personality disorder (0.563), PTSD (0.557), and major depressive disorder (0.556).
- **Young adult presentations** show highest profile fit with anxiety disorders (0.700), PTSD (0.655), schizophrenia (0.646), schizoaffective disorder (0.637), persistent depressive disorder (0.623), and major depressive disorder (0.620).
- **Somatic-predominant presentations** show highest diagnostic fit with schizophrenia (0.859), PTSD (0.745), and anxiety disorders (0.699), with significant symptom behavior fit with auditory hallucinations (0.808), antipsychotic medications (0.789), and persecutory delusions (0.763).
- **Depressive/thought-disturbance presentations** show highest fit with schizophrenia (0.877), schizoaffective disorder (0.822), PTSD (0.797), persistent depressive disorder (0.789), and major depressive disorder (0.780). Despite the schizophrenia fit, active hallucinations and delusions may not be a prominent clinical feature — highlighting that profile fit reflects symptomatic patterning rather than confirmed diagnosis.
- **Within-normal-limits profiles** (e.g., Alexandra Stanley, npsych230406) show no statistically significant fit with any clinical diagnostic group. Highest fit is instead with healthy screening populations: potential kidney donors (0.555), egg donors/gestational carriers (0.549), and law enforcement officer candidates (0.493).
- **Mildly defensive, within-normal-limits profiles** (e.g., Anna Finkel, npsych230202) show similarly low fit with clinical diagnostic groups — all below 0.42. Context-specific fit is highest for egg donors/gestational carriers (0.371), child custody evaluations (0.345), potential kidney donors (0.322), and law enforcement officer candidates (0.315).
- **Subclinical/low-acuity college-aged profiles** (e.g., Elea Zhong, npsych230518) show top diagnostic group fit with unspecified somatic symptom and related disorder (0.132) and adjustment disorders (0.127), well below the significance threshold. Context-specific fit is highest for college students (0.310). No coefficient reaches statistical significance (≥0.42). PAI Cluster 5 shows the highest cluster fit (0.208).
- **Subclinical profiles with mood variability and substance use denial** (e.g., **Tianyu Wang, npsych220505, age 24**) show highest diagnostic group fit with schizophrenia (0.278), schizoaffective disorder (0.265), PTSD (0.207), anxiety disorders (0.181), and bipolar I disorder/mania (0.172) — none reaching statistical significance. Context-specific fit is highest for college students (0.240). PAI Cluster 6 shows the highest cluster fit (0.381). Symptom behavior fit is led by auditory hallucinations (0.210) and antipsychotic medications (0.202), though these do not reach significance. No context-specific or diagnostic group coefficient reaches the 0.42 threshold.
- **Primarily alcohol-focused profiles with mild defensiveness** (e.g., **Ed Gonzalez, EG24, age 62**) show highest diagnostic group fit with alcohol use disorders (0.171) — the only positive diagnostic coefficient — and negative fit with all other clinical groups. Context-specific fit is statistically significant for potential kidney donors (0.486), law enforcement officer candidates (0.473), and egg donors and gestational carriers (0.460), and for the "Fake good" response set (0.460). Notably negative fit with motor vehicle accident claimants (-0.473) and fake bad response set (-0.469). Cluster 9 shows the highest cluster fit (0.463 — statistically significant). Symptom behavior fit is modest and positive only for spouse abusers (0.126). This profile fit pattern is consistent with a middle-aged adult with acknowledged alcohol problems presenting without amplification of psychological symptoms and with a generally healthy self-presentation outside the substance use domain.
- **Within-normal-limits adult profiles without college student context** (e.g., **Alexander Jacobs, AlJac, age 32**) show the highest diagnostic group fit with substance use disorders (0.107) and bipolar I disorder/mania (0.043) — both well below the significance threshold, and no negative fit with severe clinical groups that would be notable (e.g., schizophrenia at -0.248, PTSD at -0.245). Context-specific fit is **statistically significant for college students (0.537)** — the strongest fit across all norm groups for this case — despite the respondent being 32 years old. PAI Cluster 1 shows the highest cluster fit (0.127). Symptom behavior fit is led by spouse abusers (0.111) and prisoners (0.091), both below significance. The NIM predicted profile shows a moderate fit (0.286) worth monitoring, and the "Fake good" response set shows a moderate fit (0.249). This pattern is consistent with a psychologically healthy adult male without active psychopathology whose response style most closely resembles a college student normative profile.
- **Symptom behavior fit** patterns of note include suicide history, self-mutilation, and antipsychotic medication use as common co-occurring features even in moderate presentations.

Context-specific norm group fitting allows screening for situational response sets (e.g., similarity to motor vehicle accident claimants vs. law enforcement candidates). Profile fit with the NIM predicted profile is also tracked as a validity check.

## Critical Items

The PAI includes **27 critical items** reflecting serious pathology with very low endorsement rates in normal samples. Categories include:
- **Delusions and hallucinations** (e.g., thought broadcasting — SCZ-P item #130 endorsed as MT; heard voices no one else could hear — SCZ-P item #170 endorsed as ST in one case)
- **Potential for self-harm** (e.g., suicidal planning — SUI; no interest in life — DEP-A item #206 endorsed as ST)
- **Potential for aggression** (e.g., explosive anger loss of control — AGG-P item #61 endorsed as Very True in Krishan Patel; people afraid of my temper — AGG-P item #21 endorsed as Slightly True in Krishan Patel, as ST in Gorety Nguyen, and as ST in Ed Gonzalez)
- **Traumatic stressors** (e.g., re-experiencing a horrible event — ARD-T item #34 endorsed as MT; troubled by memories of a bad experience — ARD-T item #114 endorsed as Slightly True in Krishan Patel and as ST in Gorety Nguyen; also both endorsed at ST level in the **Alexander Jacobs case**, flagging possible mild traumatic stress experiences worth exploring clinically)
- **Substance abuse, current and historical** (e.g., trouble controlling alcohol use — ALC #55 endorsed as MT by Ed Gonzalez; drinking never caused trouble — ALC #334 endorsed as False [keyed as false] by Ed Gonzalez, scoring 3; the same ALC #334 item is also flagged in the **Alexander Jacobs case** with a score of 3, warranting follow-up regarding possible history of alcohol-related difficulties; tried just about every type of drug — DRG #23 endorsed as ST; never use illegal drugs [False] — DRG #142 endorsed as Mainly True in both Gorety Nguyen and Krishan Patel)
- **Idiosyncratic context** (e.g., INF item #80 "Sometimes I get ads in the mail that I don't really want" [False keyed] endorsed as Mainly True by Ed Gonzalez — flagged as an idiosyncratic response)
- **Potential malingering** (e.g., three or four completely different personalities inside — NIM #129 endorsed as Very True in Gorety Nguyen, warranting follow-up)
- **Sleep disturbance** (e.g., no trouble falling asleep — DEP-P item #75 endorsed as Mainly True in the **Tianyu Wang case** — the sole critical item endorsed, suggesting possible sleep disturbance; the same item is also the sole critical item endorsed in the Anna Finkel and Alexandra Stanley within-normal-limits profiles; endorsed as Slightly True in Elea Zhong, suggesting mild sleep difficulty potentially linked to anxious features; endorsed as Slightly True in the **Alexander Jacobs case** as well; also endorsed in Krishan Patel)
- **Unreliability** (e.g., not needing to keep promises — ANT-E #311 endorsed as ST; taking advantage of others — ANT-E #71 endorsed as ST)

In the **Eric Roizman case (ER24)**, critical items were endorsed across multiple serious clinical domains, including psychosis, self-harm, aggression, substance abuse, and trauma — reflecting the broad and severe nature of this complex presentation. This stands in contrast to within-normal-limits profiles where only one or zero critical items may be endorsed.

In the **Ed Gonzalez case (EG24)**, critical items are limited to the substance abuse domain (ALC items #55 and #334), a mild aggression indicator (AGG-P #21), and an idiosyncratic context item (INF #80) — a focused pattern consistent with acknowledged alcohol-related problems without broader psychopathology.

In the **Tianyu Wang case (npsych220505)**, only a single critical item was endorsed (DEP-P #75, sleep disturbance), placing this case at the low end of critical item burden alongside other subclinical profiles.

In the **Alexander Jacobs case (AlJac, age 32)**, three critical items were endorsed: ALC #334 (drinking never caused trouble, keyed False — scored 3), ARD-T #34 (keeps reliving something horrible — Slightly True), and ARD-T #114 (troubled by memories of a bad experience — Slightly True), plus DEP-P #75 (no trouble falling asleep — Slightly True). This pattern, while occurring in an otherwise entirely within-normal-limits profile, warrants clinical follow-up regarding possible traumatic stress experiences and alcohol history.

Critical item review with the respondent can help clarify the clinical picture and is part of standard [[concepts/clinical-report-structure]] practice. Items are scored on the same 4-point scale (F/ST/MT/VT), and any score of 1, 2, or 3 (non-false) on a critical item is flagged.

## Clinical Features Assessed

The PAI narrative report addresses a wide range of clinical domains:

- **Anxiety** — Subjective tension, cognitive interference, physiological arousal (sweating, trembling, palpitations, shortness of breath); the ANX scale can be a primary elevation even when subjective tension is not prominent. In the Elea Zhong case, mild anxiety and stress are described as within the normal range — present but not clinically significant. In the Eric Roizman case, extreme anxiety characterized by phobias and somatic preoccupation is a central feature. See [[concepts/anxiety-clinical-features]].
- **Borderline personality features** — Emotional lability, identity instability, abandonment fears, impulsivity, self-harm risk; see [[concepts/borderline-personality-disorder]]. In the Eric Roizman case, rigid perfectionism, impulsivity, and emotional lability are prominent borderline-spectrum features.
- **Somatic concerns** — Multi-system physical complaints, conversion and somatization features, health preoccupation; social interactions dominated by somatic preoccupations. See [[concepts/somatic-symptom-disorder]]. The Eric Roizman case presents with anxiety characterized by somatic preoccupation as a key feature.
- **Psychosis** — Hallucinations, delusional beliefs, thought disorganization, ideas of reference; see [[concepts/psychosis-clinical-features]]. Active psychotic symptoms may be absent even when profile fit with schizophrenia or auditory hallucinations is high. The Eric Roizman case has critical items flagged for psychosis-range symptoms.
- **Mania/hypomania** — Elevated activity, grandiosity, racing thoughts, irritability, impulsivity. The Krishan Patel case illustrates a particularly severe manic presentation — described at a level uncommon even in clinical samples — with prominent irritability, disorganized overcommitment, accelerated thought processes, and interpersonal strain, but without prominent grandiosity. **In the Tianyu Wang case**, moderate mania-related features are present — a perceptibly high activity level, involvement in a wide variety of activities in a somewhat disorganized manner, and accelerated thought processes — without reaching clinically marked elevations. Symptoms consistent with manic or hypomanic episodes are also noted in the Eric Roizman case. See [[concepts/manic-episode-presentation]] and [[concepts/bipolar-disorder-clinical-features]].
- **Obsessive-compulsive features** — Perfectionism, rigidity, rumination, decision-making difficulty, fear of one's own impulses, maladaptive anxiety-control behaviors. The Eric Roizman case presents with rigid perfectionism as a notable feature.
- **Trauma/PTSD** — Re-experiencing, avoidance, distress from past traumatic events; endorsed via ARD-T subscale items. See [[concepts/trauma-informed-clinical-assessment]]. The Eric Roizman case includes a history of trauma among its clinical features. The **Alexander Jacobs case** shows low-level ARD-T critical item endorsement (items #34 and #114), suggesting possible mild past traumatic experiences worth exploring.
- **Depression** — Worthlessness, hopelessness, anhedonia, neurovegetative symptoms; depression may be primarily cognitive in character with minimal physiological disturbance, or present as mild/transient depressive symptomatology (as in Gorety Nguyen). Major depressive symptoms are prominent in the Eric Roizman case. See [[concepts/major-depressive-disorder-clinical-features]].
- **Paranoia/suspiciousness** — Hypervigilance, resentment, interpersonal hostility; tendency to attribute misfortune to others' neglect, hold grudges, envy others' successes, and discredit others' achievements as luck or favoritism. In manic presentations (e.g., Krishan Patel), paranoid ideation may manifest as a quick sense of being treated inequitably and belief that others are undermining one's interests. The Eric Roizman case presents with paranoid mistrust of others. See [[concepts/paranoia-and-suspiciousness]].
- **Antisocial features** — Stimulation-seeking, recklessness, thrill-seeking, impulsivity, disregard for long-term commitments; ANT is the primary elevation in the Gorety Nguyen case. The Eric Roizman case also presents with antisocial tendencies and a potential history of conduct disorder. See [[concepts/antisocial-personality-features]].
- **Substance use** — Alcohol and drug use assessed separately; see [[concepts/substance-use-clinical-assessment]]. Problematic substance use may co-occur with antisocial features, mood disturbance, or manic presentation — and denial of use in the context of elevated ALC/DRG estimated scores is clinically significant. The Eric Roizman case features significant polysubstance and alcohol dependence as central clinical findings. The **Ed Gonzalez case** represents the ALC scale as the **sole significant clinical elevation**, with frank self-acknowledgment of alcohol-related negative consequences including interpersonal difficulties, occupational problems, and possible health complications. See also [[concepts/alcohol-use-disorder-clinical-features]].
- **Thought disturbance** — Confusion, distractibility, difficulty concentrating, thought blocking, tangential or circumstantial speech. In manic presentations (e.g., Krishan Patel), thought disorganization is a key feature, with an Inattention Index at T=99 underscoring the severity. Cognitive confusion is also noted in the Eric Roizman case.
- **Impulsivity** — A major self-described problem in the Krishan Patel case; the client reported behaviors prone to self-harm (spending, sex, substance use), consistent with both manic and borderline personality features. Impulsivity is also a prominent feature in the Eric Roizman case.
- **Mood variability** — In the **Tianyu Wang case**, mood variability and moodiness are described as moderate-level concerns; others may view her as overly sensitive, and she may be dissatisfied with important relationships and uncertain about major life goals.
- **Social withdrawal and unconventional presentation** — In the **Tianyu Wang case**, others are likely to see her as withdrawn, aloof, and somewhat unconventional — a feature not typically associated with frank clinical impairment but reflecting a distinct interpersonal style.
- **Absence of psychopathology** — A within-normal-limits or subclinical profile indicates no significant problems in any of the above areas, as illustrated by Anna Finkel (npsych230202), Alexandra Stanley (npsych230406), and Elea Zhong (npsych230518). The **Ed Gonzalez case** presents a profile focused narrowly on alcohol-related concerns, with self-reported absence of significant problems in all other clinical domains. The **Tianyu Wang case** similarly reports no significant problems across antisocial behavior, anxiety, depression, somatic functioning, or substance use — with the moderate mood and interpersonal features as the only areas of clinical note. The **Alexander Jacobs case** presents a fully within-normal-limits profile with no significant psychopathology identified in any clinical domain — no unusual thoughts, no mood disturbance, no anxiety, no somatic concerns, and no substance use problems.

## Self-Concept and Interpersonal Functioning

The PAI generates interpretive hypotheses about self-concept stability and interpersonal style:

- **Self-concept** may be poorly established with significant fluctuations — oscillating between severe self-doubt and relative self-confidence, with fragile self-esteem highly reactive to interpersonal slights. The Eric Roizman case presents a particularly unstable self-concept: fluctuating between states of very poor self-esteem and severe self-doubt to periods of exaggerated confidence and overvalued accomplishments, with episodes of positive self-esteem appearing defensive in response to feelings of emptiness and lack of purpose. His self-esteem plummets in response to slights or oversights by others, and associated instability extends to identity and attitudes about major life issues — illustrating the [[concepts/unstable-self-concept]] pattern at a clinical extreme. In some presentations, self-concept is characterized by a generally harsh, negative self-evaluation. In the Krishan Patel case, self-concept shows a mixed pattern: oscillating between pessimism/self-doubt and self-confidence, with stress-reactive self-criticism and indecisiveness, but fluctuations not extreme by clinical standards. In the **Tianyu Wang case**, self-concept similarly involves both positive and negative aspects — oscillating between pessimism and self-doubt to relative self-confidence, with fluctuations within normal adult range and stress-reactive self-criticism, uncertainty, and indecisiveness. In the **Elea Zhong case**, self-concept involves a generally positive self-evaluation — confident, resilient, and optimistic — but with stress-reactive self-esteem that may produce self-doubt, uncertainty about goals, and indecisiveness during difficult periods, even if this is not readily apparent to others. The **Alexander Jacobs case** also presents a self-concept with both positive and negative aspects — self-evaluation that varies between pessimism and self-doubt to relative self-confidence, with fluctuations comparable to most adults and stress-reactive self-criticism, uncertainty, and indecisiveness. These fluctuations are not extreme and do not suggest clinically significant identity disturbance. Conversely, within-normal-limits profiles (e.g., Alexandra Stanley) reflect a generally stable and positive self-evaluation without notable reactivity. The **Ed Gonzalez case** also presents a generally stable and positive self-evaluation — confident and optimistic with a clear sense of purpose and distinct convictions, resilient and adaptive in the face of most stressors, and reasonably self-satisfied. This stands in contrast to profiles with prominent borderline or depressive features. See also [[concepts/identity-disturbance-clinical-features]].
- **Interpersonal style** is characterized along dominance (DOM) and warmth (WRM) dimensions. The Krishan Patel case presents with strong needs for affiliation and positive regard, resulting in uninhibited, attention-seeking, and dramatic social behavior. The **Tianyu Wang case** presents an interpersonal style characterized by autonomy and balance — assertiveness, friendliness, and concern for others all in the average range, typical of normal adults. The **Alexander Jacobs case** similarly presents an interpersonal style characterized by autonomy and balance — with assertiveness, friendliness, and concern for others in the average range, typical of normal adults. The **Elea Zhong case** presents an open, genuine, conforming, and unassuming style — preferring to avoid leadership, most comfortable in the background, warm and quiet, seen as eager to please, but reasonably effective interpersonally. The Eric Roizman case presents a socially uncomfortable, passive, and avoidant interpersonal style that leads to social isolation. The **Ed Gonzalez case** presents very strong needs for attention and affiliation that may result in him being perceived as controlling — seeking opportunities to interact with others as long as he can maintain some control over the relationship, potentially perceived as interfering by those around him. Passive/submissive profiles exhibit very little interest in social interaction. Modest/retiring profiles (e.g., Anna Finkel) are self-conscious in social interactions, passive, humble, and unassuming. Distant/reserved profiles (e.g., Alexandra Stanley) place low premium on close relationships, value independence, and appear reserved or aloof but not necessarily distressed. Balanced/average interpersonal profiles (e.g., Gorety Nguyen, Tianyu Wang, Alexander Jacobs) reflect typical assertiveness, friendliness, and concern for others.
- **Social support** availability is assessed; perceived nonsupport heightens risk and complicates treatment. In the Krishan Patel case, both recent stress level and perceived social support were approximately average. In the **Tianyu Wang case**, perceived social support is low — family relationships are described as somewhat distant and friends are seen as not reliably available when needed; despite this, she views her environment as reasonably stable and predictable with relatively little stress. In the **Elea Zhong case**, perceived social support is somewhat below average. The **Ed Gonzalez case** presents a favorable combination of low stress and a **large social support network** — a positive prognostic indicator for future adjustment. The **Alexander Jacobs case** similarly presents a favorable combination of low stress and a **large social support network**, with the combination of a highly developed system of social supports and a reasonably low stress environment noted as a favorable prognostic sign for future adjustment. Notably, the Eric Roizman case demonstrates an important clinical contrast: despite his socially avoidant style, he possesses a committed social support system that serves as a **protective factor and a positive indicator for future adjustment**.
- **Social avoidance and withdrawal** — See [[concepts/social-avoidance-and-withdrawal]]. In presentations marked by social discomfort and passivity, the client avoids most social interactions rather than risk being forced into active relational commitment. In the **Tianyu Wang case**, withdrawal and aloofness are perceived by others without clinical-level social dysfunction.

## Treatment Considerations

The PAI Plus report addresses three treatment domains:

1. **Potential treatment complications** — Anger management issues, verbal aggression, intimidating behavior; risk exacerbated by psychotic symptoms, agitation, affective lability. Social support deficits give the therapeutic relationship special significance. Clients may be too disorganized or overwhelmed to participate meaningfully in some treatment modalities — explicitly noted in both the Gorety Nguyen and Krishan Patel cases. Defensiveness and reluctance to discuss personal problems increases risk of early termination — this is specifically noted in the **Elea Zhong case** and also in the **Alexander Jacobs case**, where he may be somewhat defensive and reluctant to discuss personal problems, placing him at risk for early termination even without marked clinical psychopathology. Emotional constriction and resistance to psychological explanations for somatic problems are additional complications in specific presentations. In the **Tianyu Wang case**, difficulty with appropriate anger expression is noted — she describes herself as very meek and unassertive, with difficulty standing up for herself even when assertiveness is warranted. Additionally, limited social support gives special significance to the therapeutic relationship, and any impasse may need to be handled with particular care. The **Alexander Jacobs case** similarly notes difficulty with appropriate anger expression — he describes himself as very meek and unassertive, with difficulty standing up for himself even when assertiveness is warranted.
2. **Critical intervention needs** — The Eric Roizman case exemplifies the highest-priority treatment concern: **immediate suicide intervention** is indicated due to recurrent ideation and associated risk factors. Critical items flagged for self-harm and aggression underscore acute risk. Despite this, he shows good anger control and high motivation for change — positive indicators even in a high-acuity context.
3. **Motivation for treatment** — Treatment Process Index (high scores indicate engagement); RXR (low scores indicate resistance). Motivation above average for treatment settings is observed in the Krishan Patel case (TPI T=70), the Gorety Nguyen case (TPI T=65), and is also noted as elevated in the Eric Roizman case. **Motivation below average for clinical populations** is observed in the **Tianyu Wang case** (TPI T=55, comparable to adults not in treatment; below typical clinical levels — satisfied with herself, not experiencing marked distress, sees little need for change), the Elea Zhong case (TPI T=44), and the **Alexander Jacobs case** (TPI T=44 — substantially lower than typical of individuals being seen in treatment settings; satisfied with himself, not experiencing marked distress, sees little need for changes in his behavior). In the Ed Gonzalez case, treatment motivation is similarly limited by self-satisfaction, low distress, and perceived lack of need for change — even though he acknowledges alcohol-related problems. Despite low motivation, the **Tianyu Wang case**, **Alexander Jacobs case**, and the **Ed Gonzalez case** all possess identifiable strengths suggesting potential for a smooth treatment process if commitment were made.
4. **Treatment process challenges** — Trust difficulties, disorganization, somatic focus, authority conflicts, emotional constriction, reliance on therapeutic relationship as primary support, low energy and passivity impeding engagement. In manic-predominant profiles (e.g., Krishan Patel), anticipated treatment is described as fairly challenging with a difficult treatment process and probability of reversals. In antisocial-predominant profiles, the challenging treatment process similarly includes probability of reversals even when motivation is above average. The Eric Roizman case presents multiple clinical barriers to therapy: defensiveness, trust issues, emotional disorganization, resistance to psychological treatment, and emotional constriction — reflecting a difficult treatment process despite high motivation.

The combination of adequate treatment motivation with an RXR Estimated Score notably higher than the actual RXR indicates a patient who may be more resistant to change in practice than their stated attitudes suggest — a clinically important discrepancy to monitor. The **Elea Zhong case**, **Tianyu Wang case**, and **Alexander Jacobs case** all show the reverse pattern: RXR Estimated Score lower than actual RXR, suggesting stated openness to treatment is limited, with low distress and self-satisfaction further constraining practical treatment engagement. See also [[concepts/treatment-motivation-and-compliance]].

## DSM-5 Diagnostic Output

The PAI Plus report generates structured DSM-5 diagnostic hypotheses in two tiers:
- **Diagnostic Considerations** — Conditions most consistent with the profile configuration
- **Rule Out** — Conditions warranting further evaluation

Diagnostic hypotheses are advanced as possibilities, not conclusions, and must be integrated with all available clinical information. The report provides both DSM-5 and ICD-10 codes.

For within-normal-limits and mildly defensive profiles (e.g., Alexandra Stanley, Anna Finkel, **Alexander Jacobs**), the diagnostic output is simply **"No diagnosis"** — confirming the absence of clinically significant psychopathology.

### Diagnostic Patterns Across Case Examples

| Primary Consideration | Rule-Outs Observed |
|---|---|
| No diagnosis | — |
| Persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia) | Major depressive disorder, schizophrenia, other specified personality disorder (passive-aggressive) |
| Alcohol use disorder, mild; panic disorder | Bipolar II, cyclothymic disorder, GAD, OCD, PTSD, relational problems |
| Alcohol use disorder, mild; persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia) | Antisocial personality disorder, major depressive disorder, single episode |
| **Alcohol use disorder, severe** | — |
| PTSD | Schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, somatic symptom disorder, conversion disorder, neurocognitive disorder |
| Panic disorder (Diagnostic Consideration) | Bipolar I disorder manic, borderline personality disorder (Rule Out) |
| Diagnosis deferred | Generalized anxiety disorder, other specified personality disorder with mixed borderline features |
| Relational problems (Diagnostic Consideration) | Bipolar I disorder manic, unspecified personality disorder with borderline features (Rule Out) |

The **Krishan Patel case (npsych220428)** illustrates a profile where panic disorder is the primary diagnostic consideration, with bipolar I disorder (current or most recent episode manic, unspecified; DSM-5 296.40/ICD-10 F31.9) and borderline personality disorder (DSM-5 301.83/ICD-10 F60.3) as rule-outs — consistent with the high manic symptom severity, marked impulsivity, and paranoid ideation.

The Gorety Nguyen case (npsych230316) illustrates a profile where alcohol use disorder (mild) and persistent depressive disorder (dysthymia) are the primary diagnostic considerations, with antisocial personality disorder and major depressive disorder as rule-outs — consistent with the ANT primary elevation and mild depressive features.

The **Tianyu Wang case (npsych220505)** illustrates a subclinical profile where **relational problems (V61.10/Z63.0)** is the diagnostic consideration, with bipolar I disorder, current or most recent episode manic, unspecified (296.40/F31.9) and unspecified personality disorder with borderline features (301.9/F60.9) as rule-outs — consistent with moderate mood variability, moodiness, interpersonal dissatisfaction, and the absence of frank clinical psychopathology.

The **Elea Zhong case (npsych230518)** illustrates a profile where the diagnosis is **deferred** and the only diagnostic output is rule-outs: generalized anxiety disorder (300.02/F41.1) and other specified personality disorder with mixed borderline features (301.89/F60.89). This represents the mildest end of the diagnostic output spectrum — a subclinical presentation with no frank diagnosis but some features warranting monitoring.

The **Eric Roizman case (ER24)** illustrates a high-acuity, multi-diagnostic presentation. DSM-5 diagnostic possibilities span mood disorders (major depressive disorder, bipolar spectrum), anxiety disorders, substance use disorders (alcohol dependence, polysubstance dependence), personality disorders, and trauma-related conditions — consistent with the breadth of clinical scale elevations and critical item endorsements.

The **Ed Gonzalez case (EG24)** illustrates a diagnostically focused, single-diagnosis profile: **Alcohol use disorder, severe (DSM-5 303.90/ICD-10 F10.20)**. This is one of the simplest diagnostic outputs across all documented cases — a single primary diagnosis with no rule-outs — consistent with a profile in which the ALC scale is the sole significant clinical elevation and all other scales are within normal limits.

The **Alexander Jacobs case (AlJac, age 32)** illustrates a fully within-normal-limits profile with **no diagnosis** — the simplest diagnostic output, alongside other healthy/non-clinical profiles such as Alexandra Stanley and Anna Finkel. Despite the presence of a few critical item endorsements (ARD-T items and ALC #334), no diagnosable psychopathology is identified.

The mix of "rule out" diagnoses is sensitive to scale configuration: somatic-predominant profiles trigger somatic symptom disorder and conversion disorder as rule-outs; cognitive/perceptual disturbance profiles trigger schizophrenia and schizoaffective disorder; manic presentations trigger bipolar I and borderline personality disorder as rule-outs; low-acuity anxiety presentations trigger GAD and mixed borderline features as rule-outs; subclinical profiles with mood variability and poor social support trigger relational problems as a primary consideration with bipolar I and unspecified personality disorder as rule-outs.

## Clinical Applications

- **Diagnosis and differential diagnosis** (DSM-5 diagnostic hypotheses generated from scale configurations)
- **Treatment planning** (motivation, resistance, therapeutic relationship considerations)
- **Forensic and medicolegal assessment** (motor vehicle accident claimants, custody evaluations, law enforcement screening) — the Eric Roizman case includes a Motor Vehicle Accident Claimant Overlay, suggesting a medicolegal context; the Ed Gonzalez case also uses a Motor Vehicle Accident Claimant Overlay
- **Risk assessment** (suicide, violence, aggression) — see [[concepts/suicide-and-violence-risk-indices]]
- **Personality disorder evaluation** (including Alternative Model for Personality Disorders)
- **Healthy population screening** (the within-normal-limits and subclinical profiles typify psychologically healthy or minimally distressed adults presenting for non-clinical evaluation contexts)

The PAI is commonly used in contexts described by [[concepts/forensic-neuropsychological-evaluation]] and [[concepts/clinical-pdf-assessment]].

## Use in the Luria/Neuropsych Pipeline

Within the neuropsychological assessment automation ecosystem documented in this wiki, PAI reports are ingested as PDFs and processed to extract clinical scale scores, validity indicators, and narrative content. See [[concepts/pai-assessment]] and [[concepts/pai-knowledge-base]] for pipeline-specific details, [[concepts/pdf-score-extraction]] for score extraction methodology, and [[summaries/pai_00]], [[summaries/pai_01]], [[summaries/pai_02]], and [[summaries/pai_03]] for full example clinical report summaries spanning a range of clinical severity and presentation type.

Additional case examples spanning somatic presentations, young adult profiles, mixed validity patterns, and depressive/thought-disturbance presentations are documented in [[summaries/pai_04]], [[summaries/pai_05]], [[summaries/pai_06]], [[summaries/pai_07]], [[summaries/pai_09]], [[summaries/pai_10]], [[summaries/pai_100]], [[summaries/pai_102]], [[summaries/pai_11]], and [[summaries/pai_13]].

The within-normal-limits case (Alexandra Stanley, npsych230406) is documented in [[summaries/pai_14]], illustrating how a healthy adult presentation with no psychopathology is characterized across all PAI domains.

The mildly defensive, within-normal-limits case (Anna Finkel, npsych230202) is documented in [[summaries/pai_18]], illustrating a college-aged profile with mild positive impression management, entirely normal clinical scales, no DSM-5 diagnosis, and below-average treatment motivation.

The antisocial-predominant case (Gorety Nguyen, npsych230316, age 23) is documented in [[summaries/pai_23]], illustrating a profile with primary ANT elevation, co-occurring alcohol and drug use concerns, thought disorganization, mild depressive features, negative self-evaluation, and above-average treatment motivation complicated by authority conflicts and disorganization. The Suicide Potential Index elevation (T=74) in the absence of reported suicidal ideation is a notable clinical pattern in this case.

The **manic-predominant case (Krishan Patel, npsych220428, age 21)** is documented in [[summaries/pai_30]], illustrating a profile with severe manic features uncommon even in clinical samples, marked impulsivity, paranoid ideation, pronounced attentional difficulties (Inattention T=99), high reactive and instrumental aggression, substantial ALC/DRG estimated score discrepancies suggesting substance use denial, and DSM-5 considerations of panic disorder with rule-out of bipolar I disorder and borderline personality disorder.

The **subclinical, low-acuity case (Elea Zhong, npsych230518, age 20)** is documented in [[summaries/pai_312]], illustrating a profile with no marked clinical elevations, moderate anxiety within normal limits, a deferred diagnosis with GAD and mixed borderline features as rule-outs, low treatment motivation, possible substance use minimization, mildly elevated Inattention Index, and best context-specific fit with college students (0.310). This case anchors the low-distress end of the documented case spectrum.

The **high-acuity, complex case (Eric Roizman, ER24, age 55)** is documented in [[summaries/pai_316]], illustrating a multiply impaired profile with severe polysubstance and alcohol dependence, major depressive symptoms, extreme anxiety with phobias and somatic preoccupation, manic/hypomanic features, antisocial tendencies, trauma history, cognitive confusion, social isolation, paranoid mistrust, and acute suicide risk — alongside a valid protocol, high treatment motivation, good anger control, and a committed social support network as protective factors. The Motor Vehicle Accident Claimant Overlay situates this case in a medicolegal context.

The **alcohol-focused, older adult case (Ed Gonzalez, EG24, age 62)** is documented in [[summaries/pai_317]], illustrating a profile where the ALC scale is the sole significant clinical elevation, all other clinical domains are within normal limits, mild positive impression management is present but does not invalidate results, and the profile fits healthy screening populations more than clinical groups. This case represents the narrowest diagnostic output (single diagnosis: alcohol use disorder, severe) in the documented series, and the largest reverse ALC Estimated Score discrepancy.

The **subclinical mood-variable case (Tianyu Wang, npsych220505, age 24)** is documented in [[summaries/pai_40]], illustrating a female college-aged profile with no marked clinical elevations, moderate mood variability and manic-related features, notable ALC/DRG estimated score discrepancies (15T and 13T) suggesting possible substance use denial, balanced interpersonal style, perceived low social support, low treatment motivation, a relational problems diagnostic consideration with bipolar I and unspecified personality disorder as rule-outs, and the sole critical item being a sleep disturbance indicator. This case extends the documented range of subclinical presentations while adding a distinctive pattern of substance use denial without global impression management distortion.

The **within-normal-limits adult male case (Alexander Jacobs, AlJac, age 32)** is documented in [[summaries/pai_45]], illustrating a fully within-normal-limits profile with no diagnosable psychopathology, statistically significant context-specific fit with college students (0.537), no significant clinical elevations, low treatment motivation (RXR Estimated Score 11T lower than actual RXR), mild anger expression difficulty, a favorable social support environment, and three critical item endorsements (ARD-T traumatic stress items and ALC #334) warranting clinical follow-up despite the overall benign profile.
