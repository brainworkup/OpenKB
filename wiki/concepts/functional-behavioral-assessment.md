# Functional Behavioral Assessment (FBA)

A **Functional Behavioral Assessment (FBA)** is a structured, systematic process used to understand the nature and purpose of a child's problem behavior. Rather than simply describing *what* a child does, an FBA investigates *why* the behavior occurs — identifying the setting events, antecedents, and consequences that maintain it. This understanding drives the selection of targeted, individualized interventions.

FBAs are a core component of evidence-based behavioral intervention, particularly in school and clinical neuropsychological settings. They are explicitly referenced in treatment planning for both aggression and [[concepts/attention-intervention-strategies]], where antecedent instructional modifications are selected based on functional assessment data.

## Purpose

The primary goal of an FBA is to:

- Operationally define the problem behavior
- Identify environmental triggers (antecedents) and maintaining factors (consequences)
- Hypothesize the **function** of the behavior — the underlying need it serves
- Guide the selection of functionally equivalent replacement behaviors or antecedent modifications

## Common Functions of Problem Behavior

Behaviors typically serve one or more of the following functions:

| Function | Description |
|---|---|
| **Escape/Avoidance** | The child avoids a task, person, or situation |
| **Tangible Reward** | The child gains access to a desired object or activity |
| **Attention** | The child obtains social attention from peers or adults |
| **Stimulation** | The behavior is internally reinforcing (sensory feedback) |

## FBA in the Context of Aggression

In neuropsychological treatment planning for aggression, the FBA is a required precursor to **Replacement Behavior Training**. According to [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.aggression]], the FBA process involves:

1. Creating an operational definition of the aggressive behavior
2. Identifying setting events, antecedents, and consequences
3. Hypothesizing the function of the behavior
4. Testing the hypothesis by introducing the antecedent in a controlled way
5. Using the results to select a replacement behavior that serves the **same function** as the aggressive behavior

The replacement behavior must be:
- As easy for the child to perform as the problem behavior
- Capable of producing the same outcome (same reinforcement)
- Socially acceptable

## FBA in the Context of Attention Problems

In the treatment of attention problems, the FBA serves as the data-gathering foundation for **Modified Task-Presentation Strategies** — one of two primary intervention frameworks described in [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.attention-problems]]. Specifically:

- Assessment or observation data from the FBA determines which modification strategies best fit the instructor, the child, and the lesson content.
- The FBA identifies *when, where, and how* typical instruction differs from what the child needs.
- Based on this functional understanding, tasks are altered using **antecedent instructional modifications**, which may include:
  1. Offering a choice of instructional activities
  2. Providing guided notes and instruction in attending to relevant information
  3. Using high-interest activities and hands-on demonstrations
  4. Modifying in-class assignments and responses
  5. Modifying homework
  6. Highlighting relevant material with colors, symbols, or font changes

The FBA-driven observational process continues through the evaluation phase, where the clinician or teacher determines which modifications have the greatest positive impact on the child's attention and performance.

## FBA Process Steps (Clinical Procedure)

### Preparation
- Assess the child's cognitive and developmental readiness for participation
- Operationally define the target behavior in observable, measurable terms
- Assign responsibility for data collection
- Identify who will teach and monitor the replacement behavior or implement modifications

### Assessment
- Gather direct and indirect data (observation, interviews, rating scales)
- Identify patterns in the A-B-C sequence: **Antecedent → Behavior → Consequence**
- Generate a formal hypothesis about the behavior's function

### Hypothesis Testing
- Introduce the identified antecedent in a structured context
- Confirm or revise the functional hypothesis based on behavioral response

### Intervention Planning
- For aggression: select a functionally equivalent replacement behavior with immediate reinforcement and gradual fading
- For attention problems: select antecedent instructional modifications tailored to the identified functional triggers; continue those that are effective, discontinue those that are not

## FBA as Part of a Broader Intervention Ecosystem

The FBA is not an isolated procedure — it anchors a sequence of behavioral supports:

- [[concepts/behavior-modification]] — general principles of shaping behavior through reinforcement
- [[concepts/aggression-interventions]] — specific strategies for aggression, including Replacement Behavior Training and Problem-Solving Training
- [[concepts/attention-intervention-strategies]] — classroom and home strategies for attention regulation, including Modified Task-Presentation Strategies driven by FBA data
- [[concepts/iep-accommodations]] — FBA findings commonly inform IEP development in school settings
- [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]] — many children requiring FBAs show co-occurring deficits in self-regulation and planning
- [[concepts/self-monitoring-behavior]] — often co-implemented with FBA-based interventions to build metacognitive awareness

## Key References

- The **BASC-3 Behavior Intervention Guide** (referenced in both [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.aggression]] and [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.attention-problems]]) provides detailed guidance on conducting FBAs, with specific application to aggression treatment planning (Chapters 4 and 10) and Modified Task-Presentation Strategies for attention problems.

## Summary

The FBA is essential infrastructure for individualized behavioral intervention. By identifying *why* a behavior occurs — not just *what* it looks like — clinicians can design replacement strategies or antecedent modifications that are more durable, more ethical, and more likely to generalize. It is critical in planning [[concepts/aggression-interventions]], forms the empirical backbone of Modified Task-Presentation Strategies for [[concepts/attention-intervention-strategies]], and is a cornerstone of evidence-based practice in pediatric neuropsychology and special education.
