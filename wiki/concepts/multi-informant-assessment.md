# Multi-Informant Assessment

Multi-informant assessment is the practice of gathering and integrating reports from multiple observers—such as patients, parents, teachers, and clinicians—to evaluate psychological, behavioral, and cognitive functioning across contexts. In [[summaries/clinical-assessment]], it is presented as a core strategy for improving clinical interpretation when symptoms vary by setting and when any single source provides an incomplete picture.

## Why It Matters

Many clinically relevant behaviors are context dependent. A person may show difficulties in one environment but not another, or different observers may notice different aspects of the same problem. This is especially important in child and adolescent assessment, but it also applies more broadly in neuropsychological and psychological evaluation.

Multi-informant assessment helps clinicians:
- capture behavior across home, school, work, and clinic settings
- distinguish stable traits from situational responses
- identify discrepancies that may be clinically meaningful
- improve treatment planning by linking interventions to the contexts where problems occur

This makes the concept closely related to [[concepts/cross-informant-correspondence]], [[concepts/neuropsychological-assessment-workflow]], and [[concepts/trauma-informed-clinical-assessment]].

## Core Principle

The central idea is that disagreement among informants should not automatically be treated as error. As summarized in [[summaries/clinical-assessment]], low agreement can reflect real differences in how symptoms appear across situations, rather than simple unreliability.

A key supporting source discussed there is nihms658713, which reports that cross-informant agreement is often modest overall, with stronger agreement for externalizing problems than for internalizing problems. This finding supports the need for interpretation frameworks that preserve contextual meaning rather than collapsing reports into a single undifferentiated score.

## Operations Triad Model

[[summaries/clinical-assessment]] highlights the Operations Triad Model as a useful framework for understanding informant patterns:

1. **Converging operations** — multiple informants agree, suggesting the behavior is relatively consistent across settings
2. **Diverging operations** — informants disagree because functioning genuinely differs by context
3. **Compensating operations** — discrepancies arise from methodological artifacts, rater bias, or measurement limitations

This framework aligns naturally with [[concepts/cross-informant-correspondence]] because it treats agreement and disagreement as data to be interpreted, not just problems to eliminate.

## Clinical Interpretation

In practice, multi-informant assessment is most useful when clinicians ask what each informant uniquely contributes. According to [[summaries/clinical-assessment]], additional informants are justified when they add unique, context-specific information rather than redundant data.

Important interpretive questions include:
- Which settings show the problem most clearly?
- Are discrepancies consistent with developmental or environmental demands?
- Do differences suggest variable symptom expression, observer bias, or measurement issues?
- Does each informant contribute incremental validity?

This interpretive stance connects with [[concepts/behavioral-rating-scales]], [[concepts/clinical-guidelines]], and [[concepts/neurodevelopmental-clinical-intake]].

## Validity Considerations

The source summary emphasizes several cautions:
- agreement does not necessarily indicate greater severity
- low agreement does not necessarily indicate poor data quality
- rater bias may contribute, but often does not fully explain discrepancies
- measurement error can be transient, random, or systematic
- criterion contamination can inflate apparent validity in research designs

These issues make multi-informant work closely related to [[concepts/validity-and-response-styles]] and structured assessment practices in [[concepts/clinical-data-management]].

## Use in Attention and Neuropsychological Assessment

[[summaries/clinical-assessment]] places special emphasis on attention problems. Attention difficulties often vary across settings and may be longstanding yet only come to formal attention after a triggering event. In such cases, multi-informant assessment helps distinguish:
- lifelong versus acquired difficulties
- school-only, home-only, or pervasive attentional problems
- primary attention problems versus effects of anxiety, mood, or neurological factors

This links the concept to [[concepts/premorbid-vs-acquired-attention-difficulties]], [[concepts/developmental-vs-acquired-cognitive-symptoms]], [[concepts/executive-function-deficits]], [[concepts/working-memory]], and [[concepts/neurobehavioral-status-exam]].

## Best Practices

Based on [[summaries/clinical-assessment]], strong multi-informant practice includes:
- personalizing assessment to the contexts in which symptoms occur
- forming hypotheses in advance about likely convergence and divergence
- using interviews and independent observations to test those hypotheses
- selecting measures that fit the referral question and setting
- integrating results probabilistically rather than relying on any single report

These practices also support broader workflows in [[concepts/neuropsychological-assessment-pipeline]], [[concepts/neuropsychological-assessment-automation]], and [[concepts/clinical-ai-reasoning]] when assessment data are being organized or synthesized computationally.

## Relationship to Clinical Assessment

Within the wiki, multi-informant assessment is best understood as a foundational method inside [[summaries/clinical-assessment]]. Clinical assessment provides the broader framework of standardized evaluation; multi-informant assessment specifies how clinicians handle context-sensitive, discrepant, and complementary reports from different observers.
