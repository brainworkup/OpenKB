# Application Question Tracking

## Overview
Application question tracking is the practice of maintaining a clear inventory of unanswered, in-progress, and completed application prompts so founders can see remaining work at a glance. In this wiki, the concept is illustrated by [[summaries/YC Application Questions_20260413233900]] and [[summaries/YC Application Questions_20260416091345]], both of which identify the outstanding questions in a YC application and group them by section.

## Why It Matters
For startup applications, progress is often blocked not by lack of ideas but by incomplete coverage across multiple categories such as progress, market, and legal status. Question tracking helps:
- prevent missed required fields
- reveal which sections need the most work
- separate required questions from optional materials
- support planning and sequencing for submission

This makes application work more operational and less ad hoc, especially when paired with [[concepts/application-preparation]] and [[concepts/application-strategy]].

## Core Structure
A useful application question tracker typically organizes prompts by:
- section
- required vs. optional status
- completion state
- dependencies on other materials

In the source examples, the unanswered questions are grouped into three main required sections plus two optional items. The repeated structure across both source summaries shows that good tracking is not just a list of prompts, but a way to translate application ambiguity into a stable checklist with counts, categories, and clear next actions.

## Example From the Source Documents
[[summaries/YC Application Questions_20260413233900]] and [[summaries/YC Application Questions_20260416091345]] both record a count of remaining YC application questions based on review of application workspace materials.

### Progress Section
The sources identify 4 unanswered questions:
- How far along are you?
- How long have each of you been working on this, and how much of that has been full-time?
- Are people using your product?
- What tech stack are you using, or planning to use, to build this product?

These questions connect operational status and product maturity to broader founder evaluation themes such as [[concepts/founder-track-record]] and [[concepts/founder-evaluation]].

### Strategy and Market Section
The sources identify 4 grouped questions in this section, covering:
- why the founders chose the idea
- domain expertise
- evidence of user need
- competitors and differentiation
- business model and revenue potential
- company category

This area overlaps strongly with [[concepts/application-strategy]], [[concepts/competitive-landscape-analysis]], [[concepts/startup-differentiation]], and [[concepts/market-sizing]].

### Legal Section
The sources identify 3 unanswered legal-status questions:
- whether any legal entity has been formed
- whether any investment has been taken
- whether fundraising is currently underway

These questions are closely related to [[concepts/startup-fundraising]].

### Optional Items
The sources also list 2 optional items:
- coding agent session attachment
- other ideas considered for applying

These optional materials can still influence the overall strength and clarity of an application, particularly when they reinforce execution ability or decision quality. The coding session attachment also relates to [[concepts/coding-agent-session]].

## Key Takeaway From the Sources
The main tracking result repeated across the source documents is:
- 11 required unanswered questions
- 2 optional items

This turns a vague sense of incomplete work into a concrete checklist. It also shows the value of preserving question-tracking snapshots over time: even when the answer is similar, repeated reviews confirm the current state of the application and reinforce a shared understanding of what remains. That makes it easier to allocate effort, assign drafting tasks, and monitor readiness for submission.

## Practical Use
Application question tracking is most effective when used as a lightweight planning layer over application materials. A strong tracker should:
1. list every prompt exactly once
2. group prompts into meaningful sections
3. distinguish required from optional items
4. show what remains unanswered
5. link prompts to supporting evidence or draft materials

In practice, this concept supports [[concepts/startup-application-materials]], [[concepts/knowledge-capture]], and [[concepts/decision-logging]] by ensuring application work is visible and auditable over time. The newer source also reinforces that tracking often depends on comparing a checklist file with the full application structure, making the tracker a bridge between planning artifacts and final submission materials.
