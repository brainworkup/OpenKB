# Dialogic Reading

Dialogic reading is an evidence-based shared reading intervention designed to build early literacy, vocabulary, and language comprehension skills in children. Unlike passive read-aloud approaches, dialogic reading transforms the reading experience into an interactive dialogue between the adult and child, positioning the child as an active storyteller rather than a passive listener.

## Core Principles

- **Adult as questioner:** Adults ask the child specific, targeted questions before, during, and after reading to encourage engagement.
- **Child as narrator:** The child is progressively encouraged to retell, predict, and comment on the story.
- **Scaffolded interaction:** Adults provide prompts, expansions, and positive reinforcement to extend the child's verbal contributions.
- **Repeated reading:** Books are read multiple times, with each reading deepening comprehension and vocabulary exposure.

## Why It Works

Dialogic reading leverages the natural context of shared reading to stimulate language production. By requiring the child to actively respond to questions rather than simply listen, it:

- Increases expressive vocabulary through repeated, contextual word exposure.
- Builds narrative comprehension and story structure awareness.
- Develops analogical and inferential thinking as children answer open-ended questions.
- Strengthens conversational turn-taking and verbal expression skills.

## Application in Clinical Recommendations

### Verbal and Language Development

In neuropsychological recommendations for children with verbal skill deficits, dialogic reading is suggested as a home and school strategy. Specifically, adults are encouraged to:

1. Ask the child **specific questions** during reading to prompt interest and comprehension.
2. Use **open-ended follow-up questions** rather than yes/no prompts.
3. Provide **positive feedback** when the child contributes verbally, encouraging elaboration.
4. Read stories **multiple times**, varying characters, actions, or sequences across readings so the child must identify and describe changes.
5. Ask the child to **complete incomplete stories** or participate in story dramatization.

See [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.building-verbal-skills]] for the full recommendation context.

### Dyslexia and Early Literacy

In reports for children with [[concepts/dyslexia]], dialogic reading is recommended specifically to build early literacy and vocabulary skills. The interactive questioning format is noted as valuable for encouraging comprehension and enjoyment of reading — two outcomes that are especially important when a child is struggling to decode text. For a 7-year-old with provisional dyslexia, shared reading with parent involvement (at least 15 minutes daily) is recommended as part of a broader home literacy routine, with children selecting books at a mix of easy, "just right," and challenging levels, and adults and children taking turns reading aloud.

Key features in this dyslexia context include:
- **Parent reads aloud daily** to support language development and reading conceptualization.
- **Child selects books**, maintaining interest and autonomy.
- **Turn-taking** in reading reinforces how words sound and the importance of pace and expression.
- Adults use dialogic prompts to check comprehension and build vocabulary alongside phonics-based interventions such as Orton-Gillingham or Lindamood-Bell.

See [[summaries/bwu.neuro.reports.recs.dyslexia]] for the full clinical recommendation context.

## Integration with Other Interventions

Dialogic reading does not stand alone; it complements a multifaceted remediation plan:

- It pairs naturally with **phonics instruction** by exposing children to connected text while they build decoding skills.
- It supports [[concepts/phonological-processing]] by giving children repeated exposure to the sound structure of words in a meaningful context.
- It reinforces **orthographic awareness** when adults point to words during reading, mapping printed letters to spoken sounds.
- For children with co-occurring ADHD, the engaging, interactive format helps sustain attention and motivation during literacy activities (see [[concepts/attention-intervention-strategies]]).
- Story recall and change-detection tasks in dialogic reading engage [[concepts/working-memory]].

## Application to IEP and School Planning

Dialogic reading strategies may be incorporated into individualized education plans for eligible students (see [[concepts/iep-accommodations]]). Teachers can use dialogic techniques to scaffold pre-reading discussions, activating prior knowledge before assigned readings. The strategy also informs classroom read-aloud practices for students with reading disorders, encouraging active participation rather than passive listening.

## Evidence Base

Dialogic reading was originally developed by Grover Whitehurst and colleagues in the late 1980s and has since accumulated a robust evidence base across preschool and early elementary populations. Meta-analyses consistently demonstrate significant positive effects on expressive vocabulary and print awareness, particularly for children from language-rich intervention environments and those with developmental language concerns. Its use in pediatric neuropsychological reports reflects broad clinical consensus that interactive shared reading accelerates language and literacy development, especially when combined with evidence-based phonics and fluency interventions.
