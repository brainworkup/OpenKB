# Suicide and Violence Risk Indices in Psychological Assessment

Suicide and violence risk indices are quantitative indicators derived from standardized psychological instruments that help clinicians estimate the likelihood of self-directed or other-directed harmful behavior. These indices are not standalone diagnostic tools but contribute systematically to clinical decision-making, risk stratification, and treatment planning.

## Role in Psychological Assessment

Risk indices translate patterns of item endorsement across a psychological test into numeric risk estimates, typically expressed as raw scores and T scores relative to normative samples. They are particularly important in:

- **Triage and level-of-care decisions** — determining whether a client requires intensive monitoring or intervention
- **Treatment planning** — identifying areas of focus for early stages of care
- **Documentation** — providing an objective, reproducible record of risk at the time of evaluation
- **Forensic contexts** — offering structured risk data in legal and disability settings

See [[concepts/clinical-report-structure]] and [[concepts/personality-assessment-inventory]] for related assessment frameworks.

## Suicide Risk Indices in the PAI

The Personality Assessment Inventory (PAI) includes several indices specifically designed to assess suicidal risk.

## Interpreting Elevated Risk Indices: Key Principles

1. **Validity must be established first:** [[summaries/pai_10]] is the definitive example in this case series of a profile where all risk index elevations cannot be clinically interpreted because the response pattern has been declared invalid. Validity determination is the necessary first step before any risk index interpretation.
2. **Context matters:** An elevated index is a probabilistic signal, not a diagnosis. It must be interpreted alongside self-report, clinical interview, history, and collateral sources.
3. **Convergence strengthens confidence:** When multiple indices (SPI, S_Chron, VPI, VARI), critical item endorsements, and coefficient-of-fit data all point toward risk, clinical concern is substantially higher than when only one indicator is elevated. [[summaries/pai_202]] exemplifies maximal convergence across all risk domains. [[summaries/pai_314]] (Tiffany Cha) exemplifies convergence across both the suicide domain and the violence domain. [[summaries/pai_62]] (Woody Chiang) illustrates a distinctive pattern of convergence: SPI 84 T, VPI 66 T, and VARI 72 T all elevated; all three AGG-P critical items endorsed; broad symptom behavior group fits leading with assault history (.815) — yet no SUI critical item endorsement and no self-reported self-harm distress, suggesting that the convergence is primarily in the aggression domain. [[summaries/pai_316]] (Eric Roizman) exemplifies convergence across multi-domain clinical severity.
4. **Discrepancies are also informative:** When objective indices are elevated but self-reported ideation or intent is absent, this may reflect denial, limited insight, or trait-level rather than state-level risk — as seen in [[summaries/pai_00]], [[summaries/pai_03]], [[summaries/pai_06]], [[summaries/pai_09]], [[summaries/pai_100]], [[summaries/pai_102]], [[summaries/pai_23]], [[summaries/pai_26]], [[summaries/pai_30]], [[summaries/pai_47]], and [[summaries/pai_62]]. In [[summaries/pai_04]], [[summaries/pai_11]], [[summaries/pai_17]], [[summaries/pai_314]], [[summaries/pai_316]], and [[summaries/pai_202]], objective indices and self-reported ideation converge, substantially strengthening concern.
5. **Mixed validity complicates risk interpretation:** [[summaries/pai_06]], [[summaries/pai_09]], [[summaries/pai_102]], [[summaries/pai_13]], [[summaries/pai_15]], [[summaries/pai_17]], and [[summaries/pai_202]] all present validity concerns that require interpretive caution. [[summaries/pai_10]] presents the extreme case: complete profile invalidity that prohibits any clinical inference from risk indices whatsoever. [[summaries/pai_62]] (Woody Chiang) presents subtle negative impression management concerns — not rising to the level of profile invalidity, but sufficient to suggest that clinical scale elevations may overrepresent pathology in certain areas. This occupies an intermediate interpretive position between the fully valid profiles (e.g., [[summaries/pai_26]], [[summaries/pai_199]], [[summaries/pai_30]]) and the invalid profile ([[summaries/pai_10]]). The clinical hypotheses for [[summaries/pai_62]] are reviewable but require this caveat to be held in mind throughout.
6. **Response set fit supersedes symptom behavior group fit:** In [[summaries/pai_10]], the extremely high fit with random responding and NIM means that any apparently meaningful fit with clinical symptom groups is an artifact of the response pattern. In [[summaries/pai_62]], the NIM predicted profile coefficient (.769) is the highest response set fit — consistent with the subtle negative impression management interpretation — and this moderates confidence in the very high symptom behavior group coefficients (e.g., assault history: .815).
7. **Profile-level fit can diverge from scale-level elevations:** Multiple cases illustrate statistically significant fits with auditory hallucinations and antipsychotic medications that coexist with explicit narrative exclusion of active psychosis. [[summaries/pai_62]] shows auditory hallucinations: .741 — a statistically significant and substantial coefficient — despite no mention of frank psychotic features in the clinical narrative. The thought confusion and distractibility described are consistent with cognitive disorganization rather than true psychosis, but the high coefficient warrants clinical inquiry.
8. **Personality features amplify risk:** Borderline personality traits (impulsivity, volatile relationships, identity disturbance, fear of abandonment) are well-established moderators that elevate both suicide and violence risk. See [[concepts/borderline-personality-disorder]]. [[summaries/pai_202]] has the highest diagnostic group coefficient of fit for borderline personality disorder (0.894) in the entire series; [[summaries/pai_314]] has the second highest (0.880). Manic features amplify aggression risk through irritability, impulsivity, and overcommitted, disorganized behavior. Antisocial personality traits amplify risk through recklessness, stimulation-seeking, and disregard for consequences. [[summaries/pai_62]] shows the highest diagnostic group fit with antisocial personality disorder (0.759) — making it a case where the primary personality risk context is antisocial rather than borderline, similar to [[summaries/pai_23]] and [[summaries/pai_47]].
9. **Internalizing profiles warrant equal vigilance:** [[summaries/pai_04]], [[summaries/pai_11]], [[summaries/pai_13]], [[summaries/pai_15]], [[summaries/pai_17]], and [[summaries/pai_199]] all present profiles with low or moderate broad aggression indices yet carry suicide signals. Low externalizing scores should not create false safety when internalizing risk indicators are elevated.
10. **A low-risk profile does not mean clinical irrelevance:** [[summaries/pai_05]] demonstrates that a PAI profile entirely within normal limits still yields important clinical hypotheses.
11. **Experimental indices require caution:** Indices marked with an asterisk (*) in the PAI have limited cross-validation research and should be interpreted conservatively.
12. **Treatment Process Index (TPI):** An elevated TPI signals the treatment course itself will likely be difficult. In [[summaries/pai_47]] (Lauren Hurlbut), TPI is 91 T — the highest in the entire series. In [[summaries/pai_202]], TPI is 86 T — the second highest. In [[summaries/pai_15]] and [[summaries/pai_314]], TPI is 81 T. In [[summaries/pai_62]], TPI is 76 T — substantially elevated, placing Woody Chiang in the upper tier for treatment process difficulty. In [[summaries/pai_17]] and [[summaries/pai_30]], TPI is 70 T. In [[summaries/pai_102]], TPI is 70 T. In [[summaries/pai_23]], TPI is 65 T. In [[summaries/pai_09]] and [[summaries/pai_06]], TPI is 65 T. In [[summaries/pai_13]], [[summaries/pai_26]], and [[summaries/pai_199]], TPI is 60 T. In [[summaries/pai_11]] and [[summaries/pai_100]], TPI is 44 T. [[summaries/pai_316]] presents a narrative-confirmed difficult treatment trajectory with multiple enumerated barriers.
13. **Acute vs. chronic risk distinction:** The pairing of SPI and S_Chron Index allows clinicians to differentiate situationally driven risk (high SPI, normal S_Chron, as in [[summaries/pai_03]], [[summaries/pai_04]], [[summaries/pai_100]], and [[summaries/pai_199]]) from chronic trait-level vulnerability (both indices extremely elevated, as in [[summaries/pai_01]], [[summaries/pai_202]], and [[summaries/pai_314]]). [[summaries/pai_62]] presents a distinctive pattern: SPI 84 T (acute, very high) paired with S_Chron 60 T (mild) — suggesting the index elevation is primarily situationally driven despite its magnitude. This contrasts sharply with [[summaries/pai_314]] (SPI 84 T, S_Chron 77 T), where the same acute score is paired with substantial chronic vulnerability.
14. **VPI-VARI divergence warrants interpretive caution:** When the composite VPI is elevated but the experimental VARI is within normal limits — as in [[summaries/pai_102]] and [[summaries/pai_15]] — the violence risk signal depends more heavily on the composite index. In [[summaries/pai_62]], the VARI (72 T) actually exceeds the VPI (66 T) — a concordant and convergent pattern where the experimental index amplifies rather than moderates clinical concern. This is comparable to [[summaries/pai_314]] (VARI 81 T, VPI 75 T) in structure.
15. **Manic presentation creates a distinctive risk profile:** [[summaries/pai_30]] and [[summaries/pai_47]] are the clearest manic-spectrum cases in the series. [[summaries/pai_47]] adds the dimension of prominent antisocial personality features co-occurring with the manic presentation. [[summaries/pai_62]] differs from both manic cases: the clinical profile is not characterized by mood elevation or grandiosity, but rather by emotional lability, tension, impulsivity, and antisocial character features — consistent with the borderline/antisocial risk configuration rather than the manic-antisocial configuration of [[summaries/pai_47]].
16. **Borderline personality disorder creates a convergent multi-domain risk profile:** [[summaries/pai_202]] and [[summaries/pai_314]] represent the two cases with the highest diagnostic group fit for borderline personality disorder (0.894 and 0.880 respectively) and correspondingly the two most convergent multi-domain risk profiles among the interpretable cases. [[summaries/pai_62]] has borderline personality disorder as a rule-out diagnosis with a coefficient of fit of 0.711 — statistically significant — indicating that borderline features are present and clinically relevant even if the borderline profile is not as dominant as in [[summaries/pai_202]] or [[summaries/pai_314]].
17. **Immediate intervention threshold:** Several cases in the series are described as requiring immediate clinical intervention rather than routine follow-up. [[summaries/pai_04]], [[summaries/pai_202]], and [[summaries/pai_316]] (Eric Roizman) each explicitly trigger language about immediate evaluation and intervention. [[summaries/pai_62]] does not reach this threshold, as the treatment section does not report self-harm distress; however, the elevated SPI (84 T) combined with the aggression profile warrants close monitoring.
18. **Inattention as a treatment and risk modifier:** The Inattention Index, when substantially elevated, signals that the client may struggle to attend meaningfully to treatment tasks. In [[summaries/pai_47]] and [[summaries/pai_30]], the Inattention Index is 99 T — the joint highest in the entire series. In [[summaries/pai_62]] and [[summaries/pai_314]], the Inattention Index is 88 T — substantially elevated — creating a meaningful clinical barrier to sustained engagement. In [[summaries/pai_62]], the clinical description of thought processes marked by confusion, distractibility, and difficulty concentrating is directly consistent with this index elevation and may complicate meaningful participation in some forms of treatment.

## Social Support as a Risk Moderator

Across all cases, social support is an important contextual variable. In [[summaries/pai_02]], perceived social support is below average. In [[summaries/pai_06]], social support is explicitly described as offering little support. In [[summaries/pai_09]], the respondent also believes his social relationships offer little support. In [[summaries/pai_100]], both recent stress and perceived social support are described as about average — a favorable prognostic sign. In [[summaries/pai_102]] (Ashish Gautam), the respondent reports having a number of supportive relationships despite social withdrawal. In [[summaries/pai_11]] (AJ Zhang), perceived social support is somewhat lower than average. In [[summaries/pai_13]] (Sourina Mehryar), perceived social support is explicitly described as offering little support. In [[summaries/pai_15]] (Aria Dewey), the respondent believes her social relationships offer little support. In [[summaries/pai_17]] (Summer Young), both recent level of stress and perceived level of social support are described as about average — a favorable prognostic sign. In [[summaries/pai_23]] (Gorety Nguyen), both recent level of stress and perceived level of social support are described as about average — a favorable prognostic sign. In [[summaries/pai_26]] (Alex Shirley), perceived social support is below average; the respondent is characterized as withdrawn and introverted with limited social network, and the treatment section specifically identifies the significance of the therapeutic relationship given this deficit. In [[summaries/pai_30]] (Krishan Patel), both recent level of stress and perceived level of social support are described as about average — a favorable prognostic sign. In [[summaries/pai_47]] (Lauren Hurlbut), perceived social support is explicitly described as offering little support: family relationships may be somewhat distant or ridden with conflict, and friends may not be available when needed. In [[summaries/pai_62]] (Woody Chiang), perceived social support is described as offering little support: family relationships may be somewhat distant, and friends may not be available when needed. Despite the lack of social support, the respondent views her environment as reasonably stable and predictable, with relatively little stress arising from major life areas — a partially mitigating feature. The treatment section specifically identifies current difficulties in her social support system as giving special significance to the therapeutic relationship and any impasse needing to be handled with particular care. This poor social support picture, combined with the elevated SPI (84 T), Treatment Process Index (76 T), and Inattention Index (88 T), positions [[summaries/pai_62]] as a case where both risk and treatment difficulty are substantively elevated. In [[summaries/pai_199]] (Dylan Kay), both recent level of stress and perceived level of social support are described as about average — a favorable prognostic sign. In [[summaries/pai_202]] (Raven Colbert), social relationships are perceived as offering little support, and the lack of social support is explicitly identified in the treatment section as a risk-amplifying factor heightening suicide concern. In [[summaries/pai_316]] (Eric Roizman), by contrast, the report identifies a committed social support system as a protective factor and a positive indicator for future adjustment — an unusual feature in a profile of this severity.

## Defensiveness as a Risk Interpretation Challenge

[[summaries/pai_05]] uniquely illustrates the clinical challenge posed by a positive impression management response set. [[summaries/pai_06]] presents simultaneous elevation of both positive and negative distortion indicators. [[summaries/pai_10]] represents the extreme endpoint: an entirely invalid profile where all clinical inference is suspended. [[summaries/pai_17]] (Summer Young) presents a distinctive validity profile with simultaneous positive and negative distortion signals. [[summaries/pai_23]] (Gorety Nguyen), [[summaries/pai_26]] (Alex Shirley), and [[summaries/pai_199]] (Dylan Kay) present clean validity pictures. [[summaries/pai_30]] (Krishan Patel) presents a validity picture that is largely clean for general response distortion but with specific concerns about denial of substance use. [[summaries/pai_47]] (Lauren Hurlbut) presents a similar pattern: general response style indicators are within acceptable limits, but specific concerns about denial of alcohol are flagged — with the ALC Estimated Score 29T higher than the actual ALC scale representing the largest such discrepancy in the interpretable series. [[summaries/pai_62]] (Woody Chiang) presents subtle negative impression management concerns — not rising to the level of profile invalidity, but sufficient that the evaluator is cautioned that clinical scale elevations may overrepresent the extent and degree of significant test findings in certain areas. The key distortion indicators for [[summaries/pai_62]] include: Malingering Index T=57, Rogers Discriminant Function T=62, Negative Distortion Scale T=59, Hong Malingering Index T=59, Multiscale Feigning Index T=65 — all mildly elevated, creating a cumulative signal of possible negative impression management without any single indicator reaching a threshold for clear malingering. The profile fit with the NIM predicted profile (0.769) is the strongest context-specific fit for [[summaries/pai_62]], consistent with this interpretation. [[summaries/pai_314]] (Tiffany Cha) presents a clean validity picture for general response distortion — with only substance use denial concerns specifically flagged. [[summaries/pai_316]] (Eric Roizman) presents a valid profile: forthright responding confirmed, no NIM/PIM threshold violations. See [[concepts/validity-and-response-styles]] for related discussion.
