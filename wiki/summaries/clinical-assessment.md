# Clinical Assessment

Clinical assessment is the systematic evaluation of cognitive, neurological, and psychological functioning using standardized instruments, checklists, and protocols. This page frames assessment as central to diagnosis, treatment planning, and intervention, with particular emphasis on the limits of single-source evaluation and the importance of context-sensitive, multi-informant methods.

## Core Role

Clinical assessment supports:
- diagnosis
- treatment planning
- intervention recommendations
- longitudinal case conceptualization

A recurring theme is that symptoms and behavior vary across settings, especially in children and adolescents, so assessment often requires reports from multiple informants rather than reliance on a single observer.

## Key Assessment Domains

Common domains include:
- **Memory** — verbal, visual, and working memory
- **Language** — receptive, expressive, and processing-related functions
- **Visual-spatial skills** — perception, construction, navigation
- **Executive functions** — planning, inhibition, flexibility
- **Attention** — sustained, selective, and divided attention

These domains connect closely to neuropsychology and likely overlap with concept pages around executive functioning and attention assessment.

## Standardized Instruments and Structured Evaluation

The document emphasizes the use of validated, normed instruments administered under controlled conditions, along with structured checklists to ensure comprehensive coverage. It notes that dimensional rating scales often produce better cross-informant correspondence than categorical approaches, which has implications for instrument selection.

## Multi-Informant Assessment

A major focus is disagreement across informants such as parents, teachers, clinicians, and patients. Drawing on nihms658713, the page notes that cross-informant agreement is typically low (meta-analytic average around **r = .28**), with better agreement for externalizing than internalizing problems.

The document uses the **Operations Triad Model (OTM)** to interpret discrepancies:
1. **Converging operations** — agreement across informants
2. **Diverging operations** — meaningful context-specific behavioral differences
3. **Compensating operations** — discrepancies due to method effects or rater bias

This reframes disagreement as potentially informative clinical data rather than mere error. Related concept pages are explicitly identified:

## Validity and Incremental Use of Informants

The document highlights several interpretive cautions:
- agreement does not reliably track severity
- rater bias, including depression-related distortion, has mixed support
- developmental and social desirability effects are limited explanations
- measurement error can be transient, random, or systematic

Additional informants should only be used when they provide **incremental validity** through unique, context-specific information. The text also warns about **criterion contamination**, especially in studies where outcomes are not independent of the informants being evaluated.

## Best Practices in Clinical Assessment

Three practical recommendations are emphasized:
1. **Personalize assessments** to the contexts where symptoms occur
2. **Use proactive procedures** by generating hypotheses about convergence/divergence before testing
3. **Apply evidence-based medicine tools**, including diagnostic likelihood ratios, for probabilistic decision-making

These points suggest possible future concept pages around evidence based medicine and assessment validity.

## Attention Problems as a Clinical Theme

The page identifies attention difficulties as a common referral concern and highlights a recurring pattern in report fragments such as bwu.neuro.reports.nse.01.00 20240731130738 and bwu.neuro.reports.nse.01.00 20240731133947: attention problems may be longstanding yet untreated until a triggering event prompts evaluation.

This pattern matters for:
- onset and chronicity assessment
- treatment history documentation
- differential diagnosis
- distinguishing developmental from acquired attentional problems
- evaluating cross-context variability and comorbidity
